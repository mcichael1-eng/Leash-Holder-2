## #

- **2N** — Two complete, independent systems, each able to carry the full load alone, for example two separate power paths from two utility feeds through two sets of UPS and switchgear to every rack. *(Chapter 38)*
- **2N+1 and distributed redundancy** — Variations with even more spare capacity (two full systems plus a spare), or with spare capacity shared across several blocks, for example three power systems each loaded to two-thirds so that any two can carry the load if one fails. *(Chapter 38)*
- **3D stacking (hybrid bonding)** — Building chips upward by bonding dies directly on top of each other, copper pad to copper pad, so logic and memory can sit microns apart. NVIDIA's 2028 Feynman is reported to use 3D die stacking. *(Chapter 35)*
- **6ND rule** — A rule of thumb that training a Transformer takes about 6 × N × D floating-point operations, where N is the number of (active) parameters and D is the number of training tokens. *(Chapter 19, Chapter 37)*
- **800 V DC distribution** — NVIDIA's announced power architecture, planned for production alongside its Kyber racks in 2027, that delivers 800-volt direct current to racks. NVIDIA says it carries 85% more power through the same conductor than 415 V AC, cuts copper use by about 45% and improves end-to-end efficiency by up to 5%. *(Chapter 34)*

## A

- **A2A (Agent2Agent protocol)** — An open standard, created by Google and now a Linux Foundation project, that lets AI agents built by different companies discover each other and hand tasks to one another. It complements MCP: MCP is agent-to-tool, A2A is agent-to-agent. *(Chapter 28)*
- **Ablation** — An experiment that changes or removes one ingredient while keeping everything else fixed, to measure that ingredient's effect. *(Chapter 19)*
- **Abstention** — A model declining to answer, for example by saying "I don't know" or "I can't verify that", instead of guessing. *(Chapter 18)*
- **A/B test** — An experiment that shows two versions of a product to randomly split groups of users and compares outcomes statistically, such as resolution rate or customer satisfaction. *(Chapter 31)*
- **Accelerator** — Any chip specialized to do one kind of work, here AI math, much faster and more efficiently than a general-purpose CPU. GPUs, TPUs and NPUs are all AI accelerators. *(Chapter 2, Chapter 32, Chapter 37)*
- **Acceptable use policy** — A list of prohibited uses attached to a model license or service (for example weapons development or harassment), which you agree to follow when you download or use the model. *(Chapter 30)*
- **Acceptance rate** — The share of draft tokens the large model accepts. High acceptance means large speed-ups; low acceptance means the draft is wasted work. *(Chapter 23)*
- **Accessibility tree** — A structured, text-based description of a screen's interface (every button, link, field and label) that operating systems and browsers build for screen readers used by blind people. Agents can read it alongside, or instead of, screenshots. *(Chapter 22)*
- **Access rules (row-level security)** — Settings on a database that decide, row by row, which users may read or change which data, for example "members can see only their own votes". Missing or wrong rules are the most common cause of data leaks in app-builder apps. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Accuracy** — The share of all predictions that were correct: (TP + TN) ÷ total. It is easy to understand and badly misleading when one class is rare. *(Chapter 9)*
- **Action** — A step an automation performs, such as adding a row to a spreadsheet, sending a message or creating a calendar event. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Activation checkpointing (recomputation)** — Saving only some activations and recomputing the rest during the backward pass. It trades extra compute (up to about one extra forward pass, roughly a third more work, since the forward pass is 2 of the 6 FLOPs per parameter per token) for much less memory. *(Chapter 33, Chapter 37)*
- **Activation function** — A simple nonlinear "bend" applied to each number after a layer's multiply-and-add, such as "replace negatives with zero". Without it, stacking layers would be pointless. *(Chapter 5, Chapter 12)*
- **Activations** — The intermediate results computed at every layer during the forward pass, which training must keep until the backward pass uses them to compute gradients. Their size grows with batch size and sequence length and can rival everything else combined. *(Chapter 33, Chapter 37)*
- **Active learning** — A strategy where the model chooses which unlabeled examples it would most benefit from having a human label, usually the ones it is least sure about, so that expensive human labeling time is spent where it helps most. *(Chapter 8)*
- **Active parameters** — In a mixture-of-experts model, the parameters actually used to process one token, as opposed to the total stored. Compute per token depends on active parameters; memory depends on the total. *(Chapter 5, Chapter 17, Chapter 18, Chapter 24, Chapter 37)*
- **Adam** — An optimizer (2014) that combines momentum with a separate, automatically adjusted step size for each parameter, based on running averages of each parameter's gradients and squared gradients. *(Chapter 13)*
- **AdamW** — Adam with weight decay applied separately ("decoupled") from the gradient-based step. It has been the standard optimizer for Transformers since the late 2010s. *(Chapter 13)*
- **Adapter** — The small set of trained add-on weights produced by LoRA, saved as its own file. It only works together with the exact base model it was trained on. *(Chapter 30)*
- **Adaptive thinking** — A mode in which the model itself decides how much to reason for each request, guided by an effort setting rather than a fixed token budget. *(Chapter 21, Chapter 25)*
- **Adder (half adder and full adder)** — A circuit of logic gates that adds binary numbers. A half adder adds two bits; a full adder adds two bits plus a carry from the previous column, so full adders can be chained to add numbers of any length. *(Chapter 2)*
- **Adiabatic (evaporative) cooling** — Spraying or wicking a little water into the air entering a cooler so that evaporation lowers the air's temperature before it reaches the coils. It uses some water, but only on hot days, and much less than a cooling tower. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Advanced packaging** — Techniques for combining several dies (for example GPU dies and HBM memory stacks) in one package with very dense, very short connections between them, so they behave almost like one chip. *(Chapter 36)*
- **Advantage** — How much better (positive) or worse (negative) an action turned out than expected. The "expected" reward it is compared against is called the baseline. RL raises the probability of actions with positive advantage and lowers those with negative advantage. *(Chapter 20)*
- **Adversarial input** — An input written on purpose to make a system misbehave, such as a request to reveal its instructions or to grant an unauthorized discount. *(Chapter 31)*
- **Agent** — An AI system that takes a series of actions toward a goal, such as searching, clicking, running code or editing files, deciding each next step from the results of the last, rather than just answering once. *(Chapter 1, Chapter 8, Chapter 21, Chapter 25, Chapter 28)*
- **Agent Card** — A public description file in A2A that advertises what an agent can do, how to reach it and how to authenticate, like a business card for software agents. *(Chapter 28)*
- **Agent framework** — A larger, more opinionated toolkit for building AI applications and agents, with its own structure for steps, state and memory, that usually works across many model providers. *(Chapter 28)*
- **Agentic AI Foundation (AAIF)** — A Linux Foundation organization launched in December 2025 to govern open agent standards. It started with three projects: MCP (from Anthropic), goose (an open-source agent from Block) and AGENTS.md (from OpenAI). *(Chapter 28)*
- **Agentic engineering** — Building software with coding agents in a disciplined way: a clear written spec, an approved plan, tests written first, small reviewed changes, instruction files, automated checks and sandboxes. The human stays responsible for design and review. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Agentic RAG** — RAG in which the language model itself decides whether to search, what to search for and how many times, using retrieval as a tool inside a loop, rather than the app always searching once before the model answers. *(Chapter 27)*
- **Agent loop** — The cycle at the heart of every agent: the model looks at the goal and what it knows so far, decides on an action (usually a tool call), the application runs it, the result is added to the context, and the model decides again, until it decides it is done or a limit stops it. *(Chapter 28)*
- **Agent mode** — A chat-app feature in which the AI carries out multi-step tasks, such as browsing websites, filling in forms or creating and editing files, rather than only answering. *(Chapter 25, Chapter 29)*
- **Agent SDK** — A code library from a model provider or open-source project that supplies the agent loop, tool handling, memory and other plumbing, so developers can build agents without writing everything from scratch. *(Chapter 28)*
- **Agent Skill** — A folder containing a SKILL.md instruction file (with a name, description and instructions) plus optional scripts and reference files, which an AI loads only when a task needs it. It began at Anthropic and was released as an open standard (Chapter 28). *(Chapter 25, Chapter 28)*
- **AGENTS.md (and CLAUDE.md)** — Plain-text instruction files placed in a project folder that agents read automatically at the start of work: how to build and test the project, conventions to follow, and things never to do. AGENTS.md is the cross-vendor name; CLAUDE.md is Claude Code's, and Claude Code also reads AGENTS.md. *(Chapter 28)*
- **Agent swarm** — A setup in which a main agent breaks a large task into parts and hands them to many sub-agents working in parallel, then combines their results. *(Chapter 21)*
- **Aggregate index** — A single score that combines many benchmarks, such as the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index or the Epoch Capabilities Index. It is convenient for ranking but hides where a model is strong or weak. *(Chapter 24)*
- **AGI (artificial general intelligence)** — A hypothetical AI system that can do most intellectual work as well as a capable human, across domains rather than in one narrow area. There is no agreed test for it, which is why claims that a model "is AGI" should be read with care (see Chapter 60). *(Chapter 7)*
- **AI agent** — An AI system that pursues a goal over many steps by taking actions, such as searching, clicking, running code or calling tools, rather than giving a single answer (see Chapter 28). *(Chapter 7)*
- **AI agent node** — An automation step that runs a full agent loop (Chapter 28): the model gets a goal plus a set of tools and decides for itself which actions to take, rather than following fixed steps. *(Chapter 29)*
- **AI app builder** — A web service such as Lovable, Bolt, Replit Agent or v0 that turns a plain-language description into a working web app, writing the code with an agent and hosting the result for you, usually with a database and user logins included. *(Chapter 29)*
- **AI effect** — The habit of no longer calling something "AI" once it works reliably. Spam filters, route planning and face unlock were all AI research problems before they became ordinary features. *(Chapter 7)*
- **AI factory** — NVIDIA's marketing term for a data center whose product is tokens (AI outputs), stressing throughput, tokens per watt and cost per token rather than general-purpose computing. *(Chapter 34)*
- **AI R&D automation** — AI systems doing the work of AI researchers and engineers, which could speed up AI progress itself. Labs and evaluators now measure it directly. *(Chapter 24)*
- **AI step** — A step inside an automation that sends data to a language model with an instruction ("extract vendor, date and total as JSON") and passes the model's answer to the next step. *(Chapter 29)*
- **AI training campus ("AI factory")** — A data center, often hundreds of megawatts to gigawatts in size, built mainly to train or serve AI models on tightly networked accelerators. "AI factory" is NVIDIA's marketing term, stressing that the product is tokens. *(Chapter 38)*
- **AI winter** — A period when disappointment caused funding and interest in AI to collapse. There were two major ones, roughly the mid-1970s and the late 1980s to early 1990s. *(Chapter 1, Chapter 7)*
- **AlexNet** — The 2012 CNN by Krizhevsky, Sutskever and Hinton that won the ImageNet competition by a huge margin. It is widely seen as the start of the modern deep learning era. *(Chapter 14)*
- **Algorithm** — A step-by-step method for solving a problem, like a recipe. The same algorithm can be written in any programming language, or in plain words. *(Chapter 1, Chapter 3, Chapter 7, Chapter 8)*
- **Algorithmic fairness** — The study of whether models treat groups equitably. It includes several competing mathematical definitions of "fair" that often cannot all be satisfied at once. *(Chapter 11)*
- **ALiBi (Attention with Linear Biases)** — A position method that subtracts a penalty from each attention score in proportion to the distance between the two tokens, so the model naturally pays more attention to nearby text. *(Chapter 15)*
- **Alignment** — Which input words a translation model relies on when producing each output word. Attention weights give a readable picture of it. *(Chapter 14, Chapter 20)*
- **Alignment tax** — Any loss of capability or usefulness caused by alignment and safety training, such as worse benchmark scores, blander writing or unnecessary refusals. *(Chapter 20)*
- **All-gather** — A collective in which each GPU contributes one slice and every GPU ends up with all the slices joined together. *(Chapter 37)*
- **All-reduce** — A collective in which every GPU contributes numbers (for example its gradients), they are summed, and every GPU receives the full sum. It is the backbone of data-parallel training. *(Chapter 37)*
- **All-to-all** — A collective operation in which every GPU sends a different chunk of data to every other GPU. It is the main communication pattern of expert parallelism. *(Chapter 37)*
- **All-to-all communication** — A data exchange where every chip sends a different piece of data to every other chip. MoE layers do it twice: once to dispatch tokens to experts and once to bring results back. *(Chapter 17)*
- **ALU (arithmetic logic unit)** — The part of a processor that performs arithmetic (add, subtract, multiply) and logic (compare, AND, OR) on numbers held in its registers. *(Chapter 2)*
- **Amdahl's law** — The rule that the speedup from parallel hardware is limited by the part of a job that cannot be parallelized. If 5% of a task must run one step at a time, no number of processors can make the whole task more than 20 times faster. *(Chapter 32)*
- **Analog computing** — Doing math with continuous electrical quantities (such as currents adding up in a wire) rather than 0s and 1s. It can be very energy-efficient but is noisy and imprecise, which is hard for large neural networks. *(Chapter 35)*
- **Analytical Engine** — Charles Babbage's 1830s design for a general-purpose mechanical computer, programmed with punched cards. It was never completed, but its design anticipated the parts of a modern computer. *(Chapter 7)*
- **ANN (Approximate Nearest Neighbor) search** — Methods that find vectors very close to the query without comparing it to every stored vector. They are thousands of times faster than brute force on large collections, at the cost of occasionally missing one of the true best matches. *(Chapter 27)*
- **Annealing** — Lowering the learning rate toward zero near the end of training, often while switching to the best data, so the model settles into a good final state. *(Chapter 19)*
- **Annotation (labeling)** — Adding labels to raw data, such as tagging images, transcribing audio, marking names in text or rating chatbot answers, so models can learn from it. *(Chapter 11)*
- **Anomaly detection** — Spotting examples that are unusually different from the rest, such as a card payment that does not look like you, or a machine vibrating in a new way before it fails. *(Chapter 8, Chapter 10)*
- **Anti-distillation safeguard** — Any measure that makes a model's outputs less useful for copying it, such as detecting mass-extraction accounts, classifiers that flag distillation patterns, or showing only summaries of the model's raw reasoning. *(Chapter 20)*
- **Any-to-any model** — A model that can accept many kinds of input (text, images, audio, video) and produce many kinds of output, choosing the output modality to suit the request. *(Chapter 22)*
- **API (Application Programming Interface)** — A defined way for one program to request services from another, like a restaurant menu and a waiter. AI companies sell model access through APIs. *(Chapter 4, Chapter 18, Chapter 24, Chapter 26)*
- **API gateway** — The front-door service of an API that checks keys or logins, enforces rate limits and routes each request to the right back-end system. *(Chapter 4, Chapter 44)*
- **API key** — A secret code, like a password, that identifies who is using an API so they can be billed and limited. Anyone who gets your key can spend your money. *(Chapter 4, Chapter 26)*
- **Approach temperature** — How close a heat exchanger or cooler can bring a fluid to the temperature of the thing cooling it. A dry cooler with a 5 °C approach can, at best, cool water to 5 °C above the outdoor air temperature. *(Chapter 40)*
- **ARC-AGI** — A family of puzzle benchmarks from François Chollet's ARC Prize Foundation, designed to test whether a system can learn a new skill from a few examples rather than recall knowledge. ARC-AGI-3 (2026) uses interactive game-like environments with no instructions. *(Chapter 24)*
- **ARIMA** — A classic statistical forecasting model that predicts the next value from recent past values and recent past forecast errors, after removing trend. *(Chapter 10)*
- **Arithmetic intensity** — The number of math operations performed for each byte moved from memory. High intensity keeps the math units busy; low intensity leaves them waiting. *(Chapter 23, Chapter 33)*
- **Arm** — A company, and the instruction set it licenses, whose energy-efficient designs power nearly all phones, Apple's Macs and a growing share of data-center CPUs such as NVIDIA Grace and Vera and Google Axion. *(Chapter 32)*
- **Artifact or Canvas** — A side panel where the AI creates and edits a document, code or a small interactive app next to the chat, so you can see and refine a finished object instead of scrolling through messages. *(Chapter 25)*
- **Artificial general intelligence (AGI)** — A hypothetical AI that can learn and perform most intellectual tasks a human can, across many domains. There is no single agreed definition, and people use the term in quite different ways. *(Chapter 1)*
- **Artificial intelligence (AI)** — The field of getting machines to do things that would require intelligence if people did them, such as recognizing faces, understanding language, translating, planning, making decisions or controlling a robot. AI names a goal; many very different techniques have been used to pursue it. *(Chapter 1, Chapter 7)*
- **Artificial neuron** — A tiny calculation loosely inspired by a brain cell: it takes several input numbers, combines them into one total, and produces an output depending on whether that total is large enough. Millions to billions of them make up a neural network. *(Chapter 7, Chapter 12)*
- **Artificial superintelligence (ASI)** — A hypothetical AI that greatly exceeds the best humans at virtually every intellectual task. *(Chapter 1)*
- **ASCII** — An early encoding (American Standard Code for Information Interchange) that gives numbers 0 to 127 to English letters, digits and common symbols. "A" is 65, "a" is 97, and the space is 32. *(Chapter 3)*
- **ASHRAE thermal guidelines** — Widely used recommendations from ASHRAE's data center technical committee for the temperature and humidity of air entering IT equipment (a recommended range of about 18–27 °C), plus classes for liquid-cooling water temperatures. *(Chapter 40)*
- **ASIC (Application-Specific Integrated Circuit)** — A chip designed for one job. AI ASICs such as Google's TPU, Amazon's Trainium, Microsoft's Maia and Meta's MTIA trade the flexibility of a GPU for better efficiency on AI math. *(Chapter 32, Chapter 35)*
- **ASR (automatic speech recognition)** — Converting spoken audio into text, also called speech-to-text. *(Chapter 22)*
- **Attack success rate (ASR)** — The share of attack attempts that achieve the attacker's goal. Measuring it before and after a fix tells you whether the fix worked. *(Chapter 31)*
- **Attention** — A mechanism that lets a model, when handling one word or token, look at all the others and decide which ones matter most, using dot products as relevance scores (see Chapter 15). *(Chapter 7, Chapter 14, Chapter 18)*
- **Attention head** — One of the parallel attention calculations inside a multi-head attention layer. Different heads learn to track different relationships. *(Chapter 15)*
- **Attention logit** — The raw score from a query–key dot product, before softmax turns the scores into attention weights. *(Chapter 17)*
- **Attention sink** — The tendency of trained models to dump large amounts of attention on the very first token, using it as a place to "park" attention when nothing else is relevant. Some designs add an explicit sink; others remove the need for one. *(Chapter 15, Chapter 17)*
- **Attention weights (attention matrix)** — The percentages, produced by softmax from query-key scores, that say how much each token draws from each other token. Arranged as a grid with one row per token, they form the attention matrix. *(Chapter 15)*
- **AUC (Area Under the ROC Curve)** — A single score from 0.5 to 1.0 summarizing the ROC curve. It equals the chance that the model gives a randomly chosen positive example a higher score than a randomly chosen negative one. 0.5 is a coin flip; 1.0 is perfect ranking. *(Chapter 9)*
- **Authentication** — Checking who someone is, usually with a password, a code sent to their phone or a "Sign in with Google" button. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Authorization** — Checking what a signed-in person is allowed to see and do. A common vibe-coding bug is getting authentication right but authorization wrong, so every logged-in user can read everyone's data. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Autoencoder** — A neural network trained to compress its input into a small code and then rebuild the input from that code. The squeeze forces it to learn the essential structure of the data. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Automated test** — A small piece of code that runs part of a program with known inputs and checks that the output is what it should be. It reports "pass" or "fail" in seconds, with no human needed. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Automatic differentiation (autograd)** — Software that records the steps of a calculation and then computes all the derivatives automatically by applying the chain rule. It is why AI engineers never have to work out a network's slopes by hand. Often shortened to autograd. *(Chapter 6, Chapter 12)*
- **Automation bias** — People's tendency to over-trust automated suggestions and stop checking them, especially when the system is usually right. *(Chapter 31)*
- **Automation platform** — A service such as Zapier, Make or n8n that connects many apps and runs workflows between them on your behalf, around the clock, without you writing a program. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Autoregressive generation** — Producing output one token at a time, where each new token is chosen using everything before it, then fed back in as input for the next step. *(Chapter 15, Chapter 18)*
- **Autoregressive image generation** — Creating an image by predicting its tokens one at a time, each conditioned on the conversation and all the image tokens so far, the same way a language model writes text. A decoder then turns the tokens into pixels. *(Chapter 22)*
- **Autoregressive model** — A model that generates output one piece at a time, each piece conditioned on everything generated so far. Chatbots write text this way, and some image models paint images this way. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Auxiliary-loss-free balancing** — DeepSeek's method of balancing experts by nudging a per-expert bias used only for selection, raising it for underused experts and lowering it for overloaded ones, without adding a term to the loss. *(Chapter 17)*
- **Auxiliary loss (load-balancing loss)** — An extra penalty added to the training loss that discourages the router from overusing some experts. *(Chapter 17)*
- **Availability ("the nines")** — The fraction of time a system is up, often written as a number of nines: 99.9% ("three nines") allows about 8.8 hours of downtime per year, 99.99% about 53 minutes, and 99.999% about 5 minutes. *(Chapter 38)*

## B

- **Backbone** — The highest-capacity long-distance links of the internet, mostly fiber-optic cables between major cities and across oceans. *(Chapter 4)*
- **Backend** — The behind-the-scenes part of an app that runs on servers, handling logic, security checks and communication with databases and other services. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Back-end-of-line (BEOL)** — The later part of chip manufacturing, which builds the many layers of metal wiring that connect the transistors to each other and to the outside world. *(Chapter 36)*
- **Back-end vs. front-end network** — The back-end (or compute fabric) carries GPU-to-GPU traffic for training and large inference jobs and is built for maximum speed. The front-end connects servers to users, the internet and storage, like an ordinary data center network. *(Chapter 34)*
- **Background agent (cloud agent)** — An agent that runs on a remote computer for minutes to hours while you do something else, then reports back, for example by opening a pull request with finished code or delivering a research report. Many products also let you schedule such runs to happen automatically at set times. *(Chapter 28, Chapter 29)*
- **Backpropagation (backprop)** — The method for computing the gradient of the loss with respect to every parameter of a neural network in one backward sweep, by applying the chain rule layer by layer from the output back to the input. It was popularized in a 1986 paper by Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams. *(Chapter 6, Chapter 7, Chapter 12)*
- **Backpropagation through time (BPTT)** — Training a recurrent network by unrolling it across all its time steps and running backpropagation backward through the whole chain, so every step's share of the error is measured. *(Chapter 14)*
- **Backside power delivery** — Building a chip's power-supply wiring on the back of the wafer, under the transistors, instead of mixing it in with the signal wiring on top. It frees space and delivers power more efficiently. *(Chapter 36)*
- **Backtesting** — Testing a forecasting method by repeatedly training only on data up to a past date, forecasting the period after it, and comparing with what actually happened. *(Chapter 10)*
- **Backup generator** — An engine-driven generator, usually diesel and sometimes natural gas, that powers a site during a grid outage. Typical data center units are 2 to 3 megawatts or more each. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Bagging** — Short for "bootstrap aggregating": training many models on different random resamples of the rows and averaging or voting their predictions. It mainly reduces variance. *(Chapter 10)*
- **Bandwidth** — How much data can be moved per second, like the width of a pipe. Memory bandwidth is measured in gigabytes or terabytes per second. *(Chapter 2, Chapter 4)*
- **Barge-in** — Your ability to interrupt a voice assistant mid-sentence and have it stop, listen and respond to what you just said. *(Chapter 22)*
- **Base die (logic die)** — The bottom die of an HBM stack, which manages the stack and talks to the processor. From HBM4 onward it can be custom logic made at a chip foundry such as TSMC rather than at the memory maker. *(Chapter 33, Chapter 36)*
- **Baseline** — A simple reference model that any real model must beat, such as "always predict the most common class," "always predict the average" or "tomorrow will be the same as today." *(Chapter 9)*
- **Base model** — A model straight out of pretraining. It continues text in whatever style it sees, but does not reliably follow instructions or act like an assistant. *(Chapter 18, Chapter 20)*
- **Base rate** — How common something is before you look at any specific evidence, such as a disease affecting 1% of people. Ignoring the base rate is one of the most common reasoning mistakes. *(Chapter 6)*
- **Batch and step** — A batch is the group of training examples processed together before one update. A step is one such update. Large pretraining runs use batches of millions of tokens per step. *(Chapter 19)*
- **Batch API** — An API option where you submit many requests at once and receive results later, typically within 24 hours, usually at about half the standard price. *(Chapter 23, Chapter 24, Chapter 26)*
- **Batching (continuous batching)** — Processing many users' requests together on the same GPUs at the same time, which makes far better use of the hardware than handling them one by one. *(Chapter 4, Chapter 23, Chapter 33, Chapter 37)*
- **Batch (mini-batch)** — A group of examples (images, sentences, users' requests) processed together in one go. Batching keeps the chip busy and is a major reason AI is affordable to run. *(Chapter 5, Chapter 8, Chapter 12)*
- **Batch normalization** — A 2015 normalization method that rescales each feature using the average and spread across the examples in the current batch. It is a key ingredient of image networks. *(Chapter 13)*
- **Batch size** — The number of examples in each mini-batch, a hyperparameter. Small batches give noisy but cheap steps; large batches give smoother but costlier steps. *(Chapter 12)*
- **Bayesian optimization** — A smarter search that uses the results so far to guess which untried settings are most promising, and tries those next. *(Chapter 9)*
- **Bayes' rule** — A formula for updating a belief when new evidence arrives: P(A given B) = P(B given A) × P(A) ÷ P(B). It combines how well the evidence points to A with how common A was to begin with. *(Chapter 6)*
- **Beachfront (shoreline)** — The length of a chip's edge available for connections to memory and other chips. HBM stacks must sit along the edges of the compute die, so the perimeter limits how many stacks, and how much bandwidth, a package can have. *(Chapter 33)*
- **Benchmark** — A standard test, usually a fixed dataset with a scoring rule, used to compare AI systems. Benchmarks drive progress but can be overfit or outgrown (see Chapter 9 and Chapter 24). *(Chapter 7, Chapter 9, Chapter 24)*
- **Benchmark contamination** — When the questions and answers of a public test end up in a model's training data, for example because they were posted online and scraped. Scores then partly measure memory rather than ability. *(Chapter 9, Chapter 19, Chapter 24)*
- **Benchmark saturation** — The point at which top models score so high on a test that it can no longer tell them apart, forcing the field to build harder tests. *(Chapter 7, Chapter 9, Chapter 24)*
- **BERT** — A 2018 encoder-only Transformer from Google, trained by hiding words and predicting them from both sides. It transformed language understanding tasks and was added to Google Search in 2019. *(Chapter 15)*
- **Best-of-N** — Generating N candidate answers and choosing the best with a verifier, a reward model or a judge. *(Chapter 21)*
- **BF16 (bfloat16)** — A 16-bit (2-byte) format created by Google that keeps FP32's huge range but only about 2 to 3 significant digits. It became the default for training large models. *(Chapter 5, Chapter 13, Chapter 18, Chapter 32)*
- **Bias (statistical)** — A parameter that is added after the multiplying, shifting every output up or down by the same amount, like the taxi's flag-drop fee. It has nothing to do with unfair bias in the social sense. *(Chapter 5, Chapter 8, Chapter 9, Chapter 12)*
- **Bias–variance tradeoff** — The classic tension in model building. Making a model more flexible lowers its bias but raises its variance; the best model balances the two for the amount of data you have. *(Chapter 9)*
- **Bi-encoder** — A model that encodes the question and each passage separately into vectors, which are then compared. It is fast because passage vectors are computed ahead of time, but it never sees the question and passage side by side. Standard embedding search uses bi-encoders. *(Chapter 27)*
- **Binary** — Counting with only two digits, 0 and 1. Each position is worth double the one to its right (1, 2, 4, 8, 16...), so the binary number 1101 means 8 + 4 + 0 + 1 = 13. *(Chapter 2)*
- **Binary classification** — Classification with exactly two possible answers, such as fraud or not fraud, or pass or fail. *(Chapter 8)*
- **Binary search** — Finding an item in a sorted list by repeatedly checking the middle and throwing away the half that cannot contain it. It finds anything among a million sorted items in at most 20 steps. *(Chapter 3)*
- **Binning** — Sorting finished chips by how well they perform, and selling partly defective or slower ones as lower-tier products with some parts disabled. *(Chapter 36)*
- **Biometrics** — Identifying people by physical features such as fingerprints, palm veins, faces or irises, used at data centers alongside badges and PIN codes. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Bisection bandwidth** — The total bandwidth crossing an imaginary cut that splits a network into two equal halves. It measures how well the whole cluster can communicate at once. *(Chapter 34)*
- **Bit** — The smallest unit of information: a single choice between two possibilities, written 0 or 1 (off or on, no or yes). The word is short for "binary digit." *(Chapter 2)*
- **Bit depth** — How many bits each sample uses, which sets how finely the wave's height is measured. CD audio uses 16 bits, giving 65,536 possible levels. *(Chapter 3)*
- **Bits per weight** — The average number of bits used to store each parameter in a quantized model, including the scaling numbers. A "4-bit" file often averages about 4.5 to 5 bits per weight. *(Chapter 30)*
- **Bitter Lesson** — Rich Sutton's 2019 argument that, over the long run, general methods that scale with computation (search and learning) beat approaches that rely on hand-built human knowledge. *(Chapter 7)*
- **Block-scaled 4-bit formats (NVFP4, MXFP4)** — 4-bit number formats in which each small block of values (16 for NVFP4, 32 for MXFP4) shares a scale factor, letting very coarse numbers still cover a wide range. They roughly double speed again over FP8. *(Chapter 37)*
- **Block scaling (microscaling)** — Giving each small block of numbers its own scale factor, so one outlier only distorts its own block. MXFP4, an open industry standard, shares one scale per 32 numbers; NVIDIA's NVFP4 uses blocks of 16 with a finer 8-bit scale. *(Chapter 23)*
- **Blowdown** — Water deliberately drained from a cooling tower to stop dissolved minerals from building up as the rest evaporates. It is replaced with fresh "makeup" water, adding to total water use. *(Chapter 40)*
- **BM25 (Best Matching 25)** — The standard formula for ranking documents in keyword search, developed in the 1990s by Stephen Robertson and colleagues. It rewards documents that contain the query's words, gives rare words more weight, gives diminishing credit for repeated words, and adjusts for document length. *(Chapter 27)*
- **BMS (building management system)** — Software and sensors that monitor and control a building's mechanical and electrical systems, such as pumps, fans, chillers, breakers and alarms. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Boolean logic** — The algebra of true and false (1 and 0), using operations such as AND, OR and NOT. It was devised by George Boole in the 1850s and describes exactly how logic gates behave. *(Chapter 2)*
- **Boosting** — Building models in sequence, each trained to fix the errors of the combined models so far, then adding them all together. *(Chapter 10)*
- **Bottleneck (information bottleneck)** — A point in a system where all information must squeeze through a small, fixed-size space, so some of it is inevitably lost. *(Chapter 14)*
- **Bounding box and segmentation mask** — Two kinds of image label. A bounding box is a rectangle drawn around an object; a segmentation mask outlines the object's exact shape pixel by pixel. *(Chapter 11)*
- **BPE (Byte-Pair Encoding)** — A method for building a tokenizer's vocabulary. Start with single characters (or bytes), count every adjacent pair in a large body of text, merge the most frequent pair into a new token, and repeat until the vocabulary reaches the size you want. *(Chapter 15, Chapter 18)*
- **Bradley–Terry model** — A formula that turns two scores into the probability that one item beats the other: the bigger the gap between the scores, the more confident the prediction. Chess ratings use the same idea. *(Chapter 20)*
- **Branch** — A separate line of development inside a repository. You can experiment on a branch while the main version (usually called "main") stays untouched. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Brittleness** — The tendency of a rule-based system to fail suddenly and badly on cases its designers did not anticipate, instead of degrading gracefully. *(Chapter 7)*
- **Browser agent** — An agent that operates a web browser to navigate sites, fill in forms and gather information, reading either screenshots or the page's underlying structure (the DOM, the browser's internal tree of everything on the page). *(Chapter 28)*
- **BTU (British thermal unit)** — An old unit of heat energy, about 1,055 joules: the heat needed to warm one pound of water by one degree Fahrenheit. Cooling gear is often rated in BTU per hour; 1 kW of heat equals about 3,412 BTU per hour. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Bug** — A mistake in code that makes a program behave wrongly or crash. *(Chapter 3)*
- **Building shell** — The structure of a data center building itself, meaning foundations, frame, walls and roof, before the power, cooling and computing equipment is installed. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Build-to-suit and turnkey** — In build-to-suit, a developer builds a data center to one tenant's design under a long lease. Turnkey means the developer delivers a finished, ready-to-use hall with power and cooling installed, so the tenant only adds IT equipment. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Bus** — A shared set of wires that carries data between parts of a computer, such as between the processor and memory. *(Chapter 2)*
- **Busbar** — A thick, rigid bar of copper (or aluminum) that carries large electrical currents, used along the back of AI racks to feed every tray, instead of many separate cables. *(Chapter 34)*
- **Busway (busbar trunking)** — A rigid overhead rail of copper or aluminum conductors running along a row, into which racks plug through tap-off boxes. It is faster to install and change than individual cables. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Byte** — A group of 8 bits, which can hold one of 256 values (0 to 255). A byte is enough to store one basic English character, and file and memory sizes are measured in bytes. *(Chapter 2)*

## C

- **Cable tray (ladder rack)** — An open metal channel or ladder-shaped frame, hung from the ceiling or mounted above racks, that carries bundles of power or network cables along rows. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Cache** — A small, very fast memory built onto the processor chip that keeps copies of recently used data close at hand, so the processor does not have to wait for slower main memory. *(Chapter 2, Chapter 32, Chapter 33)*
- **Cache hit** — A request whose prefix matches stored cache, so that part is read cheaply and quickly instead of being recomputed. A cache miss means it is processed at full price. *(Chapter 23)*
- **Cache hit and cache miss** — A cache hit is a request that reuses a stored prefix and pays the low "cache read" price. A cache miss is a request whose prefix was not stored (or had expired, or changed by even one character) and pays full price, plus a small surcharge to write the cache. *(Chapter 26)*
- **Cache lifetime (TTL)** — How long a cached prefix is kept before being deleted ("time to live"), for example 5 minutes or 1 hour, often extended each time it is used. *(Chapter 23)*
- **Calibration** — How well a model's stated confidence matches how often it is actually right. A well-calibrated forecaster's "70% chance of rain" days see rain about 70% of the time. *(Chapter 6, Chapter 8, Chapter 18)*
- **Campus** — A site with several data center buildings sharing one substation, one fiber entrance and support infrastructure. The largest AI campuses in 2026 are measured in gigawatts. *(Chapter 34, Chapter 38)*
- **Canary (canary token)** — A unique, harmless marker planted in a prompt or document so that its appearance where it should not be (in an answer, a log or a web request) proves that a leak or an injection happened. *(Chapter 31)*
- **Carbon intensity (of electricity)** — How much carbon dioxide is emitted to generate one kWh, in grams or kilograms. It ranges from very low on grids dominated by hydro, nuclear, wind or solar to high on coal-heavy grids. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Carrier** — A telecommunications company that owns or operates long-distance or local network links, such as a fiber company or internet backbone provider, and sells connections into data centers. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Carrier hotel** — A building, often in a big city, whose main job is to host the meeting points of many networks, full of meet-me rooms and cross-connects. One Wilshire in Los Angeles and 60 Hudson Street in New York are classic examples. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Carry** — The extra 1 passed to the next column when a column's sum is too big to fit in one digit, as when 1 + 1 in binary gives 0 with 1 carried. *(Chapter 2)*
- **Cascade (voice pipeline)** — A voice assistant built as a chain: ASR turns your speech into text, a text language model writes a reply, and TTS speaks it. Each stage is a separate model. *(Chapter 22)*
- **Catastrophic forgetting** — When training a model on a narrow new task makes it noticeably worse at things it used to do well, because the shared weights were pushed too far. *(Chapter 30)*
- **Categorical feature** — A feature that takes one value from a fixed set of categories, such as color, country or plan type, rather than a measured number. *(Chapter 11)*
- **Causal mask** — A rule applied inside attention that blocks each token from seeing tokens that come after it. Future scores are set to minus infinity before softmax, so their weights become exactly zero. *(Chapter 15)*
- **CDN (content delivery network) and edge** — A network of many small sites near users, called the edge, that keeps copies of popular content and accepts connections close to you, making websites and apps faster. *(Chapter 4)*
- **CDU (coolant distribution unit)** — A cabinet of pumps and a heat exchanger that circulates clean coolant through the racks' cold plates and hands the heat to the building's facility water loop, while controlling flow, pressure and temperature. *(Chapter 38, Chapter 40)*
- **Cell** — One block in a notebook, either text or code. You run a code cell by clicking its play button or pressing Shift+Enter, and its output appears below it. *(Chapter 26)*
- **Cell state** — The LSTM's long-term memory lane. It passes from step to step and is changed only by gated, mostly additive updates, so information and gradients can travel a long way without fading. *(Chapter 14)*
- **Cell tower (base station)** — The radio equipment that connects your phone to the mobile network. It converts your phone's radio signal into data on fiber or microwave links into the carrier's network. *(Chapter 44)*
- **Certificate (digital)** — An electronic document, vouched for by a trusted certificate authority, that proves a server really belongs to the name it claims, such as claude.ai. *(Chapter 4)*
- **Chain of command (principal hierarchy)** — The rules for whose instructions a model follows when they conflict: typically the AI developer first, then the operator (the business that deploys the model with its own system prompt, such as a bank running an AI helper), then the user, each within limits set by the level above. *(Chapter 20)*
- **Chain-of-thought (CoT)** — Having a model write out intermediate reasoning steps before its final answer, which improves accuracy on multi-step problems. It later became built-in "thinking" (see Chapter 21). *(Chapter 7, Chapter 25)*
- **Chain of thought (CoT)** — The intermediate reasoning text a model writes before its final answer: working out steps, checking them and sometimes backtracking. *(Chapter 21)*
- **Chain-of-thought monitorability** — How well people or automated monitors can read a model's visible reasoning to catch mistakes or bad intent. It depends on the reasoning being written out and faithful to what the model actually computed. *(Chapter 24)*
- **Chain-of-thought prompting** — Getting a model to reason step by step by including worked examples with written-out reasoning in the prompt, or simply by adding an instruction such as "Let's think step by step." *(Chapter 21)*
- **Chain rule** — The rule that the rate of change through a chain of steps is the product of each step's rate of change. If y changes 6 times as fast as u, and u changes 3 times as fast as x, then y changes 18 times as fast as x. *(Chapter 6, Chapter 12)*
- **Channel** — One layer of numbers in an image or in a network's internal picture. A color photo has three channels (red, green, blue); a layer deep inside a vision network may have hundreds of channels, each tracking a different pattern. *(Chapter 14)*
- **Character training** — Post-training aimed at giving a model a stable, intended personality, such as curiosity, warmth, directness and honesty about its own uncertainty, rather than only teaching it rules. *(Chapter 20)*
- **Chat app** — The website or phone app you type into. It wraps a model with a hidden system prompt, memory, file handling, tools such as web search, safety filters and a user interface. *(Chapter 25)*
- **Chatbot** — A computer program you talk to in ordinary language, which replies in ordinary language. Modern chatbots such as ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini are apps built on top of large language models. *(Chapter 1)*
- **Chatbot app** — The consumer product (ChatGPT, Claude.ai, the Gemini app) that wraps one or more models with a user interface, hidden instructions, tools, memory and safety systems. *(Chapter 18)*
- **Chat template** — The exact text format, including special marker tokens, that a model was trained on for turning a multi-turn conversation into one token sequence. *(Chapter 18)*
- **Checkpoint** — A saved snapshot of a model's parameters (and usually its optimizer state) at a point in training, used to resume after failures and to roll back after instabilities. A released model's "weights file" is a checkpoint. *(Chapter 13, Chapter 19, Chapter 37)*
- **Chiller** — A large machine that uses a refrigeration cycle to make cold water for a building. Chillers can cool water below the outdoor temperature, which nothing else can do, but they are among a data center's biggest electricity users. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Chip (integrated circuit)** — A small slice of silicon with billions of microscopic switches and wires manufactured onto it. Processors, memory and AI accelerators are all chips. *(Chapter 1)*
- **Chiplet** — A smaller die designed to be combined with other dies inside one package, instead of building one huge die. Chiplets raise yield and let companies mix different manufacturing processes. *(Chapter 34, Chapter 36)*
- **CHIPS and Science Act** — A 2022 US law providing about $52.7 billion (including about $39 billion in manufacturing incentives) plus tax credits to bring chip manufacturing back to the United States. *(Chapter 36)*
- **Choke point** — A step in a supply chain that only one or a few companies (or countries) can perform, so that a disruption or a government restriction there stops the whole chain. *(Chapter 36)*
- **Chunked prefill** — Splitting a long prompt's prefill into smaller chunks and mixing them into ordinary decode steps, so a new long request doesn't freeze the token streams of users already being served. *(Chapter 23)*
- **Chunking** — Splitting documents into smaller passages, called chunks, that are embedded, retrieved and cited individually. Chunks are usually a few hundred tokens long. *(Chapter 27)*
- **Chunk overlap** — Repeating a little text at the start of each chunk from the end of the previous one, so that a sentence or idea split at a chunk boundary still appears whole in at least one chunk. *(Chapter 27)*
- **Churn** — Customers leaving a service, for example by cancelling a subscription. "Churn prediction" means estimating which customers are likely to leave soon. *(Chapter 10)*
- **CI (continuous integration)** — An automated service that runs the test suite (and other checks) every time someone pushes code or opens a pull request, and marks the change green or red. GitHub Actions is a common example. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Citation** — A pointer from part of an answer to the source that supports it, usually a numbered link. *(Chapter 25, Chapter 27)*
- **Classical machine learning** — The family of machine-learning methods developed mostly before the deep-learning boom of the 2010s, such as regression, decision trees and clustering. They usually have from a handful to a few million parameters and run comfortably on an ordinary laptop. *(Chapter 10)*
- **Classification** — A supervised task where the answer is a category, such as spam or not spam, which digit is in an image, or which language a sentence is written in. *(Chapter 8)*
- **Classifier** — A model that sorts inputs into categories, such as spam or not spam, fraud or legitimate, cat or dog. *(Chapter 1)*
- **Classifier-free guidance (CFG)** — A technique that makes generated images follow the prompt more closely by computing the model's prediction with and without the prompt and exaggerating the difference between them. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Class imbalance** — When one category vastly outnumbers another, such as 10 sick people among 1,000, or a handful of fraudulent payments among millions of normal ones. It makes accuracy misleading and calls for other metrics. *(Chapter 9)*
- **Cleanroom** — A room where the air is continuously filtered and controlled so that it holds only a tiny number of particles, plus stable temperature, humidity and pressure. Chip fabs are built around enormous cleanrooms. *(Chapter 36)*
- **Client** — A device or program that sends requests to another computer, such as your phone's chatbot app or your web browser. *(Chapter 4, Chapter 26)*
- **Client-side tool** — A tool that your own application runs when the model asks for it, such as a function that queries your company database. You write it, you host it, and you control exactly what it can do. *(Chapter 28)*
- **CLIP (Contrastive Language–Image Pretraining)** — An OpenAI model from 2021 that learned a shared embedding space for images and text from about 400 million image–caption pairs. It lets a computer score how well a caption matches a picture. *(Chapter 16, Chapter 22)*
- **Clipping (in PPO)** — Capping how much a single training update can change the probability of any action, usually to within about 20% of its old value, so that training stays stable. *(Chapter 20)*
- **Clock speed (GHz)** — How many ticks per second a chip's internal metronome makes, measured in hertz. Work inside the chip happens in step with these ticks, so a faster clock means more steps per second. *(Chapter 2, Chapter 32)*
- **Closed (proprietary) model** — A model whose weights are not released; you can use it only through the maker's app or API, under the maker's rules and prices. GPT-6 Astra, Claude Opus 5.5 and Gemini 4 Argon are closed. *(Chapter 24, Chapter 26)*
- **Cloud computing** — Renting computing power, storage and software over the internet, paying for what you use, instead of owning and running the machines yourself. *(Chapter 4)*
- **Cluster** — A large collection of accelerators networked together to work on shared jobs, often a single training run. Frontier AI clusters in 2026 range from about 100,000 to over 1,000,000 accelerators. *(Chapter 34, Chapter 37)*
- **Clustering** — Grouping similar examples together without predefined labels, such as discovering that a shop's customers fall into "bargain hunters," "weekend families" and "big one-off spenders." *(Chapter 8, Chapter 10)*
- **CMP (chemical mechanical planarization)** — Polishing a wafer flat with a rotating pad and a chemical slurry, so each new layer is built on a perfectly level surface. *(Chapter 36)*
- **CNN (Convolutional Neural Network)** — A neural network built mainly from convolutional layers, which slide small learned filters across an image (or a sound, or any grid-like data) and reuse the same weights everywhere. CNNs dominated computer vision from 2012 until about 2020 and are still widely used. *(Chapter 14)*
- **Codec** — Software that compresses and decompresses audio or video (the name comes from coder-decoder). Common video codecs include H.264, HEVC and AV1. *(Chapter 3)*
- **Code completion (autocomplete)** — An AI feature that predicts the next few words or lines of code as you type and offers them as a suggestion you can accept or ignore. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Code execution (code interpreter)** — A sandboxed tool in which the model writes and runs small programs (usually Python) to calculate, analyze files or draw charts. Often called code interpreter or data analysis. *(Chapter 25, Chapter 28)*
- **Code grader** — An automatic check written as a simple rule or program, such as exact match, "contains this word", a length limit, valid JSON or tests passing. It is fast and consistent but can only check what a rule can express. *(Chapter 31)*
- **Code point** — The number Unicode assigns to one character, written like U+0041 (the letter "A", which is 65 in ordinary decimal numbers). *(Chapter 3)*
- **Code review** — Another person (or a reviewing tool) reading the diff in a pull request to catch bugs, security problems and confusing code before it is merged. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Coding agent** — An AI agent specialized in software work. It reads a codebase, plans changes, edits files, runs tests and commands, fixes its own errors and can commit and open pull requests. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Cold plate (direct-to-chip liquid cooling)** — A metal block with tiny internal channels, clamped onto a chip, through which liquid coolant is pumped to carry heat away. It is standard on Blackwell-class AI racks. *(Chapter 34, Chapter 40)*
- **Cold start (cold-start data)** — A small supervised fine-tuning set used before reinforcement learning to give the model a readable, well-formatted reasoning style, so that RL starts from a sensible point. *(Chapter 21)*
- **Cold-start problem** — The difficulty of recommending to a brand-new user, or recommending a brand-new item, when there is no history of behavior to learn from. *(Chapter 10)*
- **Collaborative filtering** — Recommending items based on the behavior of similar users ("people like you also liked") or similar items, without needing to know what the items are. *(Chapter 10)*
- **Collective communication** — A coordinated exchange of data among many chips at once, such as all-reduce (everyone contributes numbers, they are summed, and everyone gets the total) and all-to-all (everyone sends a different piece to everyone else). Libraries such as NVIDIA's NCCL perform them. *(Chapter 34, Chapter 37)*
- **Colocation (colo)** — Renting space, power and cooling in someone else's data center and bringing your own equipment. Retail colocation means renting a few racks or a cage; wholesale colocation means renting whole halls or buildings. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Combinatorial explosion** — The way the number of possible combinations grows so fast as a problem gets bigger that checking them all becomes impossible, even for very fast computers. *(Chapter 7)*
- **Commissioning (Cx)** — Systematic testing that every system in a data center works as designed before it goes live, from Level 1 (factory tests of each piece of equipment) up to Level 5 (integrated systems tests, such as cutting the utility feed while the hall runs at full simulated load). *(Chapter 38)*
- **Commit** — A saved snapshot of changes in a Git repository, with a message describing what changed and why. *(Chapter 3, Chapter 29)*
- **Common Crawl** — A nonprofit's free, massive, regularly updated archive of web pages. Most large language-model training datasets start from it. *(Chapter 11, Chapter 19)*
- **Compaction** — Summarizing older parts of a long session, keeping the important facts, decisions and open threads, so the context window does not overflow and the model can keep working. *(Chapter 21, Chapter 24, Chapter 25, Chapter 28)*
- **Compiler** — A program that translates a whole program from a programming language into machine code before it runs. *(Chapter 3)*
- **Complement** — The event that something does not happen. Its probability is 1 minus the probability of the event itself. *(Chapter 6)*
- **Composition (of functions)** — Feeding the output of one function into another to make a new, combined function. A neural network is a long composition of simple functions called layers. *(Chapter 5)*
- **Compression** — Making data take fewer bytes by describing it more cleverly. The compressed version must be decompressed before use. *(Chapter 3)*
- **Compute** — Shorthand for computing power, or for the total amount of calculation used for a job, as in "training compute." It is measured in operations and is one of the main limits on AI progress. *(Chapter 1, Chapter 7)*
- **Compute-bound** — A task whose speed is limited by how fast the chip can do arithmetic. Prefill and training are usually compute-bound. *(Chapter 23, Chapter 33)*
- **Compute-optimal (Chinchilla-optimal)** — The split between model size and training data that gives the lowest loss for a given compute budget. Chinchilla found it at roughly 20 tokens per parameter. *(Chapter 19)*
- **Computer use** — An AI agent controlling a computer by looking at screenshots and issuing mouse clicks, scrolls and keystrokes, the way a person would. Anthropic released it for Claude in October 2024; OpenAI's Operator followed in January 2025. *(Chapter 22, Chapter 28)*
- **Computer vision** — The branch of AI that extracts information from images and video, such as recognizing objects, reading text or measuring depth. *(Chapter 1)*
- **Compute tray** — A removable server sled in a rack-scale AI system. In NVIDIA's NVL72 racks, each compute tray holds two superchips, which means four GPUs and two CPUs, plus their network cards and liquid cooling. *(Chapter 34)*
- **Concept drift** — A change over time in the relationship between inputs and the correct answer, so patterns the model learned no longer hold. *(Chapter 10)*
- **Concurrently maintainable** — A design in which any single component can be taken out of service for planned maintenance without shutting down the IT equipment. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Conditional computation (sparse activation)** — Running only part of a network for each input, chosen by the input itself. MoE is the main example: most experts sit idle for any given token. *(Chapter 17)*
- **Conditional probability** — The probability that A happens given that B is known to have happened, written P(A | B). You compute it by narrowing your attention to only the cases where B is true. *(Chapter 6)*
- **Conduction** — Heat moving through a solid, or a still liquid, by direct contact, the way a metal spoon warms up in hot soup. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Confidence interval** — A range that probably contains the true value of a measurement, allowing for the random wobble that comes from testing only a sample. A 95% interval is built so that, over many repeats, it would contain the true value 95% of the time. *(Chapter 24)*
- **Confounder** — A hidden factor that influences two things at once and makes them look causally linked when they are not, like summer heat behind both ice-cream sales and drownings. *(Chapter 6)*
- **Confusion matrix** — A table that counts a classifier's predictions by type: true positives, false positives, false negatives and true negatives for a yes-or-no task, or every combination of actual and predicted class for a task with more classes. *(Chapter 9)*
- **Connectionism** — The approach of building intelligence from large networks of simple connected units that learn from data, rather than from hand-written rules. *(Chapter 7)*
- **Connector** — An add-on that lets a chat app reach another service, such as Gmail, Google Drive, Slack, a calendar or GitHub, so the model can search or act on your data there. Different apps call these connectors, apps or plugins. *(Chapter 25, Chapter 28)*
- **Constitutional AI** — Anthropic's method (2022) of training a model with AI feedback guided by a written list of principles. The model critiques and revises its own answers, and an AI judge applying the principles produces preference labels for reinforcement learning. *(Chapter 20)*
- **Constitutional classifiers** — Anthropic's safety classifiers, trained on synthetic examples generated from a written list of allowed and disallowed content. *(Chapter 20)*
- **Constitution (for an AI)** — A foundational document describing a model's values, priorities and character, used both to guide training and to explain the model's intended behavior to the public. *(Chapter 20)*
- **Constrained decoding** — The technique behind structured outputs. At each step of generation, the model is only allowed to choose tokens that keep the output valid according to the schema, so invalid JSON becomes impossible rather than merely unlikely. *(Chapter 26)*
- **Containment** — Doors at the ends of an aisle and panels over its top that enclose either the hot or the cold aisle, so that hot and cold air cannot mix and cooling energy is not wasted. *(Chapter 38, Chapter 40)*
- **Content-based filtering** — Recommending items whose attributes (genre, author, ingredients) resemble those of items the user already liked. *(Chapter 10)*
- **Content block** — One piece of a response or message. A response is a list of blocks, which can include text, thinking (the model's reasoning, often hidden or summarized), and tool-use requests. Messages you send can include text, image and document blocks. *(Chapter 26)*
- **Context engineering** — Deciding everything that goes into the model's context window at each step (instructions, documents, tool results, memory, conversation history) and what stays out. Anthropic defines it as "the set of strategies for curating and maintaining the optimal set of tokens (information) during LLM inference". *(Chapter 25)*
- **Context extension** — Techniques for making a trained model handle longer texts than it was mostly trained on, typically by rescaling its position encoding (as YaRN does with RoPE) and briefly training on long documents. *(Chapter 15, Chapter 19)*
- **Context parallelism (ring attention)** — Splitting one very long sequence across many GPUs, each holding a chunk of tokens and its part of the KV cache, and passing keys and values around a ring so every chunk can attend to every other. *(Chapter 23, Chapter 37)*
- **Context rot** — The decline in a model's recall and accuracy as its context window fills with more and more tokens. Even when everything technically fits, the model attends less reliably to any one piece. *(Chapter 25, Chapter 27)*
- **Contextual embedding** — A word's vector computed from the specific sentence it appears in, so the same word gets different vectors in different contexts. Every modern language model works with contextual embeddings. *(Chapter 14)*
- **Contextual retrieval** — A technique that adds a short, AI-written explanation of where each chunk sits in its document (which document, which section, what it refers to) before the chunk is embedded and keyword-indexed, so that chunks that depend on context can still be found. *(Chapter 27)*
- **Context vector** — The vector a decoder uses as its summary of the input. In early seq2seq it was simply the encoder's final hidden state; with attention it is recomputed for every output word. *(Chapter 14)*
- **Context window** — The maximum amount of text, measured in tokens, that a model can consider at once, including your whole conversation and any files you attach (see Chapter 18). *(Chapter 7, Chapter 15, Chapter 18, Chapter 25, Chapter 27, Chapter 33)*
- **Continual learning** — A model's ability to keep learning from experience after deployment, updating what it knows instead of being frozen at its training cutoff. Today's models mostly learn only within their context window. *(Chapter 24)*
- **Continuous batching** — Scheduling at the level of individual decode steps: after every step, finished requests leave the batch and waiting requests join immediately, so the GPU's slots stay full. It is standard in modern serving engines. *(Chapter 23)*
- **Contrastive learning** — Training embeddings by pulling matching pairs (a photo and its caption) close together and pushing non-matching pairs apart, so that closeness in the space means similarity in meaning. *(Chapter 16, Chapter 22, Chapter 27)*
- **ControlNet** — An add-on network that steers a diffusion model with structural guides such as edge maps, human poses or depth maps, while leaving the original model unchanged. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Convection** — Heat carried away by a moving fluid, such as air pushed by a fan or water pumped through a pipe. Almost all data center cooling is forced convection. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Convolution** — Sliding a small grid of weights across an input (such as an image) and, at each position, multiplying the overlapping numbers together and adding them up. The result is a new grid that shows where the pattern in the weights was found. *(Chapter 14)*
- **Convolutional neural network (CNN)** — A neural network for images that slides small learned filters across the picture to detect patterns like edges, then shapes, then objects (see Chapter 14). *(Chapter 7)*
- **Cooling tower** — An outdoor structure in which warm water trickles over packing material while fans pull air through it; a small part of the water evaporates, and that evaporation cools the rest. Very energy-efficient, but it consumes water. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Coordinate** — One number that gives a position along one direction (an axis) of a graph. The point (3, 5) has an x-coordinate of 3 and a y-coordinate of 5. *(Chapter 5)*
- **Co-packaged optics (CPO)** — Placing the optical converters right next to the switch chip (or eventually the GPU) inside the same package, instead of in pluggable modules at the front panel. It shortens the power-hungry electrical path and cuts energy per bit. *(Chapter 34)*
- **COP (coefficient of performance)** — Heat moved divided by electricity used. A chiller with a COP of 5 removes 5 kW of heat for every 1 kW of electricity. Large water-cooled chillers often reach around 5–7; air-cooled ones are lower, often around 3. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Copilot and autopilot** — Two product stances. A copilot suggests and the human decides; an autopilot acts on its own within set limits. Most products mix both, choosing separately for each kind of action. *(Chapter 31)*
- **Copyright** — The legal rights an author has over an original creative work, including control over copying and distribution. It covers most books, articles, photos, music and code automatically, without registration. *(Chapter 11)*
- **Core** — One independent processing unit inside a chip, with its own control unit, ALU and registers. A CPU has a handful to a few hundred large cores; a GPU has thousands of small ones. *(Chapter 2, Chapter 32)*
- **Corpus** — A large collection of text used for training. The plural is corpora. *(Chapter 19, Chapter 27)*
- **Correlation** — A measure, from −1 to 1, of how strongly two quantities tend to move together in a straight-line way. It describes a pattern; it does not say why the pattern exists. *(Chapter 6)*
- **Cosine decay** — A schedule that lowers the learning rate from its peak to near zero along a smooth half cosine curve: slowly at first, faster in the middle, gently at the end. *(Chapter 13)*
- **Cosine similarity** — A score from −1 to 1 measuring how closely two vectors point in the same direction, ignoring their lengths. It is the dot product divided by both vectors' lengths: 1 means same direction, 0 unrelated, −1 opposite. *(Chapter 5, Chapter 14, Chapter 27)*
- **Cost per million tokens** — What it costs (to the provider) or what it is priced (to you) to process one million tokens. For providers, it is roughly the hourly cost of the hardware divided by millions of tokens produced per hour. *(Chapter 23, Chapter 37, Chapter 44)*
- **Cost per task** — The money (usually compute cost) a model spends to complete one benchmark task or one real job. Two models with similar scores can differ in cost per task by 100× or more. *(Chapter 24)*
- **CoT faithfulness** — Whether a model's written chain of thought accurately reflects the factors that actually drove its answer. *(Chapter 21)*
- **CoT monitoring** — Using another model or a person to read a reasoning model's chain of thought (and its actions) to catch signs of bad intent, such as planning to cheat a test. *(Chapter 21)*
- **CoWoS (Chip-on-Wafer-on-Substrate)** — TSMC's packaging technology that places GPU dies and HBM stacks side by side on a silicon interposer. Its limited capacity has been a key bottleneck for AI accelerators since 2023. *(Chapter 33, Chapter 34, Chapter 36)*
- **CPU (central processing unit)** — The general-purpose processor that runs a computer's programs one instruction after another. It is fast and flexible, excellent at varied, step-by-step work with many decisions, but it does only a limited number of calculations at once. *(Chapter 2, Chapter 32)*
- **CRAC (computer room air conditioner)** — A room air-conditioning unit with its own built-in refrigeration compressor that cools and circulates the air in a data hall. *(Chapter 40)*
- **CRAH (computer room air handler)** — A fan-and-coil unit that cools a data hall's air by blowing it across coils filled with chilled water from a central plant. It has no compressor of its own. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Credit assignment** — Working out which of many actions deserves credit or blame for a final result. With a single reward at the end of a long reasoning chain, the model cannot directly tell which step was the clever one or the mistake. *(Chapter 21)*
- **Cross-attention** — Attention in which one sequence's queries look at another sequence's keys and values, for example a translation decoder looking at the encoded source sentence, or an image generator looking at the text prompt. *(Chapter 15, Chapter 16)*
- **Cross-connect** — A physical cable, usually fiber, run inside the meet-me room to link one party's equipment directly to another's, for example a tenant to a carrier, or two tenants to each other. Colocation operators charge a monthly fee per cross-connect. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Cross-encoder** — A model that reads the question and one passage together and outputs a relevance score. It is far more accurate than comparing separate vectors, but too slow to run against millions of passages, so it is used on a shortlist. *(Chapter 27)*
- **Cross-entropy (log loss)** — The standard loss for models that predict categories or next tokens: the average of minus the log of the probability the model gave to each correct answer. It measures how surprised the model was by the truth. *(Chapter 6, Chapter 8)*
- **Cross-entropy loss** — The standard loss for classification and next-token prediction: the negative logarithm of the probability the model gave to the correct answer. It measures how surprised the model was by the truth. *(Chapter 12, Chapter 15, Chapter 19)*
- **Cross-validation (k-fold)** — Cutting the data into k equal parts called folds, then training k times, each time holding out a different fold for validation and training on the rest, and finally averaging the k scores. Five and ten folds are common. *(Chapter 9)*
- **CSV (comma-separated values)** — A plain-text table where each line is a row and commas separate the columns. Every spreadsheet program can open and save it. *(Chapter 3)*
- **CUDA** — NVIDIA's software platform, released in 2007, that lets programmers run general calculations on its GPUs. The huge ecosystem of tools built on it is a major reason NVIDIA dominates AI hardware. *(Chapter 2, Chapter 7, Chapter 32, Chapter 35)*
- **CUDA core** — NVIDIA's name for a GPU's basic arithmetic unit, which handles ordinary (non-matrix) math one number at a time per thread. *(Chapter 32)*
- **CUDA, Triton and kernels** — CUDA is NVIDIA's platform for programming its GPUs; Triton is a Python-like language for writing GPU code; a kernel is one optimized routine that runs on the GPU, such as a fast matrix multiplication. *(Chapter 13)*
- **CUE (carbon usage effectiveness)** — Kilograms of carbon dioxide emitted per kWh of IT energy. It equals PUE multiplied by the carbon intensity of the electricity the site uses. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Custom GPT or Gem** — A personalized assistant (OpenAI's and Google's names) with its own instructions, knowledge files and tools, which you can reuse and share with others. Claude offers similar packaging through Projects and Skills. *(Chapter 25)*
- **Custom instructions** — Preferences you set once ("I'm a high-school teacher; be concise; use metric units") that the app adds to the context of every chat. They work like a small system prompt that you control. *(Chapter 25)*
- **CVE (Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures)** — The public catalog of known software security flaws, each with an ID such as CVE-2025-6514. A companion scale, CVSS, rates each flaw's severity from 0 to 10. *(Chapter 31)*
- **CXL (Compute Express Link)** — An open standard, built on the PCIe connector, that lets processors attach and share extra memory. It allows servers to pool memory beyond what sits on their own boards. *(Chapter 33)*

## D

- **Dangerous-capability evaluation** — A test of whether a model could meaningfully help someone cause serious harm, for example in cyberattacks or biological weapons, or act dangerously on its own. Results decide which safeguards a model needs before release (see Chapter 57). *(Chapter 24)*
- **DAPO (Decoupled Clip and Dynamic Sampling Policy Optimization)** — A 2025 variant of GRPO that adds a looser upper clip, filtering of groups that give no signal, token-level loss weighting and gentle penalties for overlong answers. *(Chapter 21)*
- **Dark fiber** — Optical fiber that has been laid but is not "lit" by any equipment. A company can lease it and attach its own lasers and receivers, gaining a private, very high-capacity link it controls completely. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Data** — Recorded facts or measurements: numbers, text, images, sounds, clicks. In AI, data is what a model learns from. *(Chapter 11)*
- **Data augmentation** — Creating extra training examples by changing existing ones in ways that should not change the answer, such as flipping, cropping or brightening a photo, or paraphrasing a sentence. *(Chapter 9, Chapter 13, Chapter 14)*
- **Database** — Organized storage software designed to save, search and update large amounts of data quickly and safely. *(Chapter 3, Chapter 11, Chapter 29)*
- **Data center** — A building packed with computers, network equipment, power equipment and cooling. When you use a chatbot, the real work happens on specialized chips inside one of these buildings, not on your phone. *(Chapter 1, Chapter 4, Chapter 38)*
- **Data cleaning** — Finding and fixing incorrect, duplicate, missing or inconsistent data, such as merging duplicate records, standardizing spellings and units, and correcting impossible values. *(Chapter 11)*
- **Data curation** — Cleaning, filtering, deduplicating and balancing raw data before training, so that the model learns from useful, varied, legal text. *(Chapter 19)*
- **Data drift** — A change over time in the inputs a model sees, so they no longer resemble the training data. *(Chapter 10)*
- **Data exfiltration** — Getting private data out of a system to an attacker, for example by making an AI agent include it in a link, an image address, an email or a web request. *(Chapter 31)*
- **Data exhaust** — Data produced as a side effect of everyday digital activity, such as clicks, searches, location pings and app usage, rather than collected on purpose. *(Chapter 11)*
- **Dataflow architecture** — A chip design in which the hardware is configured so data streams through a fixed arrangement of compute units for a given model, rather than fetching instructions one at a time. SambaNova calls its version a Reconfigurable Dataflow Unit (RDU). *(Chapter 35)*
- **Data hall** — One large room of white space inside a data center building, often designed for 5 to 30 or more megawatts of IT equipment in AI designs. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Data lake** — A large, low-cost store of raw data in many formats, kept as-is and cleaned only when someone needs it. *(Chapter 11)*
- **Data leakage** — When information that would not be available at prediction time, or information from the test set, sneaks into training, producing scores that look excellent and mean nothing. *(Chapter 9)*
- **Data mixture** — The share of training tokens drawn from each source, such as web text, code, math, books, each language and synthetic data. *(Chapter 19)*
- **Data parallelism (DP)** — Splitting the training data across GPUs that each hold a full copy of the model, then averaging their gradients after every step so all copies stay in sync. *(Chapter 37)*
- **Data pipeline** — An automated series of steps that moves data from its sources, transforms it, and delivers it to where it is used, such as a warehouse, a dashboard or a training job. *(Chapter 11)*
- **Data poisoning** — Deliberately planting false or malicious content in data an AI system learns from or retrieves, such as a fake policy page added to a wiki, to steer its answers. *(Chapter 27)*
- **Data quality** — How fit data is for its purpose, usually judged on accuracy, completeness, consistency, timeliness, validity and uniqueness. *(Chapter 11)*
- **Data residency** — A guarantee that your requests are processed in a particular region, such as the US or the EU, often required by law or company policy and sometimes sold at a premium. *(Chapter 26)*
- **Data retention** — How long a provider keeps your prompts, files and responses after processing them, and for what purposes (safety review, legal holds, model training). *(Chapter 25)*
- **Dataset** — An organized collection of examples (texts, images, labeled records) used to train or test AI. *(Chapter 3, Chapter 8, Chapter 11)*
- **Datasheet (data card)** — Standard documentation describing a dataset's origin, contents, collection and labeling process, intended uses, limitations and license. *(Chapter 11)*
- **Data type** — The kind of value something is: a whole number (integer), a decimal (float), text (string), true-or-false (boolean), a list, and so on. The type decides what you can do with it. *(Chapter 3)*
- **Data wall** — The idea that AI progress could stall because the supply of high-quality, human-generated training data is running out. *(Chapter 11, Chapter 19)*
- **Data warehouse** — A central store of cleaned, structured data organized for analysis and reporting. *(Chapter 11)*
- **DBSCAN** — A density-based clustering method that grows clusters through crowded regions, finds clusters of any shape and labels isolated points as noise. *(Chapter 10)*
- **DCI (data center interconnect)** — High-capacity links, usually over leased dark fiber or wavelengths, that connect data centers to each other within a metro area or across long distances. *(Chapter 38)*
- **DCIM (data center infrastructure management)** — Software that tracks a data center's assets, space, power and cooling capacity rack by rack, so operators know what is installed where and how much headroom is left. *(Chapter 38)*
- **DDIM (denoising diffusion implicit model)** — A 2020 sampler that takes larger, non-random steps along the same path as DDPM, producing good images in around 50 steps instead of 1,000. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Debugging** — Finding the cause of a bug and fixing it. It is a normal, everyday part of programming, not a sign of failure. *(Chapter 3)*
- **Decision boundary** — The dividing line (or, with more features, the dividing surface) where a classifier switches from predicting one class to another. *(Chapter 8, Chapter 12)*
- **Decision tree** — A model shaped like a flowchart of yes/no questions about the features ("Months subscribed 5 or more?"), learned from data, with a prediction stored at each final branch. *(Chapter 10)*
- **Decode** — The second phase of answering, in which the model generates the response one token at a time, each new token depending on all the tokens before it. Its speed sets how fast the text streams. *(Chapter 4, Chapter 23, Chapter 33, Chapter 37, Chapter 44)*
- **Decoder** — The part of a model that takes an internal representation and produces the output, usually one piece at a time, each piece feeding into the next. *(Chapter 14, Chapter 16)*
- **Decoder-only model** — A Transformer in which each token can only see itself and earlier tokens, trained to predict the next token. The GPT family and every major chatbot, including ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini, are decoder-only. *(Chapter 15)*
- **Decontamination** — Removing benchmark test questions (and close copies of them) from training data, so that evaluation scores reflect real ability rather than memory. *(Chapter 19, Chapter 24)*
- **Deduplication** — Finding and removing repeated or near-identical records. At web scale it is critical, because duplicated text makes a model memorize and wastes training compute. *(Chapter 11, Chapter 19)*
- **Deep learning** — Machine learning using neural networks with many layers. It has powered most AI progress since about 2012, including image recognition, speech recognition and chatbots. *(Chapter 1, Chapter 5, Chapter 7, Chapter 12)*
- **Deep research** — A chat-app mode in which the AI spends several minutes running many searches, reading sources and then writing a long report with citations. *(Chapter 25)*
- **Defect density (D0)** — The average number of fatal defects per square centimeter of wafer. Mature processes reach around 0.1 or lower; new processes start higher and improve over time. *(Chapter 36)*
- **Deliberative alignment** — A safety-training method, introduced by OpenAI in late 2024 for its reasoning models, in which the model learns to recall the relevant parts of its written spec and reason about them explicitly before answering. *(Chapter 20)*
- **Delimiter** — A marker that separates parts of a prompt, such as triple quotes, a line of dashes, Markdown headings or XML tags. *(Chapter 25)*
- **Delta rule** — An update rule for a memory matrix that first checks what the memory currently returns for a key and then writes only the difference from the new value, so outdated information is overwritten instead of piled up. *(Chapter 17)*
- **Dennard scaling** — The historical rule that as transistors shrank, their power use shrank in proportion, so chips could get denser and faster without running hotter. It broke down in the mid-2000s, ending the era of rapidly rising clock speeds. *(Chapter 2, Chapter 32)*
- **Denoiser (noise-prediction network)** — The neural network at the heart of a diffusion model. Given a noisy input, the timestep and the prompt, it predicts the noise that was added (or, in some variants, the clean image). It is usually a U-Net or a Transformer. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Dense layer (fully connected layer)** — A layer in which every neuron receives every output of the previous layer. Mathematically it is one matrix multiplication plus a bias, followed by an activation. *(Chapter 12)*
- **Dense model** — A model that uses all of its weights for every token. A 70-billion-parameter dense model does about 70 billion multiply-adds for each token it reads or writes. *(Chapter 17, Chapter 18)*
- **Dense vs. sparse FLOPS** — A dense figure counts every multiplication. A sparse figure assumes a special pattern in which half the numbers are zero and can be skipped, which doubles the headline. Dense is the realistic baseline; most real workloads do not get the sparse speed-up. *(Chapter 35)*
- **Dependency** — A piece of outside code (a library or package) that your project relies on. A typical web app has dozens or hundreds of dependencies, each one a possible source of bugs or security problems. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Deploy** — Copying a version of your code onto the servers where it will run for real users, and switching it on. Many teams deploy several times a day. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Deposition** — Adding a thin layer of material across a wafer, for example by letting gases react on its surface. The most precise form, atomic layer deposition (ALD), grows a film one layer of atoms at a time. *(Chapter 36)*
- **Deprecation** — A provider announcing, then carrying out, the retirement of an older model. Code that names a retired model stops working, so production systems track deprecation notices and re-test before switching. *(Chapter 26, Chapter 31)*
- **Depth** — The number of layers in a network. A deeper network can build more complex features out of simpler ones, step by step. *(Chapter 12)*
- **Derivative (slope)** — The slope of a function at a single point: how much the output changes for a tiny nudge of the input, divided by the size of the nudge. It answers "if I nudge this input a little, how much does the output move, and in which direction?" *(Chapter 6, Chapter 12)*
- **Design partner (custom silicon partner)** — A company such as Broadcom, Marvell, MediaTek or Alchip that turns a customer's chip architecture into a manufacturable product, supplying high-speed I/O circuits, physical design, packaging and the relationship with the foundry. *(Chapter 35)*
- **Dictionary** — A collection of labeled values, written in curly brackets, such as {"name": "Ada", "age": 36}. You look up a value by its label (its "key"). *(Chapter 3)*
- **Die** — A single rectangular piece of silicon, cut from a larger round wafer, that contains one chip's circuitry. A modern AI chip package may contain two or more dies plus separate memory stacks. *(Chapter 2, Chapter 32, Chapter 34, Chapter 36)*
- **Dielectric fluid** — A liquid that does not conduct electricity, so it can touch powered electronics without causing short circuits. Mineral oils, synthetic oils and certain engineered fluorinated fluids are examples. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Diff** — A side-by-side or line-by-line view of what changed between two versions of a file: lines removed are usually shown with a minus sign and lines added with a plus sign. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Differential privacy** — A mathematical guarantee, achieved by adding calibrated random noise, that a result barely changes whether or not any single person's data is included, so no individual can be singled out. *(Chapter 11)*
- **Diffusion language model (dLLM)** — A text generator that starts from a fully masked answer and fills in many tokens in parallel over several refinement passes, instead of writing strictly one token at a time from left to right. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Diffusion model** — An image (or video) generator that learns to turn random noise into a picture step by step, guided by a text description (see Chapter 16). *(Chapter 7, Chapter 16, Chapter 22)*
- **Dimension** — How many numbers a vector has. [3, 120, 15] is a 3-dimensional vector; the vectors inside a chatbot typically have thousands of dimensions. *(Chapter 5, Chapter 27)*
- **Dimensionality reduction** — Compressing many features into a few new ones while keeping the important information, such as summarizing a 20-question personality survey into two scores. *(Chapter 8, Chapter 10)*
- **Direct and indirect prompt injection** — Direct injection is typed by the person using the system ("ignore your previous instructions and..."). Indirect injection is hidden inside content the AI reads on the user's behalf, such as a web page, email, PDF, product review or tool result; there, the user is the victim rather than the attacker. *(Chapter 31)*
- **Direct-to-chip (DTC) liquid cooling** — Cooling in which coolant is pumped through cold plates sitting directly on GPUs and CPUs. It is the standard approach for Blackwell-class AI racks. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Direct water** — Water used on the data center site itself, mainly for cooling towers and evaporative coolers. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Disaggregated serving** — Running prefill and decode on separate pools of chips, each sized and tuned for its phase, and transferring each request's KV cache from the prefill pool to the decode pool over a fast network. *(Chapter 23, Chapter 33, Chapter 35, Chapter 37)*
- **Discriminative model** — A model that takes an input and predicts a label or a number about it, such as "spam" or "cat, 97%". It learns to tell things apart, not to create them. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Discriminator** — In a GAN, the network that looks at an example and outputs the probability that it is real rather than generated. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Distillation** — Training a smaller, cheaper "student" model to imitate a larger, more capable "teacher" model, using the teacher's outputs or its full probability distributions as training targets. *(Chapter 20, Chapter 24, Chapter 30)*
- **Distillation attack** — Mass querying of another company's model, often through fake or resold accounts, to collect outputs for training a competing model, usually in violation of the provider's terms of service. *(Chapter 20)*
- **Distributed training** — Training one model on many accelerators at once, with the work and the memory split among them and the chips coordinating constantly over fast networks. *(Chapter 37)*
- **Distributional hypothesis** — The idea that words appearing in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings. "Coffee" and "tea" both appear near "cup," "hot" and "drink," so they must be related. *(Chapter 14)*
- **Distribution shift** — When the data a model meets in real use differs from the data it was trained and tested on, such as a model trained on one hospital's patients being used at another hospital, or a shopping model trained before a pandemic being used during one. Also called data drift. *(Chapter 9)*
- **District heating** — A network of insulated underground pipes that delivers hot water from central sources to heat many buildings in a town, common in Scandinavia, Germany and other cold regions. *(Chapter 40)*
- **DiT (Diffusion Transformer)** — A diffusion model whose denoiser is a Transformer working on patches of the latent instead of a U-Net. It improves smoothly with more parameters and compute and is the standard design for modern image and video generators. *(Chapter 16)*
- **DNS (Domain Name System)** — The internet's phone book, which turns names like claude.ai into the numeric IP addresses computers use. *(Chapter 4)*
- **Document understanding** — Making sense of a whole document, including layout, headings, tables, forms, checkboxes, signatures and how parts relate, not just its characters. *(Chapter 22)*
- **Domain name** — A human-readable name for an internet address, such as claude.ai. The ending (.com, .ai, .org) is called the top-level domain. *(Chapter 4)*
- **Doping** — Adding tiny, controlled amounts of other elements (such as boron or phosphorus) to silicon to change how easily it conducts, usually by firing a beam of charged atoms into the wafer ("ion implantation") and then heating it. It creates the "switch" regions of a transistor. *(Chapter 36)*
- **Dot product** — Multiply two equal-length vectors entry by entry and add up the results. [2, 1, 3] · [1.50, 4.00, 0.50] = 3 + 4 + 1.5 = 8.5. A large dot product means the vectors "agree". *(Chapter 5, Chapter 27)*
- **Double descent** — The surprising finding that test error can fall, rise and then fall again as models grow far beyond the size needed to fit the training data exactly. It shows that the classic bias–variance picture is incomplete for very large models. *(Chapter 9, Chapter 13)*
- **Doubling time** — How long something growing exponentially takes to double. Something that doubles every 5.2 months grows about 5 times in a year. *(Chapter 6)*
- **DPO (Direct Preference Optimization)** — A post-training method (2023) that learns directly from pairs of chosen and rejected responses, with no separate reward model and no reinforcement-learning loop. It raises the probability of chosen responses relative to rejected ones, measured against a frozen reference model. *(Chapter 20, Chapter 30)*
- **DPU (Data Processing Unit) and SuperNIC** — Network cards with their own processors that take over networking, storage and security chores from the main CPU (examples: NVIDIA BlueField, AMD Pensando). NVIDIA uses "SuperNIC" for its fastest GPU-facing network cards. *(Chapter 34)*
- **Draft model** — The small, fast model (or set of extra prediction heads) that proposes tokens for speculative decoding. It only needs to guess well on easy, predictable tokens. *(Chapter 23)*
- **Dragonfly** — A topology in which chips or switches are organized into groups that are densely connected inside, and every group has direct links to every other group, keeping the number of hops between any two points small. *(Chapter 34)*
- **DRAM (dynamic RAM)** — Dense, cheaper memory that stores each bit as a tiny electric charge in a capacitor guarded by one transistor. The charge leaks, so it must be refreshed many times per second. It is the main memory of computers and the material HBM is made from. *(Chapter 33)*
- **Dr. GRPO** — A 2025 variant of GRPO that removes the per-answer length normalization and the standard-deviation scaling, fixing biases that made wrong answers grow long. *(Chapter 21)*
- **Dropout** — A regularization trick for neural networks that randomly switches off a fraction of the network's units during each training step, so no single unit can become a crutch. Chapter 13 covers it. *(Chapter 9, Chapter 13, Chapter 14)*
- **Dry cooler** — A large radiator with fans that cools liquid using outside air, without evaporating any water. It saves water but cannot cool the liquid below the outdoor air temperature, so it struggles on hot days. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Dual-use capability** — An ability that can serve both good and harmful ends. A model that finds software vulnerabilities helps defenders patch them and could help attackers exploit them. *(Chapter 24)*
- **DUV (deep ultraviolet) lithography** — Lithography using 193 nm (or older 248 nm) ultraviolet laser light. It prints features down to about 38 nm in one exposure and can reach 7 nm-class chips with multiple exposures. *(Chapter 36)*
- **DyT (Dynamic Tanh)** — A 2025 proposal to replace normalization layers with a learned squashing function, tanh(α × x), which matched normalized Transformers in many experiments. *(Chapter 13, Chapter 17)*

## E

- **Early fusion** — Building a multimodal model that mixes tokens from different media in one network from the start of training, so it learns them together. *(Chapter 16, Chapter 22)*
- **Early stopping** — Halting training when the validation error stops improving, even though the training error could still go down. It catches the moment in Figure 9.4 just before memorization sets in. *(Chapter 9, Chapter 13)*
- **Economizer** — Equipment that enables free cooling. An air-side economizer brings filtered outdoor air into the hall when it is cool enough; a water-side economizer lets dry coolers or towers cool the facility water directly, bypassing the chillers. *(Chapter 40)*
- **EDA (electronic design automation)** — The software used to design and verify chips. Three companies dominate: Synopsys and Cadence (US) and Siemens EDA (owned by Germany's Siemens). No leading-edge chip can be designed without their tools. *(Chapter 36)*
- **Edge case** — An unusual but legitimate input at the boundary of the rules, such as a return requested a few days after the deadline or a question in another language. *(Chapter 31)*
- **Edge data center** — A small data center placed close to users, for example in a city or at a cell tower site, to cut delay for applications that need fast responses. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Effort** — A setting on many reasoning models that controls how much they think before answering. Low effort is fast and cheap; high effort is thorough and slower. *(Chapter 25)*
- **Egress allow-list** — A network rule that lets a system connect only to a short list of approved internet addresses and blocks every other outbound connection. It closes many exfiltration routes at once. *(Chapter 31)*
- **ELIZA effect** — The human tendency to read understanding, feelings or intentions into a computer program's replies, named after Joseph Weizenbaum's simple 1960s chatbot. *(Chapter 7)*
- **Elo rating** — A scoring system, borrowed from chess, that rates competitors by who beats whom in head-to-head comparisons. A higher Elo means the model's outputs win more often; differences between ratings matter more than the absolute numbers. *(Chapter 24)*
- **Embedding** — A vector that represents a word, token, image, song or anything else, arranged so that similar things get similar vectors. The imaginary space these vectors live in is called the embedding space, a map where distance means difference in meaning. *(Chapter 5, Chapter 7, Chapter 10, Chapter 14, Chapter 18, Chapter 27)*
- **Embedding matrix** — A big table of learned numbers with one row per vocabulary token. Looking up a token's row gives its starting vector. It is learned during training like every other weight. *(Chapter 15)*
- **Embedding model** — A neural network whose only job is to turn text (or images) into embedding vectors. It reads a passage and outputs one vector. It is much smaller and cheaper than a chat model, and the same text always produces the same vector. *(Chapter 27)*
- **Embedding space (vector space)** — The imaginary space where embeddings live, with one direction per number in the vector. Distances and directions in it carry meaning. *(Chapter 14)*
- **Embedding table (embedding matrix)** — A large table inside a language model with one row of numbers (one vector) per token in its vocabulary. Turning a token ID into a vector is just looking up its row. Also called the embedding matrix. *(Chapter 5, Chapter 14)*
- **Embodied AI** — AI that perceives and acts through a physical body, such as a robot arm, a humanoid or a car, rather than only through text and screens. *(Chapter 22)*
- **Embodied carbon** — The emissions released in manufacturing and constructing something, such as the concrete, steel, copper and chips in a data center, as opposed to the emissions from running it. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Emergent abilities** — Capabilities that appear to be absent in smaller models and present in larger ones, seemingly arriving suddenly as scale increases. Whether they are truly sudden is debated. *(Chapter 19)*
- **Encoder** — The part of a model that reads the input and turns it into an internal numerical representation. *(Chapter 14, Chapter 16)*
- **Encoder-decoder model** — A Transformer with both halves: an encoder reads the input fully, and a decoder generates the output while attending to it through cross-attention. The original Transformer, Google's T5 and OpenAI's Whisper speech recognizer use this layout. *(Chapter 15)*
- **Encoder-only model** — A Transformer whose tokens can all see each other in both directions, built to understand text rather than generate it. It is used for classification, search and embeddings; BERT is the classic example. *(Chapter 15)*
- **Encoding** — An agreed rule for turning something (letters, colors, sounds) into numbers and back again. The same numbers mean nothing without the encoding that explains them. *(Chapter 3)*
- **Encryption** — Scrambling data with a secret key so that only someone with the right key can unscramble and read it. *(Chapter 4)*
- **Endpoint** — The specific URL where an API receives a particular kind of request, such as https://api.anthropic.com/v1/messages. *(Chapter 4, Chapter 26)*
- **Endpointing (turn detection)** — Deciding that the speaker has finished their turn, rather than merely pausing to think, so the assistant can start replying. Too eager and it interrupts you; too cautious and it feels sluggish. *(Chapter 22)*
- **End-to-end encryption** — Encryption where only the people communicating can read messages, not even the company running the service. Chatbots cannot work this way, because the provider's model must read your text. *(Chapter 4)*
- **Ensemble** — A combination of several models whose predictions are merged, by voting or averaging, so that their individual errors partly cancel out. *(Chapter 10)*
- **Enterprise data center** — A data center owned and operated by an ordinary company (a bank, a hospital, a retailer) for its own use, often on its own premises ("on-prem"). *(Chapter 38)*
- **Entity List** — A US government list of foreign companies that need a special license before receiving US technology. Huawei and SMIC are on it. *(Chapter 36)*
- **Entropy collapse** — A failure in RL training in which the model becomes too certain too early, always choosing the same tokens, so it stops exploring alternatives and stops improving. *(Chapter 21)*
- **Environment** — The world, game or simulation that a reinforcement learning agent acts in, and which responds to its actions. *(Chapter 8)*
- **Episodic, semantic and procedural memory** — Three kinds of agent memory borrowed from psychology: episodic is what happened (a log of past sessions), semantic is facts (the user's preferences, a customer's history), and procedural is how to do things (instructions and skills). *(Chapter 28)*
- **Epoch (training pass)** — One complete pass through the entire training dataset. Small models often train for many epochs; frontier language models see most of their data only about once. *(Chapter 6, Chapter 8, Chapter 12, Chapter 19, Chapter 30)*
- **ERE (energy reuse effectiveness)** — Total facility energy minus the energy reused elsewhere (usually as heat), divided by IT energy. Unlike PUE, it can drop below 1.0 if a site exports enough useful heat. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Error message (traceback)** — The report a program prints when it crashes, listing where the problem happened and what kind of error it was. Read it from the bottom up. *(Chapter 3)*
- **Error (residual)** — The difference between what a model predicted and what actually happened for one example. A good model has small errors on examples it has never seen. *(Chapter 5)*
- **Etching** — Removing material from a wafer where it is not protected by resist, using reactive gases, plasma or liquid chemicals, so that the pattern is carved into the layer below. *(Chapter 36)*
- **Ethernet** — The world's standard networking technology, used in nearly every office, home and data center. Many companies make Ethernet switches, and newer versions are tuned for AI back-ends (for example NVIDIA Spectrum-X and Broadcom's Tomahawk switch chips). *(Chapter 34)*
- **ETL and ELT** — Two pipeline styles. Extract–Transform–Load cleans data before storing it; Extract–Load–Transform stores raw data first and cleans it later, inside the storage system. *(Chapter 11)*
- **Euclidean distance** — The straight-line distance between two points, found by subtracting the vectors and taking the magnitude of the result. It is the "as the crow flies" distance, extended to any number of dimensions. *(Chapter 5, Chapter 10)*
- **Euler's number (e)** — A constant, about 2.718, that is the natural base for growth and decay. Raising e to a power, written e^x or EXP(x), is the "exponential function" used throughout AI. *(Chapter 6)*
- **EUV (extreme ultraviolet) lithography** — Lithography using light with a 13.5 nm wavelength, created by blasting droplets of molten tin with a laser. It prints features around 13 nm in one exposure. ASML is the only supplier. *(Chapter 36)*
- **Eval-driven development** — Building AI features by writing or extending the eval first, confirming it fails, then changing the system until it passes without regressions elsewhere. *(Chapter 31)*
- **Eval (evaluation)** — A systematic test of how well an AI system performs on your tasks: a set of realistic inputs, a definition of a good answer, and a way of scoring outputs. Chapter 31 covers evals in depth. *(Chapter 26, Chapter 31)*
- **Evaluation (eval)** — Measuring how well a model performs, in a way that predicts how it will behave on real, new data. Good evaluation is honest about which mistakes the model makes and how sure we can be of the score. *(Chapter 9, Chapter 24)*
- **Evaluation harness** — The software setup used to run a benchmark: how questions are phrased, how many attempts are allowed, what tools and memory the model gets, and how answers are graded. Different harnesses can produce very different scores for the same model. *(Chapter 9)*
- **Evaluator-optimizer** — A loop in which one model produces work and another critiques it against criteria, repeating until the work passes or a limit is reached. *(Chapter 28)*
- **Exact match** — A scoring rule that gives credit only when the model's whole answer is exactly correct, with no partial credit. *(Chapter 19)*
- **Example** — One record in a dataset: one email, one house, one photo, one patient. Also called an instance, a sample or a row. *(Chapter 8)*
- **Excessive agency** — Giving an AI system more permissions, tools or autonomy than its task needs, so that a mistake or a manipulation can cause outsized damage. It is one of OWASP's top risks for AI applications. *(Chapter 31)*
- **Expected value** — The long-run average outcome of something random, found by multiplying each outcome by its probability and adding the results. A fair die's expected value is 3.5. *(Chapter 6)*
- **Expert** — One of the sub-networks inside an MoE layer, usually a feed-forward block of its own. Experts specialize in statistical token patterns, not in neat human subjects. *(Chapter 17)*
- **Expert capacity (capacity factor)** — The maximum number of tokens an expert accepts per batch, set as the fair share times a factor such as 1.25. It keeps any one chip from being overloaded. *(Chapter 17)*
- **Expert data** — Training and evaluation data written or judged by credentialed specialists, such as doctors, lawyers or PhD scientists, to teach and test models on hard professional tasks. *(Chapter 11)*
- **Expert parallelism (EP)** — Placing different experts on different chips and shipping each token to the chips that hold its chosen experts. *(Chapter 17, Chapter 23, Chapter 37, Chapter 44)*
- **Expert system** — A program from the 1970s and 1980s that encodes a human expert's knowledge as if-then rules in order to give advice in one narrow area, such as diagnosing infections or configuring computers. *(Chapter 7)*
- **Exploding gradient** — The opposite problem: learning signals grow as they pass backward through many layers, causing huge updates, unstable training and often NaN values. *(Chapter 13, Chapter 14)*
- **Exploratory data analysis (EDA)** — Examining data with summaries and charts before modeling, to understand it and spot problems such as missing values, odd distributions and errors. *(Chapter 10)*
- **Exponent and mantissa** — The two main parts of a floating-point number. The exponent sets how big or small the number is (its range); the mantissa holds its significant digits (its detail). *(Chapter 5)*
- **Exponential backoff** — A retry strategy in which you wait longer after each failed attempt (1 second, then 2, then 4, then 8), so a busy server gets breathing room instead of a flood of instant retries. *(Chapter 26)*
- **Exponential decay** — Shrinking by repeated multiplication by a number less than 1, such as halving every year. It falls fast at first and then flattens toward zero without ever quite reaching it. *(Chapter 6)*
- **Exponential growth** — Growth by repeated multiplication, such as doubling every year. It looks slow at first and then explodes, because each step adds more than the one before. *(Chapter 6)*
- **Exponential smoothing** — A forecasting method in which each new forecast is a weighted blend of the latest actual value and the previous forecast, so recent data counts most and older data fades. *(Chapter 10)*
- **Exponent (power)** — The small number that says how many times to multiply a number by itself. In 10^6 the exponent is 6, so 10^6 = 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000,000. *(Chapter 2, Chapter 6)*
- **Export controls** — Government rules that restrict selling certain technologies, such as advanced AI chips or chipmaking machines, to particular countries or companies. *(Chapter 7, Chapter 36)*
- **Extended thinking** — Anthropic's earlier mode in which a developer turned thinking on and set a fixed maximum number of thinking tokens. It has been replaced by adaptive thinking on newer models. *(Chapter 21)*

## F

- **F1 score** — A single number combining precision and recall, calculated as their harmonic mean: 2 × precision × recall ÷ (precision + recall). It is high only when both are high, so a model cannot score well by maximizing one and ignoring the other. *(Chapter 9)*
- **Fab (fabrication plant)** — A chip factory: a cleanroom building full of manufacturing tools that turn blank wafers into finished chips. A leading-edge fab costs on the order of $20 billion or more. *(Chapter 36)*
- **Fabless** — A chip company that designs chips but pays a foundry to manufacture them. NVIDIA, AMD, Broadcom, Qualcomm and Apple are all fabless. *(Chapter 35)*
- **Fabless company** — A chip company that designs chips but outsources the manufacturing. NVIDIA, AMD, Broadcom, Qualcomm and Apple are all fabless. *(Chapter 36)*
- **Facility water system (FWS)** — The building's main water loop that collects heat from CDUs and air handlers across the data halls and carries it to chillers, dry coolers or cooling towers outside. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Fair use** — A US legal doctrine allowing some unlicensed uses of copyrighted works, judged case by case on four factors, including the purpose of the use and its effect on the market for the original. *(Chapter 11)*
- **Faithfulness (groundedness)** — Whether every claim in an answer is actually supported by the retrieved passages, as opposed to added from the model's memory or invented. *(Chapter 27)*
- **False negative (FN)** — The model said no but the answer was yes: a miss, such as a sick person sent home. Statisticians call it a Type II error. *(Chapter 9)*
- **False positive (FP)** — A result that says "yes" when the truth is "no", such as a healthy person testing positive or a spam filter flagging a real email. A false negative is the reverse. *(Chapter 6, Chapter 9)*
- **False positive rate** — Of all the real negatives, the share wrongly flagged: FP ÷ (FP + TN). It equals 1 minus specificity. *(Chapter 9)*
- **Fan affinity laws** — Rules of thumb for fans and pumps: airflow rises in proportion to fan speed, but the power the fan uses rises with the cube of speed, so doubling the airflow takes roughly eight times the fan power. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Fast mode (priority tier)** — A premium option that delivers output faster for a higher per-token price, for example Claude Opus 5.5's fast mode at twice the standard rate. *(Chapter 23, Chapter 26)*
- **Fat tree (Clos or leaf-spine network)** — A layered network in which "leaf" switches connect to servers and every leaf connects to every "spine" switch above it, often with a third layer on top. Any two servers are only a few hops apart, and links get "fatter" (more total capacity) toward the top. *(Chapter 34)*
- **Fault tolerant** — A design that survives any single unplanned failure, anywhere in the power or cooling system, without affecting the IT equipment. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Feature engineering** — Creating more useful input features from raw data, such as turning a birth date into an age or a timestamp into "weekend or weekday." *(Chapter 11)*
- **Feature importance** — A score for each input feature showing how much it contributed to a model's predictions, for example how much the trees' impurity dropped thanks to splits on that feature. *(Chapter 10)*
- **Feature (learned)** — One measurable input fact about an example, such as square footage, number of links in an email, or the brightness of one pixel. In a table, each feature is a column. *(Chapter 8, Chapter 12)*
- **Feature map** — The grid of numbers produced by sliding one filter across an input. High values mark the places where the filter's pattern was found. *(Chapter 14)*
- **Feature scaling** — Rescaling numeric features so they have comparable ranges, for example so each has an average of 0 and a typical spread of 1. Without it, features measured in big units dominate distance-based algorithms. *(Chapter 10)*
- **Federated learning** — Training a shared model across many devices or institutions by sending the model to the data and collecting only updates, so the raw data never leaves its owner. *(Chapter 11)*
- **Feedback loop** — When a model's predictions influence the future data it is trained on, for example predictive policing that sends more patrols to an area, which records more incidents there. *(Chapter 11)*
- **Feed-forward block (FFN)** — The per-token neural network inside each Transformer block, usually two or three big matrices with an activation between them. In dense models it holds about two-thirds of the parameters. *(Chapter 17)*
- **Feed-forward network (FFN)** — The small multilayer perceptron inside every Transformer block. It is applied to each token's vector separately: expand to a larger size, apply a non-linear activation, shrink back. *(Chapter 15)*
- **Fermi estimate** — A quick, rough calculation that multiplies a few reasonable guesses to get an answer within about a factor of ten. It is used to sanity-check claims and plan before precise data exists. *(Chapter 2)*
- **Few-shot and zero-shot learning** — Doing a new task from just a few examples (few-shot) or none at all (zero-shot), usually by relying on a large pretrained model. When you show a chatbot three examples of the format you want and it follows the pattern, that is few-shot. *(Chapter 8)*
- **Few-shot prompting** — Putting a few worked examples in the prompt so the model continues the pattern. It was the main way to use base models before chat models existed. *(Chapter 19)*
- **Fiber-optic cable** — A cable of hair-thin glass strands that carry data as pulses of light. It carries far more data, over far longer distances, than copper. *(Chapter 4)*
- **File** — A named chunk of stored bytes, such as a document, a photo, a song or a program. *(Chapter 3)*
- **File format** — The rulebook for what a file's bytes mean and in what order they appear, such as JPEG for photos or MP3 for music. *(Chapter 3)*
- **Files API** — An API feature that lets you upload a file (such as a PDF or image) once and then refer to it by an ID in many later requests, instead of re-sending the whole file each time. *(Chapter 26)*
- **Filter (kernel)** — The small grid of learned weights used in a convolution, often 3 × 3. Each filter acts as a detector for one simple pattern, such as a vertical edge or a patch of a certain color. *(Chapter 14)*
- **Fine-grained experts** — Splitting an MoE layer into many small experts and activating more of them per token, keeping compute the same while allowing far more combinations. *(Chapter 17)*
- **Fine-tuning** — Further training an already-trained model on a smaller, specialized dataset, to adapt it to a task, a style or a domain. Chapter 30 shows how anyone can fine-tune open models. *(Chapter 8, Chapter 14, Chapter 20, Chapter 30)*
- **FinFET** — A 3D transistor in which the channel is a thin vertical "fin" of silicon and the gate wraps over it on three sides. Intel introduced it in 2011, and it powered essentially every AI chip through NVIDIA's Vera Rubin. *(Chapter 36)*
- **FlashAttention** — A way of computing exact attention in small tiles inside a GPU's fast on-chip memory, without ever storing the full score grid. It makes attention much faster and less memory-hungry with no change to the result. *(Chapter 15, Chapter 23)*
- **Floating point** — The standard way computers store decimal numbers: a sign, a scale (exponent) and a fixed number of significant digits (mantissa), like scientific notation in binary. It lets the same format hold both tiny and huge values. *(Chapter 5, Chapter 32)*
- **Floating-point format** — A way of storing a number in a fixed number of bits as a sign, an exponent (rough size) and a fraction (fine detail), so the same bits can represent both very large and very small values. FP32, BF16, FP8 and FP4 are formats with 32, 16, 8 and 4 bits. *(Chapter 13)*
- **Floating-point number** — The way computers store decimal numbers such as 3.14159 or 0.000002, using a fixed number of bits split between significant digits and a power of two, like scientific notation in binary. Fewer bits mean less precision (Chapter 5). *(Chapter 2, Chapter 18)*
- **FLOP (floating-point operation)** — One floating-point operation, a single addition or multiplication of decimal numbers. It is used to count the total amount of math a job takes, as in "this model was trained with 10^25 FLOP." *(Chapter 2, Chapter 5, Chapter 7, Chapter 12, Chapter 18, Chapter 24, Chapter 32, Chapter 37)*
- **FLOP/s (FLOPS)** — Floating-point operations per second, a measure of computing speed. A teraflop is 10^12 FLOP per second, a petaflop 10^15 and an exaflop 10^18. *(Chapter 2, Chapter 37)*
- **FLOPS (FLOP/s)** — Floating-point operations per second, a measure of speed. An H100 GPU can do about 10^15 FLOP/s (one petaFLOPS) in 16-bit math. *(Chapter 32)*
- **Flow matching** — A training method in which the model learns a velocity field, a rule giving the direction and speed to move from every point, that carries random noise to real data. Generation follows those directions from noise to a finished sample. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Form factor (SXM, OAM, PCIe card)** — The physical shape and connector of an accelerator module. SXM (NVIDIA) and OAM (an open standard) are flat, high-power modules that bolt onto a server board; PCIe cards plug into the standard slots found in ordinary computers. *(Chapter 34)*
- **Forward pass** — Running an input through a neural network from the first layer to the last to get an output. Every token your chatbot writes requires one forward pass. *(Chapter 5, Chapter 12)*
- **Forward process (noising)** — The fixed, unlearned procedure that turns a clean training image into pure noise by adding a little Gaussian noise at each of many steps. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Foundry** — A company that manufactures chips designed by other companies. TSMC is by far the largest; Samsung Foundry, Intel Foundry and China's SMIC are others. *(Chapter 36)*
- **FP16 (half precision)** — "Half precision": a 16-bit format with a little more detail than BF16 but a much smaller range (its largest value is 65,504), so it needs extra tricks to train safely. *(Chapter 5, Chapter 32)*
- **FP32** — "Single precision": 32 bits (4 bytes) per number, with about 7 significant decimal digits. It was the standard for training neural networks and is still used for the most sensitive numbers. *(Chapter 5, Chapter 32)*
- **FP4** — A 4-bit (half-byte) floating-point format that can represent only 16 distinct values: 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6 and their negatives. It works only with scaling tricks that share one scale factor across a block of numbers. *(Chapter 5, Chapter 32)*
- **FP8** — An 8-bit (1-byte) floating-point format, supported in hardware since NVIDIA's Hopper chips. Variants trade detail against range (E4M3 keeps more detail, E5M2 more range). *(Chapter 5, Chapter 32, Chapter 37)*
- **FP8 and FP4** — 8-bit and 4-bit number formats. Newer AI chips do math in them much faster than in 16-bit, at the cost of more rounding, which careful scaling tricks keep under control. *(Chapter 19)*
- **FPGA (Field-Programmable Gate Array)** — A chip whose circuitry can be rewired after manufacturing. It is slower than an ASIC but flexible, and is used for prototyping new designs and for niche, low-latency AI tasks. *(Chapter 32)*
- **Frame sampling** — Picking only some frames from a video to encode, such as one frame per second, because encoding every frame would use far too many tokens. Anything that happens entirely between sampled frames can be missed. *(Chapter 22)*
- **Framework (deep-learning framework)** — A software library that handles tensors on GPUs, automatic differentiation, ready-made layers, optimizers and data loading, so researchers can describe a model in a few lines instead of writing all the math by hand. *(Chapter 13, Chapter 29, Chapter 35)*
- **Free cooling** — Cooling a data center using cold outdoor air or water directly, with chillers switched off. It is not literally free, since fans and pumps still run, but it avoids the large electricity cost of refrigeration. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Frontend** — The part of an app that runs on your device and that you see and interact with: buttons, text, layout. On the web it is usually written in HTML, CSS and JavaScript. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Front end and back end** — The front end is the part of a system you see and touch (an app's screen). The back end is the behind-the-scenes servers that store data and do the heavy work. *(Chapter 3)*
- **Front-end-of-line (FEOL)** — The first part of chip manufacturing, which builds the transistors themselves in and on the silicon surface. *(Chapter 36)*
- **Frontier lab** — A company that trains frontier models, such as OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Meta and xAI, along with leading Chinese labs such as DeepSeek. *(Chapter 1)*
- **FrontierMath** — A benchmark from Epoch AI of unpublished, expert-written mathematics problems, ranging from hard to research-level, with answers that can be checked automatically. Its Erdős extension contains problems no one had solved before. *(Chapter 24)*
- **Frontier model** — One of the most capable AI models in existence at a given time, typically built by a handful of well-funded labs. *(Chapter 1, Chapter 7, Chapter 24)*
- **Full-duplex** — A conversation in which both sides can speak and listen simultaneously, as on a telephone, rather than strictly taking turns as on a walkie-talkie. Full-duplex voice models can say "mm-hm" while you talk and notice when you interrupt. *(Chapter 22)*
- **Full fine-tuning** — Fine-tuning that updates every weight in the model. It is the most flexible kind but needs far more memory and compute than adapter methods like LoRA. *(Chapter 30)*
- **Full stack** — Every layer of technology needed to deliver a product, from raw materials and hardware at the bottom to the app you touch at the top. For AI it runs from sand and silicon through chips, buildings, power, data and models to your screen. *(Chapter 44)*
- **Function (programming)** — A named, reusable block of code that takes inputs and returns an output, like tip(80, 15) returning 12.0. *(Chapter 3, Chapter 5, Chapter 28)*

## G

- **GAN (generative adversarial network)** — A 2014 method in which a generator network tries to create realistic fakes and a discriminator network tries to spot them, so each forces the other to improve (see Chapter 16). *(Chapter 7, Chapter 16)*
- **Gate-all-around (GAA) / nanosheet transistor** — A transistor in which the channel is a stack of thin horizontal silicon sheets and the gate completely surrounds each one. Samsung shipped it first at its 3 nm node in 2022; TSMC's N2 and Intel's 18A ("RibbonFET") use it too. *(Chapter 36)*
- **Gated attention** — Adding a learned sigmoid gate to the output of each attention head, so the model can turn a head's contribution down. A Qwen team paper on it won a NeurIPS 2025 Best Paper award for improving stability and removing attention sinks. *(Chapter 15, Chapter 17)*
- **Gated DeltaNet** — A linear-attention layer that combines the delta rule with a learned forget gate, letting its fixed-size memory both overwrite and gradually fade old information. Used in Qwen3-Next and Qwen3.5. *(Chapter 17)*
- **Gated model** — A model on Hugging Face that you can only download after signing in and accepting its license terms on the model page. *(Chapter 30)*
- **Gate (of a transistor)** — A learned valve in a neural network: a set of numbers between 0 and 1 (made by a sigmoid function) that is multiplied by a signal to decide how much of it passes through. 0 means "block all," 1 means "let all through." *(Chapter 14, Chapter 36)*
- **Gateway (model router)** — A middle layer between your app and model providers that sends each request to the right model, retries or switches to a fallback provider on failure, enforces budgets and records logs. *(Chapter 31)*
- **Gating weight** — The share each chosen expert gets in the final mix for a token, computed from the router's scores. With top-2 routing the two gating weights add up to 1. *(Chapter 17)*
- **Gaussian noise** — Random numbers drawn from a bell-shaped (normal) distribution centered on zero. Most values are small and a few are large. It is the "static" that diffusion adds and removes. *(Chapter 16)*
- **GDPR and CCPA** — The European Union's General Data Protection Regulation and the California Consumer Privacy Act, the best-known laws giving people rights over their personal data and placing duties on organizations that use it. *(Chapter 11)*
- **GDPval** — An OpenAI benchmark (2025) of realistic work products, such as reports, plans and spreadsheets, drawn from many occupations; expert professionals compare the model's work with work by experienced humans. Artificial Analysis runs a version called GDPval-AA that reports a rating. *(Chapter 24)*
- **GELU (Gaussian Error Linear Unit)** — A smooth, slightly curved version of ReLU that dips a little below zero for small negative inputs. It was used in GPT-2, BERT and many other Transformer models. *(Chapter 12)*
- **General availability (GA)** — The point at which a model is officially released to all paying customers with normal support, as opposed to a "preview" that may change or be limited to some users. *(Chapter 24)*
- **Generalization** — A model's ability to perform well on new examples it was not trained on. Generalization, not a perfect score on training data, is the real goal of learning. *(Chapter 6, Chapter 8, Chapter 13)*
- **Generative AI** — AI that produces new content, such as text, images, audio, video or code, rather than only classifying or scoring existing content. *(Chapter 1)*
- **Generative model** — A model that learns what a kind of data looks like well enough to produce brand-new examples of it, such as images, text, audio or video. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Generative UI** — User interfaces that an AI creates on the fly for the task at hand, such as a form, chart, table or small interactive app, instead of replying only in text. *(Chapter 31)*
- **Generator** — In a GAN, the network that turns random input numbers into a synthetic example, such as an image. *(Chapter 16)*
- **GGUF** — A single-file format for (usually quantized) models, created in 2023 by the llama.cpp project and used by llama.cpp, Ollama, LM Studio and many other local tools. One GGUF file holds the weights, the tokenizer and settings. *(Chapter 30)*
- **Gigawatt (GW)** — A billion watts of power. AI companies now describe the size of their biggest data center campuses in gigawatts, comparable to the output of a large power plant. *(Chapter 1, Chapter 7)*
- **Gini impurity** — A score from 0 to 0.5 (for two classes) of how mixed a group is: 1 minus the sum of the squared shares of each class. An all-one-class group scores 0; a 50/50 mix scores 0.5. *(Chapter 10)*
- **Git** — The free, open-source version control program used by almost all software projects. It runs on your own computer and keeps the complete history of a project in a hidden folder. *(Chapter 29)*
- **GitHub** — A website for storing Git repositories online and collaborating on them through issues, reviews and pull requests. Most open-source AI code lives there. *(Chapter 3, Chapter 29)*
- **Global batch size** — The total number of training examples (or tokens) processed in one optimizer step, summed across all GPUs. Llama 3 used batches of about 16 million tokens. *(Chapter 37)*
- **Golden dataset (golden set)** — A curated set of realistic test questions with known correct answers (and, for retrieval, the passages that contain them), used to measure a system and catch regressions after every change. *(Chapter 27, Chapter 31)*
- **Gold standard** — Trusted, correct labels used to check the accuracy of models or of human labelers, for example test questions with known answers mixed into a labeler's work. *(Chapter 11)*
- **Goodhart's law** — "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Named after the economist Charles Goodhart, who made the point about monetary policy in 1975; this popular phrasing comes from the anthropologist Marilyn Strathern. *(Chapter 9, Chapter 20, Chapter 24)*
- **Goodput** — The share of a cluster's total time that produces useful training progress, after subtracting time lost to failures, restarts and waiting. *(Chapter 19, Chapter 23, Chapter 37)*
- **Google Colab** — A free Google website (colab.research.google.com) where you can write and run Python code in your browser, with nothing to install. It is a notebook: a page that mixes runnable code, its results, charts and written notes. *(Chapter 8, Chapter 26)*
- **GPQA Diamond** — The hardest subset of a 2023 benchmark of graduate-level science multiple-choice questions written so that skilled non-experts cannot answer them even with web search ("Google-proof"). It is near-saturated in late 2026. *(Chapter 24)*
- **GPU-as-a-service** — Renting GPUs by the hour (on-demand) or under multi-month or multi-year contracts (reserved), from a hyperscaler or neocloud, instead of buying them. *(Chapter 38)*
- **GPU (graphics processing unit)** — A chip with thousands of small processing cores that do the same math on lots of data at once. Built originally for video-game graphics, GPUs are now the main engine of AI. *(Chapter 1, Chapter 2, Chapter 7, Chapter 12, Chapter 14, Chapter 32)*
- **GPU-hour** — One GPU running for one hour. Training jobs and cloud rental prices are measured in GPU-hours. *(Chapter 19, Chapter 24, Chapter 37, Chapter 44)*
- **GPU library** — A collection of pre-written, heavily tuned kernels for common operations. NVIDIA's cuBLAS handles matrix math and cuDNN handles neural-network layers, so most programmers never write those kernels themselves. *(Chapter 35)*
- **GQA (grouped-query attention)** — An attention design in which several query heads share one set of keys and values. Llama 3 70B has 64 query heads sharing 8 key-value heads, which shrinks its KV cache eightfold compared with giving every head its own. *(Chapter 23)*
- **Grader** — The part of an RL environment that decides the reward at the end of an attempt, using tests, checks on the final state, a rubric judge or a mix. *(Chapter 21, Chapter 30)*
- **Gradient** — The list of partial derivatives for every knob of a function. It points in the direction that increases the output fastest, so stepping the opposite way decreases it fastest. *(Chapter 6, Chapter 8, Chapter 12, Chapter 33, Chapter 37)*
- **Gradient boosting** — Boosting in which each new tree is fitted to the gradient of the loss (for squared error, simply the residuals), so the ensemble walks downhill on its errors one small tree at a time. *(Chapter 10)*
- **Gradient checking** — Verifying computed slopes by nudging one parameter by a tiny amount, rerunning the forward pass, and comparing the change in loss with the slope that backpropagation produced. *(Chapter 12)*
- **Gradient checkpointing** — A memory-saving training trick that throws away some intermediate results during the forward pass and recomputes them when needed, trading extra compute (roughly a third more) for much less memory. *(Chapter 30)*
- **Gradient clipping** — Capping the gradient norm at a chosen limit by scaling the whole gradient down when it is too large, which prevents one bad step from wrecking training. *(Chapter 13)*
- **Gradient descent** — An optimization method that repeatedly moves every parameter a small step in the direction that reduces the loss, using the gradient. It is how virtually every neural network is trained. *(Chapter 6, Chapter 8, Chapter 12, Chapter 19)*
- **Gradient norm** — A single number for the overall size of a gradient, computed as the square root of the sum of the squares of all its slopes. A sudden jump in it is an early warning of instability. *(Chapter 13)*
- **Graph** — A set of things (nodes) joined by connections (edges). A molecule is a graph with atoms as nodes and bonds as edges; a road map has intersections and roads. *(Chapter 17)*
- **Graph convolutional network (GCN)** — A 2016 GNN design in which each node's update is a learned, weighted average of its neighbors' features, a graph version of the sliding filters in a CNN. *(Chapter 17)*
- **Graph neural network (GNN)** — A neural network that works on graph-shaped data by repeatedly updating each node from its neighbors. *(Chapter 17)*
- **GraphRAG** — A retrieval approach, popularized by Microsoft Research in 2024, that uses a language model to build a knowledge graph from documents, groups the graph into communities of related entities, summarizes each community, and answers broad questions from those summaries. *(Chapter 27)*
- **Gray space** — The rooms that hold support equipment: electrical rooms with switchgear and UPS systems, battery rooms, mechanical rooms with pumps and heat exchangers, and fire-suppression gear. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Greedy decoding** — Always choosing the single most probable next token. It is predictable but tends to produce bland, repetitive text. *(Chapter 6, Chapter 18)*
- **Grid search** — Trying every combination from a list of values for each hyperparameter, such as every pairing of 3 tree depths with 3 learning rates. *(Chapter 9)*
- **Grokking** — A training phenomenon in which a network first memorizes its training data and then, much later, suddenly generalizes, as if it had finally understood the rule. *(Chapter 13)*
- **Gross margin** — The share of revenue left after the direct costs of delivering a product, which for AI products are mostly model and hosting costs. A $20 subscription with $3 of model costs has a gross margin of (20 − 3) ÷ 20 = 85%. *(Chapter 31, Chapter 35)*
- **Groundedness check** — An automatic test of whether each claim in an answer is supported by the source documents the system retrieved, used to catch hallucinations in retrieval-based apps. *(Chapter 31)*
- **Grounding** — Tying a model's answer to specific provided sources, such as search results or documents, rather than to its memory. *(Chapter 18, Chapter 25, Chapter 27)*
- **Grouped-query attention (GQA)** — An attention design in which many query heads share a smaller number of key-value heads. It shrinks the KV cache by the sharing factor with little loss in quality. *(Chapter 33)*
- **GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization)** — A reinforcement-learning method that samples a group of answers to the same prompt, scores each one, and rewards each answer by how much better or worse it did than the group's average. It needs no separate value model. *(Chapter 21)*
- **GRU (Gated Recurrent Unit)** — A simplified LSTM, introduced in 2014, with two gates (update and reset) and no separate cell state. It is cheaper to run and often performs about as well. *(Chapter 14)*
- **GSPO (Group Sequence Policy Optimization)** — A 2025 variant of GRPO from the Qwen team that applies importance ratios and clipping to whole answers instead of individual tokens, improving stability, especially for mixture-of-experts models. *(Chapter 21)*
- **Guardrails** — Checks and limits placed around an AI model, on what goes in, what comes out and what actions it can take, that keep the system on task, safe and compliant. *(Chapter 31)*
- **Guidance scale** — The number (often between about 3 and 12 in image tools) that sets how hard classifier-free guidance pushes toward the prompt. Low values give loose, varied images; high values give literal but often oversaturated ones. *(Chapter 16)*

## H

- **H100** — NVIDIA's flagship data-center GPU of 2022–2024, with 80 GB of memory and about 989 trillion 16-bit operations per second at peak. It is the workhorse behind many 2023–2025 training runs. *(Chapter 19)*
- **H100-equivalent (H100e)** — A unit, used by analysts such as Epoch AI, that expresses a cluster's computing power as the number of NVIDIA H100 GPUs that would match it, so clusters built from different chips can be compared. *(Chapter 34, Chapter 37)*
- **Hallucination** — A fluent, confident model output that is false or not supported by any source, such as an invented quotation, citation or fact. *(Chapter 18, Chapter 25, Chapter 27)*
- **Handoff** — Passing a conversation or task from one agent to another, more specialized agent, for example from a general support agent to a billing agent. *(Chapter 28)*
- **Hard constraint** — A rule in a model spec or constitution that no operator or user instruction can override, typically covering catastrophic harms such as weapons of mass destruction. *(Chapter 20)*
- **Harness (scaffold)** — The software wrapped around a model during a task (its prompts, tools, memory handling and retry rules). Changing the harness can change results dramatically, as ARC-AGI-3 showed. *(Chapter 21, Chapter 24, Chapter 28, Chapter 29)*
- **HBM3E / HBM4 / HBM4E** — Successive generations of High Bandwidth Memory. HBM3E (about 1–1.2 TB/s per stack) powers most 2024–25 accelerators; HBM4 doubles the interface to 2,048 bits and powers 2026 chips such as NVIDIA Rubin and AMD MI455X; HBM4E is the planned 2027 follow-on. *(Chapter 33)*
- **HBM (high-bandwidth memory)** — Memory chips stacked vertically and placed right beside an AI processor, connected by thousands of wires, so the processor can be fed data at terabytes per second. It is the critical, and often scarce, memory of AI chips. *(Chapter 2, Chapter 23, Chapter 33, Chapter 34, Chapter 36, Chapter 44)*
- **Header** — A labeled line of extra information sent with a request, such as your API key or the format of the data. Headers are like the writing on the outside of an envelope. *(Chapter 26)*
- **Heat** — Energy that flows from a hotter thing to a colder thing because of their difference in temperature. Data center engineers measure the rate of heat flow in the same units as electrical power: watts, kilowatts and megawatts. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Heat exchanger** — A device that moves heat from one fluid to another without letting them mix, usually through thin metal plates or tubes. A car radiator is a heat exchanger between engine coolant and outside air. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Heat flux** — The amount of heat flowing through a given area, usually in watts per square centimeter. AI chips have very high heat flux because so much power is concentrated on a small piece of silicon. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Heat pump** — A machine that uses electricity to move heat from a cooler place to a warmer one, the same cycle as a chiller run "the other way". It can boost lukewarm data center water to temperatures hot enough for heating homes. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Heat rejection** — The final step of cooling: getting the heat out of the building and into the outdoor air (or, occasionally, a river, lake or sea). *(Chapter 40)*
- **He initialization** — A 2015 variant of Xavier initialization with slightly larger weights, designed for ReLU networks, in which half the signals are zeroed out at each layer. *(Chapter 13)*
- **Held-out set** — Test questions kept private and never published, so they cannot leak into training data. Scores on a held-out set are more trustworthy than scores on a public one. *(Chapter 24)*
- **Held-out test set** — Examples set aside before any training or prompt tuning and used only for the final, honest measurement of quality. If you peek at it while tuning, it stops being honest. *(Chapter 30)*
- **Hertz (Hz)** — One cycle per second. A gigahertz (GHz) is a billion cycles per second, so a 3 GHz processor's clock ticks 3 billion times every second. *(Chapter 2)*
- **Heuristic filter** — A hand-written rule that keeps or drops a document based on simple measurable properties, such as word count, share of symbols or amount of repetition. *(Chapter 19)*
- **HFU (hardware FLOPs utilization)** — Like MFU, but counting all FLOPs the hardware actually performs, including extra work such as recomputing activations. HFU is always at least as high as MFU, so MFU is the more honest number. *(Chapter 37)*
- **HGX and DGX** — NVIDIA's 8-GPU baseboard design, which server makers build into their own machines (HGX), and NVIDIA's own branded complete AI servers and systems (DGX). *(Chapter 34)*
- **Hidden layer** — Any layer between the input and the output. Its outputs are internal working values that the network invents during training. *(Chapter 12)*
- **Hidden size** — The number of numbers in each token's vector as it travels through a language model, such as 4,096. It sets the width of most of the model's matrices. *(Chapter 5)*
- **Hidden state** — The memory vector an RNN carries from one step to the next. It is a running summary of everything the network has read so far. *(Chapter 14)*
- **Hierarchical clustering** — Building a tree of nested clusters by repeatedly merging the closest pair of groups, so you can cut the tree at whatever level of detail you like. *(Chapter 10)*
- **High-NA EUV** — The newest generation of EUV lithography, with a numerical aperture of 0.55 instead of 0.33. It prints features about 1.7 times smaller in one exposure, at a much higher tool price. *(Chapter 36)*
- **HIP** — AMD's programming interface, which closely mirrors CUDA so that CUDA code can be translated to run on AMD GPUs with relatively few changes. *(Chapter 35)*
- **Historical bias** — When data accurately records a past that was unfair, so a model trained on it learns to repeat the unfairness. *(Chapter 11)*
- **HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small World)** — An approximate nearest-neighbor index that links each vector to a few of its close neighbors, organized in layers from sparse to dense. A search starts in the sparse top layer, hops greedily toward the query, then drops to denser layers to refine. It is the most common index inside vector databases. *(Chapter 27)*
- **Hosting** — Renting space on servers (usually in the cloud) so that your app is reachable on the internet at a web address, day and night. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Hot aisle and cold aisle** — A layout in which rows of racks face each other so that their fronts share an aisle of cool supply air (the cold aisle) and their backs share an aisle of hot exhaust air (the hot aisle). *(Chapter 38)*
- **Hot aisle / cold aisle** — A layout in which rows of racks face each other in pairs, so cold air is supplied to the shared aisle between their fronts (cold aisle) and hot exhaust collects in the shared aisle between their backs (hot aisle). *(Chapter 40)*
- **HTTP (Hypertext Transfer Protocol)** — The protocol browsers and apps use to request things from servers and receive responses. Nearly every website and AI API speaks it. *(Chapter 4, Chapter 26)*
- **HTTP method** — The verb of a request. GET means "give me something" (like loading a page); POST means "here is some data, please process it" (like submitting a form). Sending a prompt to a model is a POST. *(Chapter 26)*
- **HTTPS** — HTTP with encryption (the S stands for secure). The padlock icon in your browser means the connection uses it. *(Chapter 4)*
- **Hugging Face** — The main online hub for sharing open models and datasets, which also maintains popular libraries such as Transformers and Diffusers. *(Chapter 13)*
- **Hugging Face Hub** — The main website for sharing open AI models, datasets and demos, at huggingface.co. In October 2026 its counter showed about 3.17 million models. *(Chapter 30)*
- **Hugging Face TRL and PEFT** — Two free Python libraries from Hugging Face. PEFT ("parameter-efficient fine-tuning") adds adapters such as LoRA to a model; TRL ("transformer reinforcement learning") provides ready-made trainers for SFT, DPO and RL-style fine-tuning. *(Chapter 30)*
- **Human-in-the-loop (HITL)** — A design in which a person must review or approve certain steps before an agent continues, typically anything irreversible such as sending a message, spending money or deleting data. *(Chapter 28, Chapter 31)*
- **Humanity's Last Exam (HLE)** — A public benchmark of 2,500 expert-level questions across many fields, released in January 2025 by the Center for AI Safety and Scale AI. Questions were crowdsourced from experts and kept only if frontier models of the time failed them. *(Chapter 24)*
- **Hybrid architecture** — A model that mixes layer types, typically mostly linear-attention or state-space layers plus some full-attention layers, to get long-context efficiency while keeping exact recall. *(Chapter 17)*
- **Hybrid bonding** — Joining two stacked dies by fusing their copper pads and surrounding insulator directly, without solder bumps. It allows extremely dense vertical connections and is the basis of true 3D chip stacking. *(Chapter 36)*
- **Hybrid reasoning model** — A single model that can either answer immediately or think at length first, depending on a setting or its own judgment, instead of separate fast and reasoning models. *(Chapter 21)*
- **Hybrid search** — Running keyword search and semantic search on the same query and combining their results into one ranked list, to get the strengths of both. *(Chapter 27)*
- **HyDE (Hypothetical Document Embeddings)** — A retrieval trick in which the model first drafts a hypothetical answer to the question, and the system searches for real passages similar to that draft rather than to the question itself. *(Chapter 27)*
- **Hype cycle** — The recurring pattern in which excitement about a technology rises faster than its real capabilities, crashes when reality disappoints, and then recovers more slowly as genuinely useful applications mature. *(Chapter 7)*
- **Hyper-connections** — A generalization of residual connections that carries several parallel copies of the residual stream and learns how to mix them between layers. *(Chapter 17)*
- **Hyper-connections and mHC** — 2024–25 generalizations of residual connections that keep several parallel streams and learn how to mix them. DeepSeek's mHC (manifold-constrained hyper-connections, December 2025) restricts the mixing to keep training stable and is used in DeepSeek V4. *(Chapter 13)*
- **Hyperparameter** — A setting chosen by a person before training, such as the learning rate, the number of passes through the data, or the maximum depth of a decision tree. It controls how learning happens but is not itself learned from the data. *(Chapter 8, Chapter 12)*
- **Hyperparameter tuning** — Systematically trying different hyperparameter settings and keeping the one that scores best on validation data (or in cross-validation), never on the test set. *(Chapter 9)*
- **Hyperscaler** — A giant cloud provider operating huge data centers worldwide, such as Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure or Google Cloud. *(Chapter 4, Chapter 35, Chapter 38)*

## I

- **ICI (Inter-Chip Interconnect)** — Google's direct chip-to-chip links between TPUs, wired so that thousands of TPUs form one tightly connected pod without central switches. *(Chapter 34)*
- **IDE (integrated development environment)** — The app programmers write code in, combining a text editor, file browser, terminal and debugging tools. VS Code, JetBrains IDEs and Cursor are examples. *(Chapter 29)*
- **IDF (Inverse Document Frequency)** — A weight that makes rare words count more than common ones in keyword search. It is usually computed as the logarithm of (total documents ÷ documents containing the word). Words that appear everywhere, like "the", get a weight near zero. *(Chapter 27)*
- **IDM (integrated device manufacturer)** — A company that both designs and manufactures its own chips. Intel, Samsung and the memory makers SK hynix and Micron are IDMs. *(Chapter 36)*
- **ImageNet** — A large dataset of about 14 million labeled photos released in 2009. Its yearly competition (2010 to 2017), using 1,000 categories, became the main scoreboard for computer vision and the stage where deep learning broke through. *(Chapter 14)*
- **Image segmentation** — Labeling every pixel of an image with the object or region it belongs to, for example coloring each pixel of a scan as "tumor" or "healthy tissue." *(Chapter 14)*
- **Image-to-image (img2img)** — Generating a new image by partly noising an existing one and then denoising it under a new prompt. The strength setting controls how much of the original survives. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Image tokenizer (VQ-VAE)** — A network that converts an image into a grid of discrete tokens by matching each small region to the nearest entry in a learned codebook (a fixed list of visual patterns), and that can turn the tokens back into an image. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Immersion cooling** — Submerging entire servers in a tank of dielectric fluid, which absorbs heat from every component at once. In single-phase immersion the fluid stays liquid and is pumped to a heat exchanger; in two-phase immersion it boils and condenses on a cold coil above the tank. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Immersion lithography** — DUV lithography with a thin film of ultrapure water between the lens and the wafer, which raises the numerical aperture and lets the tool print finer features. *(Chapter 36)*
- **Importance ratio** — In PPO-style training, the ratio between how likely a token or answer is under the updated model and how likely it was under the model that generated it. Clipping limits how far this ratio can move in one update. *(Chapter 21)*
- **Incognito or temporary chat** — A chat mode that is not saved to your history and does not read from or write to memory. Useful for sensitive topics or for testing how the model behaves without your personal context. *(Chapter 25)*
- **In-context learning** — A model picking up a new task from examples supplied in its input at prediction time, with no change to its parameters, as when you show a chatbot three examples and it follows the pattern. *(Chapter 10, Chapter 18)*
- **Independence** — Two events are independent if knowing that one happened tells you nothing about the other, like two separate coin flips. Only for independent events can you multiply probabilities directly. *(Chapter 6)*
- **Indexing pipeline** — The steps that prepare documents for search ahead of time: load, parse, clean, chunk, optionally add context, embed and store. It runs once per document and again whenever a document changes. *(Chapter 27)*
- **Indirect prompt injection** — An attack in which instructions are hidden inside content an AI system reads, such as a web page, email, PDF or retrieved document, so that the model follows the attacker's text instead of its real instructions. *(Chapter 27, Chapter 28)*
- **Indirect water** — Water used elsewhere because of the data center, mainly by the power plants that generate its electricity (and, further back, by chip factories and other suppliers). *(Chapter 40)*
- **Induction head** — An attention head that finds an earlier occurrence of the current token and copies forward what came right after it. It is one of the best-understood mechanisms behind in-context learning. *(Chapter 15)*
- **Inductive bias** — The built-in assumption an algorithm makes about what kind of pattern it is looking for, such as "nearby examples share a label" or "the answer is a straight-line combination of the inputs." No algorithm can learn anything without one. *(Chapter 10, Chapter 13, Chapter 14, Chapter 17)*
- **Inference** — Using an already-trained model to produce an output. Every reply you get from a chatbot is inference; the model's parameters do not change while you chat with it. *(Chapter 1, Chapter 4, Chapter 5, Chapter 8, Chapter 12, Chapter 18, Chapter 23, Chapter 25)*
- **Inference-aware scaling** — Choosing model size and training data to minimize the total cost of training plus all expected future use, rather than training cost alone. *(Chapter 19)*
- **Inference engine** — The program that actually loads a model's weights and runs the calculations to generate tokens, optimized for particular hardware. Apps such as Ollama and LM Studio are built on top of engines. *(Chapter 30)*
- **Inference fleet** — All the accelerators a provider dedicates to serving a model to users, often thousands of racks spread over several data centers, each running its own copy (replica) of the model. *(Chapter 44)*
- **InfiniBand** — A networking technology designed for supercomputers, with very low latency and built-in guarantees that data is not dropped. NVIDIA owns the leading supplier (it acquired Mellanox in 2020) and sells it as Quantum switches, such as the 800 Gb/s Quantum-X800. *(Chapter 34)*
- **Ingot** — A large cylinder of single-crystal silicon grown from molten silicon around a seed crystal, usually by the Czochralski method. Wafers are sliced from it like bread from a loaf. *(Chapter 36)*
- **Initialization** — Choosing the starting values of all weights before training. They must be random (to break symmetry) and correctly scaled (so signals neither fade nor explode through the layers). *(Chapter 13)*
- **In-memory computing** — Doing arithmetic inside or right next to the memory cells that store the numbers, so weights barely move. Digital versions (like d-Matrix's) use ordinary logic; analog versions use electrical currents and are noisier. *(Chapter 35)*
- **Inpainting and outpainting** — Regenerating only a selected (masked) region of an image so it blends with the untouched rest, or extending an image beyond its borders with new content that continues the scene. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Input layer** — The entry point of a network, holding the raw numbers being fed in, such as pixel values or token embeddings. It has no weights of its own. *(Chapter 12)*
- **Input tokens and output tokens** — Input tokens are everything you send (system prompt, history, documents, question); output tokens are everything the model writes. Output tokens usually cost four to six times more per token, because generating text one token at a time is the slow, expensive part of inference (Chapter 23). *(Chapter 26)*
- **In-row cooler** — A cooling unit the size of a narrow rack, placed between racks in a row, that pulls hot air from the hot aisle and pushes cooled air into the cold aisle a short distance away. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Instruction** — One basic command a processor understands, such as "load a number from memory," "add these two registers" or "jump to another instruction if this number is zero." *(Chapter 2)*
- **Instruction-based editing** — Changing an existing image by describing the change in words ("remove the car," "make it winter"), with the model keeping everything else as consistent as possible. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Instruction file (AGENTS.md or CLAUDE.md)** — A plain-text file at the top of a project that coding agents read automatically before working: how to run the tests, coding conventions, what never to touch. It is a README written for agents. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Instruction hierarchy** — Training a model to give instructions different authority depending on their source, typically system above developer above user above tool output, so that text from a lower level cannot override a higher one. *(Chapter 18)*
- **Instruction set architecture (ISA)** — The vocabulary of instructions a family of processors understands, and how they are encoded. x86, Arm and RISC-V are the major ISAs; a program compiled for one will not run directly on another. *(Chapter 2, Chapter 32)*
- **Instruction tuning** — SFT on a large, varied collection of instructions ("summarize this", "write a poem about", "explain like I'm ten") paired with good responses, so the model learns to follow requests in general rather than one task. *(Chapter 20)*
- **Instruct model** — A version of a model that has been post-trained to follow instructions and chat (Chapter 20), as opposed to the raw base model, which only continues text. For chatting you almost always want the instruct version; for fine-tuning a new format, either can work. *(Chapter 30)*
- **INT8 and INT4** — Formats that store whole numbers in 8 or 4 bits, multiplied by a shared scale factor to represent decimals. They are mainly used to shrink trained models for serving. *(Chapter 5)*
- **INT8 / INT4** — 8-bit and 4-bit whole-number formats (256 and 16 possible values). They are used mainly to run already-trained models cheaply, after conversion. *(Chapter 32)*
- **Integrated circuit (chip)** — Many transistors and the wires connecting them, manufactured together on one small piece of silicon. "Chip," "microchip" and "integrated circuit" mean the same thing. *(Chapter 2)*
- **Integrated circuit (IC) / chip** — Many transistors and the wires between them, manufactured together on a single piece of silicon. "Chip" and "IC" mean the same thing. *(Chapter 36)*
- **Interactivity (tokens per second per user)** — How fast the answer streams for one person. Raising the batch size raises throughput but lowers interactivity, so providers must choose a point on the trade-off. *(Chapter 37)*
- **Inter-annotator agreement** — How often different labelers give the same label to the same item. Low agreement signals an ambiguous task or unclear instructions. *(Chapter 11)*
- **Intercept** — The point where a line crosses the vertical axis, meaning the output when the input is zero. In y = w × x + b, the intercept is b. *(Chapter 5)*
- **Interleaved input** — A single sequence that mixes text and other modalities in their natural order, such as a web page with paragraphs and photos in between. Early-fusion models are pretrained on interleaved data like this. *(Chapter 22)*
- **Internet** — The worldwide network of networks, linked by shared rules so that any connected device can reach any other. *(Chapter 4)*
- **Internet exchange point (IXP)** — A building where many networks meet and hand traffic directly to each other, so data does not have to take long detours. *(Chapter 4, Chapter 38)*
- **Interposer** — A thin slab of silicon (or organic material with small silicon bridges) with very fine wiring, on which the GPU dies and HBM stacks sit side by side. It carries thousands of connections between them over a few millimeters. *(Chapter 33, Chapter 34, Chapter 36)*
- **Interpretability** — How easily a person can understand why a model made a particular prediction. A regression with five readable weights is highly interpretable; a forest of 500 trees much less so. *(Chapter 10, Chapter 24)*
- **Interpreter** — A program that reads code and carries it out step by step as it goes, without producing a separate machine-code file first. *(Chapter 3)*
- **Inter-token latency (ITL)** — The time between successive output tokens once streaming has started, also called time per output token. Its inverse is output speed: 20 milliseconds per token is 50 tokens per second. *(Chapter 23)*
- **Inverted index** — A lookup table that lists, for every word, which documents contain it (and often where). Like the index at the back of a book, it lets a search engine jump straight to matching documents instead of reading everything. *(Chapter 27)*
- **IP address** — A number that identifies a device on the internet so packets can be delivered to it, such as 203.0.113.42. IP stands for Internet Protocol. *(Chapter 4)*
- **IPO (Identity Preference Optimization)** — A 2023 variant of DPO that adds a regularizing term so the model does not push probabilities to extremes when the same answer is always preferred, a failure mode of plain DPO. *(Chapter 20)*
- **Isolation forest** — An anomaly detector that builds random trees of random cuts and flags points that can be isolated with unusually few cuts. *(Chapter 10)*
- **ISP (Internet service provider)** — The company that connects your home or phone to the internet, such as a cable, fiber or mobile phone company. *(Chapter 4)*
- **IT load** — The power used by the computing equipment itself (servers, storage and network gear), as opposed to cooling, lighting and power-conversion losses in the building. *(Chapter 40)*

## J

- **Jaccard similarity** — A measure of overlap between two sets: the number of items they share divided by the number of distinct items in either. Identical sets score 1; sets with nothing in common score 0. *(Chapter 19)*
- **Jagged frontier** — The uneven shape of AI ability: superhuman at some tasks while failing at nearby tasks that humans find easy. It is why "Is AI smarter than people?" has no single answer. *(Chapter 1, Chapter 24)*
- **Jailbreak** — A prompt crafted by a user to trick a model into ignoring its safety training, for example through role-play or elaborate hypotheticals. *(Chapter 18, Chapter 31)*
- **JAX** — Google's library for fast numerical computing with automatic differentiation and compilation, widely used at Google DeepMind and on Google's TPU chips. *(Chapter 13)*
- **JEDEC** — The industry standards organization that defines memory technologies such as DDR, LPDDR, GDDR and HBM, so that chips from different makers work together. *(Chapter 33)*
- **JEPA (joint-embedding predictive architecture)** — Yann LeCun's approach of training a model to predict missing or future parts of its input in embedding space (the meaning) rather than pixel by pixel. *(Chapter 16, Chapter 22)*
- **Jevons paradox** — The observation, first made about coal in the 1860s, that making a resource cheaper to use can increase total consumption, because people find many more uses for it. *(Chapter 23, Chapter 35, Chapter 37)*
- **Jitter** — A small random amount added to each backoff wait, so that thousands of clients that failed at the same moment do not all retry at exactly the same moment. *(Chapter 26)*
- **Joule** — The basic unit of energy, about the energy needed to lift an apple one meter. One watt is one joule per second, and 3,600 joules make one watt-hour. *(Chapter 44)*
- **JSON (JavaScript Object Notation)** — A plain-text format for structured data using labels and values, like {"name": "Ada", "age": 36}, which can nest lists and objects inside each other. APIs and AI tools use it constantly. *(Chapter 3, Chapter 26)*
- **JSONL (JSON Lines)** — A file format where every line is one complete JSON object (Chapter 26). Most fine-tuning services and tools expect training data in this format. *(Chapter 30)*
- **JSON-RPC** — A simple, standard format for one program to ask another to run a named method with parameters and get back a result or an error, written in JSON. MCP and A2A are built on version 2.0. *(Chapter 28)*
- **JSON Schema** — A standard way of describing what a JSON object must look like: which fields it has, what type each one is (text, number, true/false, list), which are required, and which values are allowed. *(Chapter 26, Chapter 28)*
- **Junction temperature** — The temperature at the hottest point inside a chip, where the transistors are. Chips are designed to stay below a limit, typically somewhere around 85–105 °C depending on the product. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Just-in-time retrieval** — Giving a model lightweight pointers (file names, a table of contents, search tools) and letting it load the full content only when a step actually needs it, instead of pasting everything in up front. *(Chapter 25)*

## K

- **Kernel (GPU kernel)** — A single, hand-optimized routine that runs on a GPU, such as one that computes attention. New layer types need new kernels before they run fast, which takes specialist engineering. *(Chapter 17, Chapter 23, Chapter 32, Chapter 35)*
- **Kernel trick** — A mathematical shortcut that lets an SVM behave as if it had added many extra features (even infinitely many) without computing them, so it can draw curved boundaries cheaply. *(Chapter 10)*
- **Key** — In attention, a vector each token computes with a second learned matrix, advertising what information the token contains. Queries are compared with keys to score relevance. *(Chapter 15)*
- **Key-value pair** — A label and its value, written "key": value in JSON, such as "model": "claude-opus-5-5". Keys are always text in quotes; values can be text, numbers, true/false, lists or more nested objects. *(Chapter 26)*
- **Keyword search** — Search that finds and ranks documents by the words they share with the query. It matches spelling, not meaning, so "car" does not match "automobile" unless someone adds a synonym list. *(Chapter 27)*
- **Kilobyte, megabyte, gigabyte, terabyte (KB, MB, GB, TB)** — About a thousand, a million, a billion and a trillion bytes. A page of text is a few KB, a phone photo a few MB, a phone's storage hundreds of GB, and a large hard drive several TB. *(Chapter 2)*
- **Kilowatt-hour (kWh)** — A unit of energy: the energy used by drawing one kilowatt for one hour. Electricity bills are measured in kWh; one kWh is 3.6 million joules. *(Chapter 2)*
- **Kimi Delta Attention (KDA)** — Moonshot AI's 2025 variant of Gated DeltaNet in which each channel of the memory has its own forget gate. Used in Kimi Linear and Kimi K3. *(Chapter 17)*
- **KL divergence (Kullback–Leibler divergence)** — A measure of how different two probability distributions are. It is zero when they are identical and grows as they diverge. For a list of options it is the sum of p × ln(p / q) over all options, where p is the new probability and q is the old one. *(Chapter 20)*
- **KL penalty** — A term in RL training that subtracts reward in proportion to how far the model has drifted from its reference model. It keeps the model from abandoning everything it learned before in pursuit of a high score. *(Chapter 20)*
- **k-means** — A clustering algorithm that repeatedly assigns each example to the nearest of k centers and moves each center to the average of its assigned examples, until the groups stop changing. *(Chapter 10)*
- **k-nearest neighbors (kNN)** — An algorithm that predicts by finding the k most similar labeled examples and taking a vote (for categories) or an average (for numbers). k is a number the user chooses, such as 3 or 5. *(Chapter 10)*
- **Knowledge cutoff** — The date after which a model has little or no training data. Events after it are unknown to the model unless provided in the context, for example by web search. *(Chapter 18, Chapter 24, Chapter 25, Chapter 27)*
- **Knowledge graph** — A way of storing information as entities (such as people, companies and products) connected by labeled relationships (such as "supplies", "works for" or "complained about"). Wikidata is a famous public example. *(Chapter 27)*
- **KTO (Kahneman–Tversky Optimization)** — A 2024 method that learns from single responses labeled simply "good" or "bad" (like thumbs-up and thumbs-down) instead of pairs. It borrows ideas from prospect theory, which says people feel losses more than equal gains. *(Chapter 20)*
- **KV cache** — Stored intermediate results for every token the model has already read or written, so it does not redo that work for each new token. It grows with the length of the conversation. *(Chapter 4, Chapter 15, Chapter 17, Chapter 18, Chapter 23, Chapter 30, Chapter 33, Chapter 37, Chapter 44)*
- **KV cache offloading** — Moving parts of the KV cache out of scarce HBM into larger, slower memory such as the CPU's DRAM or local SSDs, and bringing them back when needed. It trades some speed for much larger capacity. *(Chapter 33)*

## L

- **L1 and L2 regularization** — Adding a penalty for large weights to the loss. L2 (also called ridge) adds the sum of squared weights and shrinks all weights toward zero; L1 (also called lasso) adds the sum of absolute weights and tends to push unhelpful weights to exactly zero, which removes those features. *(Chapter 9)*
- **Label noise** — Errors in a dataset's "correct" answers. On a benchmark, label noise caps the best achievable honest score below 100% and can punish a model for being right when the key is wrong. *(Chapter 24)*
- **Label (target)** — The "right answer" attached to a training example, such as "spam" on a spam email or "cat" on a photo of a cat. *(Chapter 1, Chapter 8, Chapter 12)*
- **Lag feature** — A past value of the series used as an input, such as "sales 7 days ago" or "sales on this date last year." *(Chapter 10)*
- **Lakehouse** — An architecture that adds warehouse-style tables, rules and fast queries on top of data-lake storage. *(Chapter 11)*
- **Lane** — One serial channel in a link. An 800 Gb/s port is usually 4 lanes of 200 Gb/s (or 8 lanes of 100 Gb/s); a 1.6 Tb/s port is 8 lanes of 200 Gb/s. *(Chapter 34)*
- **Language identification** — Automatically detecting which human language a piece of text is written in, usually with a small, fast classifier. *(Chapter 19)*
- **Language mixing** — A reasoning model switching between languages within its chain of thought, a side effect of rewarding only final answers. *(Chapter 21)*
- **Language model** — A model that assigns probabilities to sequences of text, usually by predicting the probability of each next token given all the previous ones. Chatbots are built on large language models. *(Chapter 6)*
- **Large language model (LLM)** — A very large neural network trained on huge amounts of text to predict the next token. It is the engine inside chatbots like ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini. *(Chapter 1, Chapter 7, Chapter 18, Chapter 25)*
- **Late fusion** — Building a multimodal model by attaching a separately trained encoder (for example, for images) to a language model through a small translation layer. *(Chapter 16, Chapter 22)*
- **Latency** — How long one request takes from start to finish, such as the time to fetch one piece of data or the delay before a chatbot's first word appears. *(Chapter 2, Chapter 4, Chapter 26, Chapter 31, Chapter 32)*
- **Latency budget** — The total delay a system can afford before users notice, divided among its steps. For conversation, the target is the gap between your last word and the assistant's first sound. *(Chapter 22)*
- **Latent diffusion** — Running the diffusion process inside a VAE's compressed latent space rather than on full-resolution pixels, then decoding the result. It cuts the work dramatically and is the key efficiency trick behind Stable Diffusion and most later generators. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Latent heat** — The energy a substance absorbs when it changes state, such as liquid to vapor, without changing temperature. Boiling or evaporating a liquid absorbs far more heat per kilogram than warming it a few degrees. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Latent reasoning** — Reasoning carried out inside a model's internal representations (for example by looping through layers repeatedly) rather than written out as tokens, which makes it efficient but much harder to read or monitor. *(Chapter 21)*
- **Latent space** — The compressed internal space where a model represents its data as short lists of numbers. Nearby points produce similar outputs, and moving smoothly through it changes the output smoothly. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Law of large numbers** — The principle that the average of many repeated random trials gets closer and closer to the expected value as the number of trials grows. *(Chapter 6)*
- **Layer** — One stage of a neural network that takes a vector in, multiplies it by a weight matrix, adds a bias, applies an activation function, and passes the result to the next stage. *(Chapter 5, Chapter 12)*
- **Layer normalization** — A 2016 normalization method that rescales all the features of a single example to mean 0 and spread 1, then applies a learned scale and shift. It became the standard in Transformers. *(Chapter 13)*
- **Leaderboard** — A public ranking of models by their benchmark scores. *(Chapter 9, Chapter 24)*
- **Leading question** — A question phrased so that it suggests the answer you want, such as "Isn't it true that...?" Leading questions invite sycophantic agreement. *(Chapter 25)*
- **Leaf-spine network (Clos network)** — A layered network design in which "leaf" switches connect to servers and every leaf connects to every "spine" switch above it, so that any two servers are only a few hops apart. Big clusters add a third layer on top. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Leaky ReLU** — A ReLU variant that lets a small fraction of negative inputs through (for example 0.01 × the input), so a neuron never goes completely silent. *(Chapter 12)*
- **Learning rate** — The size of each gradient-descent step, the number multiplied by the slope. Too small and training crawls; too big and it overshoots the valley and can blow up. *(Chapter 6, Chapter 8, Chapter 12, Chapter 19, Chapter 30)*
- **Learning-rate schedule** — A plan for changing the learning rate during training, typically a short ramp up followed by a long decline. *(Chapter 13, Chapter 19)*
- **Least privilege** — The security principle of giving each user, tool or agent only the minimum access it needs for its task, and nothing more. *(Chapter 28, Chapter 31)*
- **Lethal trifecta** — Simon Willison's name for the dangerous combination of an AI agent that has access to private data, is exposed to untrusted content, and can communicate externally. With all three, a hidden instruction can steal data; removing any one breaks the attack. *(Chapter 28, Chapter 31)*
- **Letter of intent** — A document stating that two parties plan to make a deal on certain terms, which is not yet a binding contract. Several headline AI compute "deals" began as letters of intent. *(Chapter 35)*
- **Library (package)** — Ready-made code that others wrote and shared so you can reuse it, such as NumPy or PyTorch. Also called a package. In Python you load one with the word import. *(Chapter 3, Chapter 26)*
- **License (software license)** — The legal terms saying what you may do with code, data or a model. Permissive licenses such as MIT and Apache 2.0 allow almost anything; "copyleft" licenses such as the GPL require you to share your changes under the same terms. *(Chapter 3, Chapter 24, Chapter 30)*
- **Linear and hybrid attention** — Linear attention (and its relatives, state-space models) replaces the growing KV cache with a fixed-size memory updated at each token. Hybrid models use mostly such layers plus a few full-attention layers for exact recall. *(Chapter 23)*
- **Linear attention** — A family of attention variants that fold all past keys and values into a fixed-size memory matrix, so each new token costs the same no matter how long the text is. *(Chapter 17)*
- **Linear function** — A function built only from multiplying inputs by fixed numbers and adding, so its graph is a straight line (or a flat plane in more dimensions). Each extra unit of input always changes the output by the same amount. *(Chapter 5, Chapter 12)*
- **Linear regression** — A model that predicts a number by fitting a straight line (or, with several features, a flat surface) through the data. It is the oldest and most widely used regression method. *(Chapter 8, Chapter 10)*
- **Linear transformation** — Any change to vectors that a matrix multiplication can produce, such as stretching, rotating, mixing or flattening. Every layer of a neural network starts with one, and training chooses which. *(Chapter 5)*
- **Line of best fit** — The straight line whose knob settings (slope and intercept) give the smallest mean squared error on a set of data points. Finding it is called linear regression. *(Chapter 5)*
- **Linux** — A free, open-source operating system that runs almost all servers, supercomputers and AI clusters, and whose kernel is the base of Android. *(Chapter 3)*
- **Lion, Shampoo and SOAP** — Alternative optimizers. Lion (2023) moves each parameter by a fixed amount in the direction of the sign of its momentum. Shampoo (2018) and SOAP (2024) use per-layer curvature information to shape their steps, at extra cost. *(Chapter 13)*
- **List** — An ordered collection of values in a program, written in square brackets, such as [72, 88, 95]. Items are numbered from 0. *(Chapter 3)*
- **Lithography scanner** — The machine that performs photolithography. It moves the mask and the wafer in perfect sync while a slit of light sweeps across, printing one chip-sized field at a time with nanometer accuracy. *(Chapter 36)*
- **llama.cpp** — A free, open-source engine written in C and C++ that runs language models efficiently on ordinary computers, using CPUs and many kinds of GPUs, with quantization from 1.5 to 8 bits. Started in 2023, it powers many local AI apps. *(Chapter 30)*
- **LLM-as-judge** — Using one language model to grade or compare the outputs of another, usually following a rubric. It is cheap and fast but inherits the judge model's own biases. *(Chapter 20, Chapter 21, Chapter 27, Chapter 31)*
- **LLMOps** — The practices and tools for running language-model applications in production: tracing, monitoring cost and latency, versioning prompts and models, managing providers, and feeding production data back into evals. *(Chapter 31)*
- **LMArena** — A public website where people chat with two anonymous models side by side and vote for the better answer; the votes are turned into Elo-style ratings. It measures what ordinary users prefer, not what is correct. *(Chapter 24)*
- **LM Studio** — A free desktop app for searching, downloading and chatting with local models through a graphical interface, with a local server mode. Its engines include llama.cpp and Apple's MLX. *(Chapter 30)*
- **Load balancer** — A traffic director that spreads incoming requests across many servers so no single one is overwhelmed, and routes around servers that have failed. *(Chapter 4, Chapter 44)*
- **Load balancing** — Techniques that keep tokens spread evenly across experts, so every expert learns and no GPU becomes a bottleneck. *(Chapter 17)*
- **Load bank** — A large bank of resistors that turns electricity into heat on purpose, used to simulate the IT load and test a data center's power and cooling before real computers are installed. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Localhost** — Your own computer acting as a server for itself. While building, programmers open addresses like http://localhost:3000 to see the app running privately on their machine. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Local minimum** — A point lower than everything immediately around it but not the lowest point overall, like a hollow on a mountainside. In fog, it can feel like the bottom. *(Chapter 6, Chapter 8)*
- **Local server (localhost API)** — A model served by an app on your own computer at an address such as http://localhost:11434 (Ollama's default) or http://localhost:1234 (LM Studio's default), so other programs on the machine can send it requests without anything leaving the computer. *(Chapter 30)*
- **Logarithm** — The inverse of an exponential: it answers "what power gives this number?" log10(1,000) = 3 because 10^3 = 1,000. Roughly, a base-10 log counts zeros and a base-2 log counts doublings. *(Chapter 6)*
- **Logic gate** — A tiny circuit, built from a few transistors, that takes one or more on/off inputs and produces an on/off output according to a fixed rule, such as AND, OR or NOT. All computer arithmetic and decision-making is built from gates. *(Chapter 2, Chapter 32)*
- **Logistic regression** — Despite its name, a classification method. It computes a weighted sum of the features, just like linear regression, and then squashes that score into a probability between 0 and 1 with an S-shaped curve. *(Chapter 8, Chapter 10)*
- **Logit** — A raw score a model gives to each possible output before it is turned into a probability. Higher logits mean the model favors that option more, but logits can be any number and need not add up to 1. *(Chapter 6, Chapter 12, Chapter 15, Chapter 18)*
- **Log-linear relationship** — A pattern in which a quantity grows by roughly the same amount each time another quantity is multiplied by a fixed factor. For reasoning, each tenfold increase in thinking tokens tends to add a similar number of accuracy points, until the gains level off. *(Chapter 21)*
- **Log-log plot** — A chart in which both axes use logarithmic scales, so each step along an axis multiplies the value by the same factor (for example 10). Power laws appear as straight lines on it. *(Chapter 19)*
- **Log-probability** — The logarithm of a probability. Because it turns multiplying into adding, models use log-probabilities to score long sequences without the numbers becoming too small to store; it is always zero or negative. *(Chapter 6)*
- **Log scale** — A chart axis where each equal step multiplies the value (1, 10, 100, 1,000) instead of adding to it. It lets one chart show both tiny and enormous numbers, and it turns exponential growth into a straight line. *(Chapter 6)*
- **Long-term memory (agents)** — Information an agent stores outside its context window, in files or databases, and retrieves when needed, so that it persists across sessions. *(Chapter 28)*
- **Loop** — An instruction to repeat steps, such as "for each photo in this folder, shrink it". Loops let a few lines of code process millions of items. *(Chapter 3)*
- **Looped (recursive) model** — A network that applies the same block of layers repeatedly to refine its answer, trading more computation per answer for fewer distinct parameters. *(Chapter 17)*
- **LoRA (low-rank adaptation)** — A cheap fine-tuning method that keeps a model's original weights frozen and trains small add-on matrices whose product represents the change. Widely used to teach image models new styles, characters and products. *(Chapter 16, Chapter 20, Chapter 30)*
- **Loss** — A single number measuring how wrong a model's predictions are on some data. Training tries to make it smaller. *(Chapter 19)*
- **Loss function** — A formula that turns a model's outputs and the correct answers into a single number measuring how wrong the model is. Training adjusts the parameters to make this number small. *(Chapter 6, Chapter 8, Chapter 12)*
- **Loss landscape** — An imaginary terrain where each location is a setting of all the model's knobs and the height is the loss there. Training is a walk downhill across this landscape. *(Chapter 6, Chapter 13)*
- **Lossless compression** — Compression that can be undone perfectly, bit for bit, such as ZIP files and PNG images. *(Chapter 3)*
- **Loss masking** — Ignoring some tokens when computing the training loss. In SFT, the system and user tokens are masked so that the model is graded only on how well it predicts the assistant's reply. *(Chapter 20)*
- **Loss spike** — A sudden jump in the training loss during an otherwise smooth run, usually a sign of numerical instability. At frontier scale each spike can waste large amounts of compute. *(Chapter 13, Chapter 17, Chapter 19, Chapter 37)*
- **Lossy compression** — Compression that throws away detail people are unlikely to notice in order to save much more space, such as JPEG photos, MP3 music and streaming video. *(Chapter 3)*
- **Lost in the middle** — The tendency of language models to use information at the beginning and end of a long context more reliably than information in the middle. *(Chapter 18, Chapter 25, Chapter 27)*
- **Low-rank matrix** — A matrix that can be written as the product of two much thinner matrices. Its "rank" is, roughly, how many independent column-times-row pieces it takes to build it. *(Chapter 30)*
- **LPDDR and GDDR** — Other kinds of DRAM. LPDDR is low-power memory used in phones, laptops and server CPUs like NVIDIA's Grace and Vera; GDDR is fast graphics memory used on gaming cards and some inference cards. *(Chapter 33)*
- **LPU (Language Processing Unit)** — Groq's inference chip, which keeps model weights in very fast on-chip memory (SRAM) instead of external memory and runs on a fixed schedule planned in advance by a compiler. It is very fast for one user but needs hundreds of chips to hold a large model. *(Chapter 32, Chapter 35)*
- **LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory)** — A type of RNN, introduced in 1997, that adds a protected memory lane (the cell state) and three gates that decide what to forget, what to store and what to output. It can remember information across far longer stretches than a plain RNN. *(Chapter 14)*

## M

- **Machine code** — Instructions written as the raw patterns of bits a processor executes directly. Every program, in any language, is eventually translated into machine code (Chapter 3 explains how). *(Chapter 2, Chapter 3)*
- **Machine learning (ML)** — Building programs that learn patterns from examples instead of following rules a programmer wrote by hand. The program adjusts its own internal numbers until its outputs match the examples well. *(Chapter 1, Chapter 7, Chapter 8)*
- **MAE (Mean Absolute Error)** — The average size of the misses, ignoring whether they were too high or too low. An MAE of 3 points means predictions were off by 3 points on average. *(Chapter 8)*
- **Magnitude** — The length of a vector: square each element, add them, and take the square root. The magnitude of [3, 4] is 5. Also called the norm. *(Chapter 5)*
- **Mamba** — A selective state-space model introduced in December 2023 whose state updates depend on the input, letting it choose what to remember. Successors are Mamba-2 (2024) and Mamba-3 (2026). *(Chapter 17)*
- **Manifold and quick disconnect** — A manifold is a set of pipes running up the back of a liquid-cooled rack that distributes coolant to each tray. Quick disconnects are dripless connectors that let a technician pull out a tray without draining the system. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Mantrap** — A small room with two interlocking doors, only one of which can open at a time, used at data center entrances so that only one authorized person passes through per badge and fingerprint check. *(Chapter 38)*
- **MAPE (Mean Absolute Percentage Error)** — The average miss as a percentage of the true value. Easy to explain to non-specialists, but it breaks down when true values are near zero. *(Chapter 9)*
- **Margin and support vectors** — The margin is the width of the empty street between the boundary and the nearest examples of each class. The support vectors are those nearest examples; they alone fix where the boundary goes. *(Chapter 10)*
- **Margin of error** — The range within which the true value probably lies, given the noise of measuring on a limited sample. A score of 90% plus or minus 1.5 points means the true accuracy is very likely between 88.5% and 91.5%. *(Chapter 9, Chapter 31)*
- **Markdown** — A simple text-formatting style (# for headings, * for bullets, ** for bold) that models read and write fluently. Chat apps display it as formatted text. *(Chapter 25)*
- **Masked diffusion** — The text version of diffusion, where "noise" means replacing tokens with a mask symbol. Generation repeatedly predicts the masked tokens and keeps the most confident ones. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Masked language modeling** — A training task in which some tokens (15 percent in BERT) are hidden and the model must predict them from the surrounding text on both sides. *(Chapter 15)*
- **Matrix** — A rectangular table of numbers arranged in rows and columns. In AI, a matrix can hold a dataset (one row per example) or a layer's weights (a machine that transforms vectors). *(Chapter 5, Chapter 12)*
- **Matrix factorization** — Splitting a big user-by-item ratings table into two smaller tables of hidden "taste" factors, one row per user and one per item, whose dot products fill in the missing ratings. *(Chapter 10)*
- **Matrix multiplication (matmul)** — Combining two matrices into a new one where each entry is the dot product of a row of the first with a column of the second. It is the single most common operation in AI and is often shortened to "matmul". *(Chapter 5, Chapter 12, Chapter 32)*
- **Matrix-vector multiplication** — Turning a vector into a new vector by taking the dot product of each row of a matrix with the vector. A matrix with 3 rows and 2 columns turns a 2-number vector into a 3-number vector. *(Chapter 5)*
- **Max output tokens (output limit)** — A separate cap on how many tokens the model may generate in one reply, which is much smaller than the full context window. *(Chapter 18)*
- **Max tokens** — The cap you set on how long the model's response may be. It is a ceiling, not a target. If the model hits it, the answer is cut off mid-thought, and on reasoning models the hidden thinking counts toward the cap too. *(Chapter 26)*
- **MCP Apps** — An MCP extension that lets a server show an interactive user interface (a form, a chart, a booking widget) inside the chat, instead of returning only text. *(Chapter 28)*
- **MCP host, client and server** — The host is the AI application the user works in (a chat app, a coding agent, an IDE). For each connection it creates an MCP client, a small connector component. Each client talks to one MCP server, the program that exposes a particular service's tools and data. *(Chapter 28)*
- **MCP (Model Context Protocol)** — An open standard introduced by Anthropic in November 2024 that lets AI applications plug into outside tools and data sources in a uniform way, like a USB port for AI. *(Chapter 7, Chapter 25, Chapter 28)*
- **MCP tools, resources and prompts** — The three things an MCP server can offer. Tools are actions the model can ask to run (search files, create an issue). Resources are data the app can read and show to the model (a file, a database schema, a calendar). Prompts are reusable prompt templates the user can pick (such as "summarize this pull request"). *(Chapter 28)*
- **Mean** — The ordinary average: add up all the values and divide by how many there are. It is sensitive to extreme values. *(Chapter 6)*
- **Mean squared error (MSE)** — The average of the squared errors over all examples. It is a single number summarizing how wrong a model is, where lower is better and zero is perfect. *(Chapter 5, Chapter 12)*
- **Median** — The middle value once all values are sorted (or the average of the two middle values if there is an even number of them). It is barely affected by extreme values. *(Chapter 6)*
- **Meet-me room (MMR)** — A secure room in a data center where the fiber cables of outside network carriers meet the building's own network and its tenants' equipment, and are connected together. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Megawatt (MW) and gigawatt (GW)** — A megawatt is a million watts, enough for very roughly 800 average US homes; a gigawatt is a thousand megawatts, about the output of one large nuclear reactor. Data centers are sized by how many of these they can draw (Chapter 39 explains watts properly). *(Chapter 38)*
- **Memorization** — When a model can reproduce exact pieces of its training data, such as a paragraph of a book or a person's phone number, raising privacy and copyright concerns. *(Chapter 11)*
- **Memory bandwidth** — How many bytes per second can move between a chip's memory and its calculators, like the width of a pipe. For AI chips it is measured in terabytes per second. *(Chapter 5, Chapter 23, Chapter 30, Chapter 33)*
- **Memory-bound** — A task whose speed is limited by how fast data can be fetched from memory, while the math units sit partly idle. Decode is usually memory-bound. *(Chapter 23, Chapter 33)*
- **Memory capacity** — How many bytes a memory can hold, such as 80 GB on an H100 or 288 GB on a Rubin GPU. It decides which models and how much conversation fit. *(Chapter 33)*
- **Memory fragmentation** — Wasted memory caused by reserving space that is never used, or by free space being scattered in gaps too small to use. In early LLM serving, fragmentation wasted a large share of KV-cache memory. *(Chapter 23)*
- **Memory hierarchy** — The ladder of storage in a computer, from tiny and instant (registers, cache) through main memory to huge and slow (SSDs, network storage). Each step down is bigger and cheaper per byte but slower, so good software keeps the data it needs near the top. *(Chapter 2, Chapter 33)*
- **Memory (in chat apps)** — Fast working space that holds the programs and data a computer is using right now. It is volatile: everything in it disappears when the power goes off. RAM stands for random-access memory. *(Chapter 2, Chapter 25)*
- **Memory latency** — How long it takes from asking for one piece of data until it arrives, measured in nanoseconds (billionths of a second). It matters most when you need one small item at a time. *(Chapter 33)*
- **Memory wall** — The growing gap between how fast processors can compute and how fast memory can supply them with data, which leaves chips waiting on memory. It is one of the main limits on AI speed. *(Chapter 2, Chapter 33)*
- **Merchant silicon** — Chips designed by one company and sold to anyone who wants to buy them, as NVIDIA and AMD sell GPUs. The opposite is in-house silicon that a company builds for its own use. *(Chapter 35)*
- **Merge** — Combining the changes from one branch into another, typically folding a finished feature branch back into main. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Merging an adapter** — Adding the adapter's change (B × A, scaled) into the base model's weights to produce a single standalone model, which is simpler to deploy but no longer swappable. *(Chapter 30)*
- **Message passing** — The core GNN step: each node gathers information from the nodes it is connected to, combines it with its own, and updates its representation. *(Chapter 17)*
- **Metadata** — Data about data, such as a photo's date, location and camera model, or a document's author and creation time. *(Chapter 3, Chapter 27)*
- **Metadata filtering** — Restricting a search to passages whose labels match certain conditions, such as "only HR documents", "only policies dated 2026" or "only documents this user may read", either before or alongside the vector search. *(Chapter 27)*
- **Meta-prompting** — Asking a model to write, critique or improve a prompt, then testing the result. Many developer consoles include a "prompt generator" or "prompt improver" built on this idea. *(Chapter 25)*
- **Metric prefix** — A word added in front of a unit to multiply it by a power of ten: kilo (thousand), mega (million), giga (billion), tera (trillion), peta (quadrillion), exa (quintillion), and for small things milli (thousandth), micro (millionth) and nano (billionth). *(Chapter 2)*
- **METR time horizon** — The length of task, measured by how long it takes a skilled human, that an AI agent can complete with 50% reliability. It has doubled roughly every four to seven months in recent years (see Chapter 57). *(Chapter 7, Chapter 24)*
- **MFU (Model FLOPs Utilization)** — The fraction of the hardware's peak arithmetic speed actually used for the model's necessary math during training. Large runs typically reach about 35–50%. *(Chapter 19, Chapter 32, Chapter 37)*
- **mHC (manifold-constrained hyper-connections)** — DeepSeek's December 2025 version of hyper-connections that constrains the stream-mixing to stable, blending-style operations so signals neither explode nor vanish over many layers. Used in DeepSeek V4. *(Chapter 17)*
- **Microbatch** — A small slice of a training batch that is sent through a pipeline on its own, so different stages can work on different microbatches at the same time. *(Chapter 37)*
- **Microscaling (MX) formats** — An open industry standard from the Open Compute Project (MXFP8, MXFP6, MXFP4) in which each block of 32 low-bit numbers shares one scale factor, a power of two. It lets very small formats cover a wide range of values. *(Chapter 32)*
- **Mid-training** — An intermediate stage between bulk pretraining and post-training that continues training on curated high-quality, long-context or reasoning-heavy data. *(Chapter 19)*
- **Mini-batch** — The small random group of training examples used to compute one gradient step. In language-model training a batch can contain millions of tokens, yet it is still a tiny sample of the full dataset. *(Chapter 6)*
- **Min-p sampling** — Keeping only tokens whose probability is at least a fixed fraction (for example 0.1) of the top token's probability, then rescaling and sampling. *(Chapter 18)*
- **MITRE ATLAS** — A public knowledge base of real attack tactics and techniques used against AI systems, organized like MITRE's ATT&CK catalog of conventional cyberattacks, used by security teams to plan defenses and red-team exercises. *(Chapter 31)*
- **Mixed precision** — Doing most of a model's arithmetic in a low-precision format while keeping key quantities (like running totals and optimizer state) in a higher-precision format. It gets most of the speed with little loss of accuracy. *(Chapter 5, Chapter 13, Chapter 19, Chapter 32, Chapter 37)*
- **Mixture of experts (MoE)** — A model design that contains many smaller sub-networks ("experts") plus a router that sends each token through only a few of them. It gives a model huge total size while keeping the work per token modest. *(Chapter 5, Chapter 15, Chapter 17, Chapter 18, Chapter 24, Chapter 30, Chapter 33, Chapter 37, Chapter 44)*
- **MLA (Multi-head Latent Attention)** — A design from DeepSeek that compresses each token's keys and values into one small "latent" vector for the cache and expands it again when needed, shrinking memory while keeping separate heads. DeepSeek, Kimi and GLM models use it. *(Chapter 15, Chapter 23)*
- **ML compiler (XLA, Triton, torch.compile)** — Software that translates a model written in a framework into efficient instructions for a specific chip, often fusing several operations into one kernel. XLA powers TPUs; Triton (created at OpenAI) lets engineers write GPU kernels in Python-like code. *(Chapter 35)*
- **MLOps** — The practices and tools for deploying, monitoring, updating and maintaining machine-learning models in real products, borrowed from software engineering's "DevOps." *(Chapter 10)*
- **MLPerf** — A set of standardized AI training and inference benchmarks run by the MLCommons industry group, where vendors submit results under shared rules. It is the closest thing to an independent, apples-to-apples comparison of AI hardware, though not every vendor participates. *(Chapter 35)*
- **MLP (feed-forward) layer** — The part of each Transformer block that processes each token's vector individually through large weight matrices. It is thought to store much of a model's factual knowledge. *(Chapter 18)*
- **MLP (multilayer perceptron)** — A neural network made of stacked dense layers. The same name is used for the feed-forward block inside every Transformer layer. *(Chapter 12, Chapter 14)*
- **MLX** — Apple's open-source machine-learning framework for Apple Silicon Macs, used to run and fine-tune models efficiently with the Mac's unified memory. *(Chapter 30)*
- **MMLU (Massive Multitask Language Understanding)** — A 2020 benchmark of multiple-choice questions across 57 school and professional subjects, from law to medicine to mathematics. It was the standard knowledge test of the early chatbot era and is now saturated for frontier models. *(Chapter 24)*
- **MNIST** — A famous dataset of 70,000 small images of handwritten digits (0 to 9), each 28 × 28 pixels. It has been the "hello world" of image recognition since the late 1990s. *(Chapter 14)*
- **Modality** — A kind of data with its own structure, such as text, images, audio, video, or sensor readings from a robot. "Multimodal" means handling more than one. *(Chapter 22)*
- **Mode collapse** — A GAN failure where the generator produces only a few kinds of outputs that fool the discriminator, instead of covering the full variety of the real data. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Model (AI model)** — The trained "brain" of an AI system: a file of learned numbers plus the recipe for using them, which takes an input (such as your question) and produces an output (such as an answer). *(Chapter 1, Chapter 5, Chapter 8, Chapter 18, Chapter 25)*
- **Model card** — A document published with a model describing what it is for, how it was trained, how it scores on evaluations, and its known limitations. The idea was proposed by researchers in 2019 as a "nutrition label" for models. *(Chapter 24, Chapter 30)*
- **Model cascade** — Trying a cheap model first and escalating to an expensive one only when needed, for example when the cheap model reports low confidence or its answer fails a check. *(Chapter 26, Chapter 31)*
- **Model collapse** — The degradation that can occur when models are trained repeatedly on model-generated data without enough fresh human data, losing rare patterns and diversity with each generation. *(Chapter 11, Chapter 19)*
- **Model complexity** — How flexible a model is: how many different patterns it is able to fit. More parameters, deeper trees or higher-degree curves mean more complexity. Also called capacity. *(Chapter 9)*
- **Model dimension (hidden size)** — The number of values in each token's vector as it travels through the model, often written d_model. GPT-2 small used 768; large modern models use several thousand. *(Chapter 15)*
- **Model merging** — Combining the weights of two or more fine-tuned versions of the same base model into a single model, without further training, to get several skills at once. *(Chapter 20)*
- **Model router** — A component that decides which model, mode or replica handles each request, based on its difficulty, the customer's settings, safety signals or which server already has the right prefix cached. *(Chapter 23)*
- **Model routing** — Automatically sending each request to the most suitable model, for example a cheap small model for easy questions and an expensive one for hard ones, to balance quality and cost. *(Chapter 24)*
- **Model snapshot (pinning)** — Using a fixed model version ID in your code so the model's behavior does not change under you without warning when the provider releases an update. *(Chapter 26, Chapter 31)*
- **Model spec** — A public document from an AI company describing how its models should behave, which instructions take priority, and how to resolve conflicts between helpfulness and safety. *(Chapter 20)*
- **Model tier** — One size or price level within a lab's model family, such as Anthropic's Haiku, Sonnet and Opus, or OpenAI's Luna, Sol and Astra. Bigger tiers are usually smarter, slower and more expensive. *(Chapter 24, Chapter 26, Chapter 31)*
- **Moderation classifier** — A smaller model that labels text or images as harmful or off-policy (violence, harassment, self-harm, sexual content and so on) so they can be blocked or reviewed. Model providers offer them, often free, and open ones exist. *(Chapter 31)*
- **Modular data center** — A data center built from prefabricated units, sometimes shipping-container sized, that arrive with power and cooling built in and can be deployed quickly. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Momentum** — An optimizer technique that keeps a running sum of recent gradients (a velocity) and steps along it, so progress speeds up along consistent slopes and zig-zags cancel out. *(Chapter 13)*
- **Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS)** — A search method that estimates how good a move is by playing out many quick simulated games from it and focusing effort on the most promising branches (see Chapter 48). *(Chapter 7, Chapter 21)*
- **Moore's law** — Gordon Moore's observation (1965, revised 1975) that the number of transistors on a chip doubles roughly every two years. It is an industry trend, not a law of nature, and it has slowed. *(Chapter 2, Chapter 7, Chapter 32)*
- **MQA and GQA (multi-query and grouped-query attention)** — Ways to shrink the KV cache by letting query heads share keys and values. In multi-query attention, all query heads share a single key-value head; in grouped-query attention, query heads are split into groups that each share one. *(Chapter 15)*
- **MQA (multi-query attention)** — The extreme version of GQA, in which all query heads share a single set of keys and values. It saves the most memory but can cost some quality. *(Chapter 23)*
- **MSE (Mean Squared Error)** — The average of the squared differences between predictions and true values. Squaring makes every error positive and punishes big misses much more than small ones: an error of 10 counts 100 times as much as an error of 1. *(Chapter 8)*
- **MTBF (mean time between failures)** — The average time between failures of a system. For a cluster, it shrinks as the cluster grows, because each added part brings its own chance of failing. *(Chapter 37)*
- **MTok** — One million tokens, the standard unit in API price lists. "$2 / MTok" means two dollars per million tokens. *(Chapter 26)*
- **Multi-agent system** — A system in which several agents, often with different instructions, tools or models, work on parts of a task and pass results between them. *(Chapter 28)*
- **Multiclass classification** — Classification with three or more possible answers, where each example gets exactly one, such as "cat," "dog" or "bird." *(Chapter 8)*
- **Multicore processor** — A chip with several complete cores that work at the same time. Multicore CPUs appeared in the mid-2000s as the industry's first answer to the power wall. *(Chapter 32)*
- **Multi-head attention** — Running several independent attention calculations ("heads") in parallel in the same layer, each with its own smaller query, key and value matrices, then joining their outputs and mixing them with one more learned matrix (the output projection). *(Chapter 15)*
- **Multi-hop reasoning** — Answering a question by chaining several facts that may be far apart in the text, for example "Who is the manager of the person who wrote the March report?" *(Chapter 17)*
- **Multimodal** — Able to handle more than one kind of data, such as text, images, audio and video, within a single model. *(Chapter 1, Chapter 25)*
- **Multimodal model** — A model that can take in, and sometimes produce, more than one kind of data, such as text, images, audio and video (see Chapter 22). *(Chapter 7, Chapter 16, Chapter 22)*
- **Multi-patterning** — Printing one layer in several exposures (or using spacer tricks) to create features finer than the light could print in one pass. It costs more and lowers yield; China relies on it because it cannot buy EUV tools. *(Chapter 36)*
- **Multiply-accumulate (MAC)** — One multiplication followed by adding the result to a running total, as in total = total + (a × b). Neural networks are built almost entirely from MACs, and one MAC counts as two operations. *(Chapter 2, Chapter 32)*
- **Multiply-add** — The pair of operations "multiply two numbers, then add the result to a running total", which is the basic step of every dot product. One multiply-add counts as 2 FLOPs. *(Chapter 5)*
- **Multi-token prediction (MTP)** — Training a model to predict the next few tokens at each position instead of just one. It can improve learning, and the extra predictions can be used to speed up generation. DeepSeek-V3 and NVIDIA's Nemotron 3 Super use it. *(Chapter 15, Chapter 17, Chapter 23)*
- **Muon** — An optimizer (Keller Jordan, December 2024) for the weight matrices of hidden layers. It takes the momentum update for each matrix and orthogonalizes it, so every direction of change gets equal strength; it is usually paired with AdamW for other parameters. *(Chapter 13)*
- **MXFP4 and NVFP4** — Four-bit number formats in which small blocks of numbers share a scale factor, so weights take about half a byte each. They are used to store and increasingly to train large MoE models. *(Chapter 17)*

## N

- **N** — The amount of equipment needed to carry the full load with nothing spare. If a hall needs five chillers running to stay cool, N = 5. *(Chapter 38)*
- **N+1** — The needed amount plus one spare unit, for example six chillers installed when five are needed. Any one can fail or be serviced without trouble. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Naive Bayes** — A probabilistic classifier that multiplies together the evidence from each feature, assuming (naively) that the features are independent of one another once you know the class. *(Chapter 10)*
- **Naive forecast** — The simplest forecast, "the next value equals the last value" (or, in the seasonal version, "equals the value one cycle ago"). It is the baseline every forecasting model must beat. *(Chapter 10)*
- **NaN (Not a Number)** — A special value a computer produces when arithmetic breaks down, for example infinity minus infinity. A loss of NaN means training has blown up and must be restarted from an earlier saved state. *(Chapter 13)*
- **Nanometer (nm)** — One billionth of a meter (10^−9 m). Transistor features measure tens of nanometers; a human hair is roughly 80,000 nanometers wide. *(Chapter 2)*
- **Narrow AI** — AI built for one task or one domain, such as a chess engine, a spam filter or a face-unlock system. Nearly all AI deployed in the world is narrow. *(Chapter 1)*
- **Native omni model** — A single model that takes in and produces several modalities directly, typically text, audio and images, rather than chaining separate speech and text models. "Omni" is short for "omnimodal." *(Chapter 22)*
- **Natural language processing (NLP)** — The branch of AI that deals with human language: reading, writing, translating and extracting meaning from text. *(Chapter 1)*
- **Natural logarithm (ln)** — The logarithm whose base is e (about 2.718). It is the reverse of the e^x button, and it is the log used in AI loss functions. ln(1) = 0, and ln of any number between 0 and 1 is negative. *(Chapter 6)*
- **NCCL and RCCL** — NVIDIA's (NCCL, "nickel") and AMD's (RCCL) libraries for collective communication, the coordinated data exchanges such as all-reduce that let thousands of GPUs train one model together. *(Chapter 35)*
- **NCCL / RCCL** — NVIDIA's Collective Communications Library (and AMD's version, RCCL), the software that runs all-reduce, all-gather and other collectives efficiently over NVLink and networks. Most training frameworks call it under the hood. *(Chapter 37)*
- **Near-duplicate detection (MinHash)** — Finding documents that are mostly the same by comparing compact fingerprints of their overlapping word sequences, instead of comparing every pair of documents in full. *(Chapter 19)*
- **Nearest neighbor** — The stored vector closest to a query vector, usually measured by cosine similarity. "Finding the k nearest neighbors" means finding the k most similar items. *(Chapter 27)*
- **Needle in a haystack** — A long-context test that hides one fact (the "needle") somewhere in a huge document and asks the model to retrieve it. Frontier models now pass simple versions easily, so harder tests are needed. *(Chapter 23)*
- **Negative prompt** — Text describing what you do not want (for example, "blurry, watermark"). It takes the place of the empty prompt in classifier-free guidance, so the image is pushed away from it. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Neocloud** — A newer cloud company that specializes in renting GPUs for AI, such as CoreWeave, Nebius or Lambda. *(Chapter 4, Chapter 35, Chapter 38)*
- **Network** — Two or more computers connected so they can exchange data, by cable or by radio. *(Chapter 4)*
- **Network switch and radix** — A switch is a box that forwards data between the many devices plugged into it. Its radix is how many ports it has; higher radix means fewer layers of switches are needed to connect a big cluster. *(Chapter 34)*
- **Neural audio codec** — A pair of neural networks that compress audio into a short stream of discrete tokens (the encoder) and rebuild natural-sounding audio from those tokens (the decoder). It is the audio equivalent of a text tokenizer, and it lets a language model "write" sound. *(Chapter 22)*
- **Neural network** — A model made of many simple connected units arranged in layers, where each unit computes a weighted sum of its inputs and applies a simple "squash." It is loosely inspired by brain neurons but is really a stack of multiplications and additions. *(Chapter 1, Chapter 5, Chapter 7, Chapter 10, Chapter 12)*
- **Neuromorphic chip** — A chip designed to mimic the brain's spiking neurons, computing only when signals arrive. It is promising for very low-power sensing, but it does not run today's large language models (see Chapter 54). *(Chapter 35)*
- **Newton–Schulz iteration** — A short recipe of repeated matrix multiplications that approximately orthogonalizes a matrix. Muon uses about five rounds because it is far faster on GPUs than computing the exact answer. *(Chapter 13)*
- **Next-token prediction** — The training task of predicting each token from all the tokens before it. Applied to trillions of tokens of text, it is how every base language model learns. *(Chapter 15, Chapter 18)*
- **NF4 (NormalFloat 4)** — A 4-bit number format designed for neural-network weights, whose 16 possible values are spaced to match the bell-shaped way weights are usually distributed, so less information is lost than with evenly spaced levels. *(Chapter 30)*
- **N-gram model** — A simple language model that predicts the next word from only the previous few words (n − 1 of them), using counts from a large body of text. A "bigram" model looks back one word. *(Chapter 6)*
- **NIC (Network Interface Card)** — The card that connects a server to a network. AI servers use very fast NICs, such as NVIDIA's ConnectX-8 at 800 Gb/s, usually one per GPU. *(Chapter 34, Chapter 38)*
- **NIST AI RMF (AI Risk Management Framework)** — A voluntary US framework, released in January 2023, for managing AI risks through four functions: Govern, Map, Measure and Manage. A Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1) followed in July 2024. *(Chapter 31)*
- **NOC and SOC** — A network operations center (NOC) is the room or team that monitors systems and networks around the clock; a security operations center (SOC) does the same for physical and cyber security. *(Chapter 38)*
- **No-code** — Building software by connecting visual blocks and filling in forms instead of writing code. "Low-code" means mostly visual, with small bits of code where needed. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Node and edge** — A node is one item in a graph (an atom, a person, a city); an edge is a connection between two nodes (a bond, a friendship, a road). *(Chapter 17)*
- **Node, leaf and depth** — In a decision tree, a node is a point where a question is asked, a leaf is a final box holding a prediction, and depth is the number of questions from the top to the deepest leaf. *(Chapter 10)*
- **Noise** — Random variation in data that hides the underlying pattern, such as measurement errors or one-off flukes. Models should learn the signal and ignore the noise. *(Chapter 6, Chapter 9)*
- **Noise schedule** — The plan for how much noise is present at each timestep, for example a little at first and then more and more until the image is gone. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Non-blocking network** — A network with enough capacity in its upper layers that every device can send at full speed at the same time without slowing others down. AI back-ends are usually built non-blocking; front-end networks usually are not. *(Chapter 34, Chapter 38)*
- **Nondeterministic** — Producing possibly different outputs for the same input on different runs. Language models are nondeterministic because they sample from probabilities, and even at temperature 0 small variations can creep in. *(Chapter 31)*
- **Nonlinear function** — Any function whose graph bends, curves or has corners, such as y = x × x or "replace negative numbers with zero". Neural networks need nonlinear steps to model anything more interesting than straight lines. *(Chapter 5)*
- **Non-linear function** — Any function whose graph is not a straight line, such as one with a bend, a curve or a threshold. Neural networks need non-linearity to model the curved, messy patterns of the real world. *(Chapter 12)*
- **Normal distribution** — The symmetrical bell-shaped pattern where most values cluster near the mean and extremes are rare. About 68% of values lie within one standard deviation of the mean and about 95% within two. *(Chapter 6)*
- **Normalization layer** — A layer that rescales a group of numbers to a standard range (for example mean 0 and spread 1) before passing them on, so that training stays stable as values flow through many layers. *(Chapter 13)*
- **Notebook (Jupyter, Google Colab)** — A document mixing text and runnable code cells, with each cell's output shown underneath. Colab runs notebooks free in your browser on Google's computers. *(Chapter 3, Chapter 26)*
- **NPU (neural processing unit)** — A small AI accelerator built into phones and laptops (Apple's Neural Engine, which debuted in 2017, is one example) for running AI features on the device itself, such as face unlock and photo search. *(Chapter 2, Chapter 32, Chapter 35)*
- **Numerical aperture (NA)** — A measure of how wide a cone of light a lens or mirror system can gather and focus. Higher NA means finer detail, but it requires larger and more precise optics. *(Chapter 36)*
- **NVFP4** — NVIDIA's 4-bit format for Blackwell and later chips. It uses smaller blocks of 16 values, each with a finer 8-bit (FP8) scale, plus one scale for the whole tensor, which makes it more accurate than plain FP4. *(Chapter 32)*
- **NVFP4 and MX formats** — Four-bit (and six- and eight-bit) number formats that attach a shared scale factor to each small block of values so that very few bits can still cover a wide range. MXFP4 is an open industry standard using blocks of 32 values; NVFP4 is NVIDIA's version using blocks of 16. *(Chapter 35)*
- **NVLink** — NVIDIA's high-speed GPU-to-GPU connection, far faster than PCIe: about 900 GB/s per GPU on Hopper (NVLink 4), 1.8 TB/s on Blackwell (NVLink 5), and 3–3.6 TB/s on Rubin (NVLink 6). *(Chapter 34)*
- **NVLink-C2C** — NVIDIA's chip-to-chip link that joins the CPU and GPUs inside a superchip so they can share memory coherently, far faster than PCIe. *(Chapter 34)*
- **NVLink domain (scale-up domain)** — The group of accelerators joined by an ultra-fast, memory-like fabric, such as the 72 GPUs in an NVL72 rack. Inside the domain, any GPU can directly read and write the others' memory. *(Chapter 34)*
- **NVLink Fusion** — NVIDIA's program that lets other companies' CPUs or accelerators connect into NVLink fabrics. AWS has said its future Trainium4 chip will support it. *(Chapter 34)*
- **NVSwitch** — A switch chip that connects many GPUs' NVLinks so that every GPU in the group can talk to every other at full NVLink speed simultaneously. *(Chapter 34)*

## O

- **OAuth** — The standard "Sign in with..." and "Allow access" system that lets one app use your account on another service without ever seeing your password, with permissions you can later revoke. *(Chapter 25, Chapter 28)*
- **Object hallucination** — When a vision-language model describes something that is not in the image, usually something that commonly appears in similar scenes, such as a refrigerator in a kitchen photo that has none, or a street name on a sign that is actually blank. *(Chapter 22)*
- **Observability** — The ability to see what is happening inside a running system from the records it produces, so you can debug problems and spot trends. *(Chapter 31)*
- **OCR (optical character recognition)** — Software that finds text in an image and converts it into machine-readable characters. Classic OCR finds text regions, recognizes characters, and outputs plain text with little sense of layout. *(Chapter 22, Chapter 27)*
- **ODM (original design manufacturer)** — A company that designs and builds servers and racks for others to sell or use. Foxconn, Quanta, Wistron and Wiwynn (all Taiwanese) build most AI servers, alongside brands like Supermicro and Dell. *(Chapter 36)*
- **Offline evaluation** — Testing an AI system on a fixed dataset before release, like a rehearsal. It is repeatable and safe but only covers the inputs you thought of. *(Chapter 31)*
- **Ollama** — A free tool for downloading and running open models with one command (for example ollama run gemma4:e2b), available as a command-line program and desktop app, with a built-in local API for other programs. *(Chapter 30)*
- **One-hot encoding** — Turning a category into several yes/no columns, one per possible value, so that "Plus" out of Free, Plus and Pro becomes [0, 1, 0]. *(Chapter 11, Chapter 14)*
- **Online evaluation** — Measuring an AI system on real production traffic, through monitoring, user feedback and experiments. It reflects real behavior but is noisier and happens after users are exposed. *(Chapter 31)*
- **Online learning** — Updating a model continuously as new data arrives, instead of training once and freezing it. Fraud detectors and news-feed rankers often update this way. *(Chapter 8)*
- **On-policy and off-policy data** — On-policy data is generated by the very model being trained, so it shows that model's real habits and mistakes. Off-policy data comes from somewhere else (people, other models, older versions), so it may not cover the errors the current model actually makes. *(Chapter 20)*
- **On-policy distillation** — A distillation method in which the student writes its own answers and the teacher grades every token of them, combining the realism of reinforcement learning (the student learns from its own mistakes) with the dense, token-by-token feedback of distillation. *(Chapter 20)*
- **OpenAI-compatible API** — An API that accepts requests in the same format as OpenAI's, so code, coding agents and apps written for the cloud can be pointed at a local model just by changing the address. *(Chapter 30)*
- **Open source** — Software whose source code is published so anyone can use, study, change and share it under a license. *(Chapter 3)*
- **Open-source AI** — In the strict sense defined by the Open Source Initiative's Open Source AI Definition 1.0 (October 2024), a model released with its weights, its code, and enough information about its training data for others to study and rebuild it. Most "open" models are open-weight but not open source in this sense. *(Chapter 30)*
- **Open-source model** — Strictly, a model released with its weights, training code and training data, so others can fully rebuild it. The term is often used loosely to mean open-weight, which is not the same thing. *(Chapter 24)*
- **Open-weight model** — An AI model whose trained parameters are published so anyone can download and run it. The training data and code are often still private, so "open-weight" is not the same as fully open source. *(Chapter 1, Chapter 3, Chapter 7, Chapter 24, Chapter 26, Chapter 30)*
- **Operating system (OS)** — The master program (Windows, macOS, Linux, Android, iOS) that manages the hardware and lets apps run and share the processor, memory, files and devices safely. *(Chapter 3)*
- **Optical circuit switch (OCS)** — A switch that steers beams of light with tiny movable mirrors, connecting fibers to each other without converting the signal to electricity. It can rewire a network's topology in software and uses very little power. *(Chapter 34)*
- **Optical fiber** — A strand of very pure glass that carries data as pulses of laser light over hundreds of meters to many kilometers with little loss. It needs power-hungry converters at both ends, unlike passive copper cable. *(Chapter 34)*
- **Optical transceiver** — A plug-in module, about the size of a pack of gum, that converts electrical signals to laser light and back, letting a switch or network card drive a fiber. 800G and 1.6T modules are standard in 2026 AI clusters. *(Chapter 34)*
- **Optimizer** — The algorithm that turns gradients into actual parameter updates. Plain gradient descent is the simplest; Adam, AdamW and Muon are smarter variants used to train modern models. *(Chapter 6, Chapter 13, Chapter 30)*
- **Optimizer state** — The extra numbers an optimizer stores for each parameter between steps, such as momentum's velocity or Adam's two running averages. For large models this extra memory can be several times the size of the model itself. *(Chapter 13, Chapter 33, Chapter 37)*
- **Orchestrator-workers** — A pattern in which a lead model breaks a task into pieces at run time, hands each piece to a worker model, and combines their results. *(Chapter 28)*
- **Order of magnitude** — A factor of ten. "Two orders of magnitude bigger" means about 100 times bigger. Estimates that are "within an order of magnitude" are within a factor of ten of the truth. *(Chapter 2)*
- **ORPO (Odds Ratio Preference Optimization)** — A 2024 method that combines supervised fine-tuning and preference learning into a single training stage, with no reference model. *(Chapter 20)*
- **Orthogonalization (of an update)** — Reshaping a matrix so that all of its independent directions have the same strength while keeping the directions themselves. Muon applies it to each weight matrix's update. *(Chapter 13)*
- **OSAT (outsourced semiconductor assembly and test)** — Companies that package and test chips for others, such as ASE (Taiwan) and Amkor (US-headquartered, with plants mainly in Asia). *(Chapter 36)*
- **OSWorld** — A benchmark of real computer tasks (in web browsers, office apps, file managers and more) run inside virtual machines, where an agent must complete each task through the screen. Scores are the share of tasks completed. *(Chapter 22, Chapter 24)*
- **Outcome reward model (ORM)** — A grader that scores only the final result of a solution, such as whether the final answer is correct. *(Chapter 21)*
- **Outlier** — A value far away from the rest of the data, like a billionaire in a bar. Outliers can drag the mean a long way, which is why the median is often the better summary. *(Chapter 6)*
- **Out-of-band management network** — A separate, independent network used only to monitor and control equipment, so technicians can reach a machine's baseboard management controller (a small built-in control chip) even when the machine's main system or main network has failed. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Output layer** — The final layer, which produces the network's answer, such as one number for a price or one score per possible next token. *(Chapter 12)*
- **Output speed (tokens per second)** — How fast text appears once generation has started, as experienced by one user. English readers skim at a few words per second, so anything above roughly 30–50 tokens per second feels instant for reading, while agents and code generation benefit from much more. *(Chapter 23)*
- **Overfitting** — When a model fits its training examples so closely, noise included, that it does worse on new data. It is like memorizing last year's exam answers instead of learning the subject. *(Chapter 6, Chapter 9, Chapter 13)*
- **Over-refusal** — A model declining a harmless request because it looks superficially similar to a harmful one. *(Chapter 18, Chapter 20)*
- **Overthinking** — A reasoning model spending far more thinking than a question needs, wasting time and money, and sometimes talking itself out of a correct first answer. *(Chapter 21)*
- **Over-training** — Training a model on far more tokens than is compute-optimal, producing a smaller model that is cheaper to run for a given level of ability. *(Chapter 19)*
- **OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications** — A widely used list of the ten most critical security risks for applications built on language models, published by OWASP, a nonprofit open security community. The current edition is dated 2025. *(Chapter 31)*

## P

- **Package** — The finished assembly of one or more dies, any memory stacks, the interposer and substrate, sealed together as one component that mounts on a board. When people say "a GPU" they usually mean one package. *(Chapter 34)*
- **Package substrate** — The layered base of a chip package, with coarser wiring than the interposer, that spreads power and signals from the chips out to hundreds or thousands of solder balls connecting to the circuit board. *(Chapter 34)*
- **Packet** — A small chunk of data, usually up to about 1,500 bytes, sent across a network with a label saying where it came from and where it is going. Big messages are split into many packets and reassembled. *(Chapter 4)*
- **Padding** — Extra pixels, usually zeros, added around the border of an input so a filter can be centered on edge pixels. With the right padding, the output stays the same size as the input. *(Chapter 14)*
- **PagedAttention** — A technique introduced with the open-source vLLM engine in 2023 that stores each request's KV cache in small fixed-size blocks scattered anywhere in memory, tracked by a lookup table, the way an operating system manages computer memory in pages. Blocks are handed out only as needed. *(Chapter 23)*
- **Pairwise comparison** — Showing a judge two responses side by side and asking which is better. It is the most common way to collect preference data. *(Chapter 20)*
- **pandas** — The standard Python library for working with tables of data, called DataFrames, much like a spreadsheet you control with code. *(Chapter 10)*
- **Parallelism (distributed training)** — Doing many operations at the same time instead of one after another. It is the central idea behind GPUs and AI accelerators. *(Chapter 2, Chapter 19, Chapter 32)*
- **Parallelization** — A workflow that runs several model calls at the same time, either on different parts of a task or on the same task several times so the results can be compared or voted on. *(Chapter 28)*
- **Parallel tool calls** — Several tool calls requested in a single model reply, so the application can run them at the same time and return all the results together, saving round trips. *(Chapter 28)*
- **Parameter (weight)** — One adjustable number inside a model that shapes its behavior, like one knob on a giant mixing desk. Modern chatbot models have billions to trillions of them. *(Chapter 1, Chapter 5, Chapter 7, Chapter 8, Chapter 12, Chapter 18, Chapter 37)*
- **Parametric knowledge** — Information a model absorbed into its weights during training and can recall without any document in front of it. *(Chapter 18)*
- **Parent-child retrieval** — A technique, also called small-to-big, that searches over small, precise chunks but passes the larger section each one belongs to into the prompt, combining sharp matching with enough surrounding context to answer. *(Chapter 27)*
- **Parsing** — Extracting usable text and structure (headings, tables, lists) from files such as PDFs, web pages, slides and spreadsheets. *(Chapter 27)*
- **Partial derivative** — The slope of a function with respect to one input while every other input is held fixed. A model with a billion knobs has a billion partial derivatives. *(Chapter 6)*
- **Pass@k** — The probability that at least one of k attempts at a problem is correct. Pass@1 is the chance of getting it right on a single try; it is the standard way to report reasoning accuracy. *(Chapter 21)*
- **pass@k and pass^k** — Two reliability measures for an agent run k times on the same task. pass@k is the chance that at least one run succeeds; pass^k is the chance that every run succeeds, the better measure of consistency. *(Chapter 31)*
- **Patch** — A small square of an image (or a small block of video across space and time) that a Transformer treats as one token. *(Chapter 15, Chapter 22)*
- **Patch panel** — A panel of labeled ports where permanent cables end, so that technicians can connect or reroute equipment with short "patch" cables. *(Chapter 38)*
- **PCIe (PCI Express)** — The standard connector and protocol inside computers that links CPUs to GPUs, network cards and SSDs. A 16-lane PCIe 5.0 slot moves about 64 GB/s in each direction; PCIe 6.0 doubles that. *(Chapter 34)*
- **PDU (power distribution unit)** — Equipment that distributes power to racks. It can be a large floor-standing cabinet with a transformer and breakers, or a rack-mounted power strip with many outlets. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Peak performance** — The theoretical maximum operations per second on a spec sheet, reached only if every unit does useful work on every tick. Real software almost never gets there. *(Chapter 32)*
- **Peak versus achieved performance** — Peak is the theoretical maximum speed on a chip's spec sheet; achieved is what real software actually gets, often 30 to 60% of peak for large training jobs and much less when waiting on memory. *(Chapter 2)*
- **Percentile** — The value below which a given percentage of the data falls. The 50th percentile is the median; the 99th percentile describes the slowest or largest 1% of cases. *(Chapter 6)*
- **Perceptron** — Frank Rosenblatt's 1957–58 learning machine: a single artificial neuron that multiplies each input by a weight, adds them up, outputs 1 if the total passes a threshold and 0 otherwise, and adjusts its weights whenever it gets an example wrong. *(Chapter 7, Chapter 10, Chapter 12)*
- **Permission-aware retrieval** — Retrieval that only searches the passages the person asking is allowed to see, usually by copying each document's access rights into its metadata and filtering every search by the user's identity. *(Chapter 27)*
- **Permission prompt** — A pause in which the agent asks you to approve a specific action, such as running a command, editing a file or pushing to GitHub. You can usually allow an action once, always, or never. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Permissive license** — A license, such as MIT or Apache-2.0, that lets almost anyone use, modify and sell products built on the work, with few conditions beyond keeping the copyright notice. *(Chapter 24, Chapter 30)*
- **Perplexity** — e raised to the average cross-entropy loss, read as "the number of equally likely choices the model is effectively picking between at each token". Lower is better. *(Chapter 6, Chapter 12, Chapter 19)*
- **Personal data** — Any information that identifies a person or relates to an identifiable person, such as names, emails, phone numbers, health details or payment information. Laws such as the EU's GDPR regulate how it is collected and protected. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Photolithography** — Printing a circuit pattern onto a wafer by shining light through a patterned stencil onto a photoresist coating. Often shortened to "lithography." *(Chapter 36)*
- **Photomask (reticle)** — A plate carrying the pattern for one layer of a chip, usually drawn four times larger than it will appear on the wafer. Light passes through it (or, in EUV, bounces off it) to print the pattern. *(Chapter 36)*
- **Photonic computing** — Performing calculations such as matrix multiplications with light passing through optical components. Converting between electrical and optical signals adds overhead, and it is not yet competitive for frontier AI. *(Chapter 35)*
- **Photonic interconnect** — Moving data between chips with light instead of electrical signals, using optical components built into or beside the chip package, to get more bandwidth per watt over longer distances. *(Chapter 35)*
- **Photoresist** — A light-sensitive coating spread on a wafer. Light changes its chemistry so that exposed (or unexposed) areas can be washed away, leaving a stencil for the next step. *(Chapter 36)*
- **Picojoule (pJ)** — A trillionth of a joule, a unit of energy. A joule is roughly the energy to lift an apple one meter; chip operations are measured in picojoules. *(Chapter 33)*
- **PII (personally identifiable information)** — Data that can identify a specific person, such as a name, email address, ID number, face or precise location. *(Chapter 11, Chapter 19, Chapter 25, Chapter 31)*
- **pip** — Python's standard tool for installing libraries, as in pip install anthropic. In Colab you type !pip install ..., where the ! means "run this as a command, not as Python". *(Chapter 26)*
- **Pipeline bubble** — Idle time in pipeline parallelism while the pipeline fills at the start of a step and drains at the end, when some stages have nothing to do. *(Chapter 37)*
- **Pipeline parallelism (PP)** — Splitting a model's layers into consecutive stages on different GPUs, like an assembly line, with each stage passing its outputs to the next. *(Chapter 37)*
- **Pixel** — One tiny dot of a digital image, usually stored as three numbers for its red, green and blue brightness. *(Chapter 3, Chapter 14, Chapter 22)*
- **Planar transistor** — The original flat transistor, with the gate lying on top of a flat channel. It was the standard from the 1960s until about 2011. *(Chapter 36)*
- **Plan mode** — A coding-agent setting in which the agent investigates the code and writes a step-by-step plan for your approval before it changes anything. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Planning (agents)** — An agent breaking a goal into smaller steps, often written down as a to-do list, then working through them and revising the plan when results are surprising. *(Chapter 28)*
- **Plugin (agent plugin)** — An installable bundle of agent capabilities, such as skills, MCP servers, sub-agents and hooks, packaged so it can be added to an agent product in one step. *(Chapter 28)*
- **Pod (SuperPOD)** — A pre-designed block of racks wired together as one unit, with its own network layer and shared cooling, that can be replicated to build a cluster. Google's TPU pods and NVIDIA's SuperPODs are examples. *(Chapter 34)*
- **Point of presence (PoP)** — A provider's network entrance in a city, where it accepts users' traffic, terminates encryption and passes requests onto its own private backbone. Big providers have hundreds around the world. *(Chapter 44)*
- **Policy** — An RL agent's strategy: its rule for choosing an action in each situation. Learning in RL means improving the policy. *(Chapter 8, Chapter 20)*
- **Polynomial** — A formula built from powers of the input, such as a + b × x + c × x². Its degree is the highest power used. Higher-degree polynomials can draw more wiggly curves. *(Chapter 9)*
- **Polysilicon** — Ultra-pure silicon made of many small crystals stuck together. It is the raw feedstock that gets melted and regrown into a single perfect crystal for chipmaking. *(Chapter 36)*
- **Pooling (max pooling, average pooling)** — Shrinking a feature map by summarizing each small region with one number: the largest value (max pooling) or the average (average pooling). It keeps the strongest signals and makes the network less sensitive to small shifts. *(Chapter 14)*
- **Positional encoding** — Information added to a Transformer so it knows the order of tokens, either by adding a position signal to each token's vector or by changing how attention compares tokens at different positions. *(Chapter 15)*
- **Position bias** — A judge's tendency to favor whichever answer appears first (or last) when comparing two answers, regardless of quality. *(Chapter 31)*
- **Post-training** — All the training that happens after pretraining (supervised fine-tuning, preference learning, reinforcement learning, safety and character training) to turn a base model into a useful, safe product. *(Chapter 20)*
- **Post-training quantization (PTQ)** — Rounding a finished model's weights to fewer bits after training, often using a small sample of data to choose good scales. Quick and cheap, with some quality loss at low bit widths. *(Chapter 23)*
- **Powered land and powered shell** — Powered land is a site that already has a secured grid connection but no building. A powered shell is a building with utility power and basic structure delivered, but without the tenant's power distribution, cooling and IT fit-out. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Power law** — A relationship where one quantity changes as another raised to a fixed power, such as area = side × side. On a log-log plot (a chart with log scales on both axes) it appears as a straight line. *(Chapter 6, Chapter 19)*
- **Power wall** — The limit, reached in the mid-2000s, where making a single processor core faster would make it too hot to cool. It ended the era of rapidly rising clock speeds. *(Chapter 32)*
- **PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization)** — A reinforcement-learning algorithm that improves a policy in small, "clipped" steps, refusing to change any action's probability too much in one update. It was the workhorse of RLHF from InstructGPT onward. *(Chapter 20)*
- **Precision** — How finely a number format can distinguish nearby values, set mostly by how many bits it uses. Higher precision means more significant digits and smaller rounding errors. *(Chapter 5, Chapter 9, Chapter 32)*
- **Precision@k** — The share of the top k retrieved passages that are actually relevant. If 2 of the top 5 are relevant, precision@5 is 40%. *(Chapter 27)*
- **Precision–recall curve** — A graph of precision against recall across all thresholds. When positives are rare, it shows the false-alarm problem much more clearly than an ROC curve. *(Chapter 9)*
- **Prediction (in AI)** — A model's output about something unknown, such as the next word in a sentence, tomorrow's temperature or whether a payment is fraudulent. Most AI outputs are predictions, even when they look like creativity. *(Chapter 1)*
- **Preference data** — Human judgments comparing two or more model outputs ("A is better than B"), used to train chatbots to give answers people prefer. *(Chapter 11, Chapter 20)*
- **Preference tuning** — Fine-tuning on pairs of a better ("chosen") and worse ("rejected") answer to the same prompt, so the model learns which kind of answer you prefer. *(Chapter 30)*
- **Prefill** — The first phase of answering, in which the model reads the entire prompt at once, in parallel. Its length mostly determines the wait before the first word. *(Chapter 4, Chapter 23, Chapter 25, Chapter 33, Chapter 37, Chapter 44)*
- **Pre-norm and post-norm** — Two placements of normalization in a Transformer block: before each sub-layer (pre-norm, the modern standard, more stable in deep stacks) or after it (post-norm, as in the original 2017 design). *(Chapter 13)*
- **Pretrained model** — A model that has already been trained, usually on a large general dataset, and is published so others can reuse it rather than train from scratch. *(Chapter 14)*
- **Pretraining** — The first, most expensive stage of building a language model, in which it learns to predict the next token across trillions of tokens of text, picking up grammar, facts and patterns along the way (see Chapter 19). *(Chapter 7, Chapter 8, Chapter 19)*
- **Primary key and join** — A primary key is a column that uniquely identifies each row, such as a user ID. A join combines two tables by matching rows that share a key value. *(Chapter 11)*
- **Principal component analysis (PCA)** — A method that finds new axes, each a weighted mix of the original features, ordered by how much of the data's variation they capture. Keeping only the first few compresses the data. *(Chapter 10)*
- **Prior and posterior** — In Bayes' rule, the prior is your belief before the evidence (1% chance of being sick), and the posterior is your updated belief after it (9% after one positive test). *(Chapter 6)*
- **Probability** — A number from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain) that measures how likely something is. It can also be written as a percentage, so 0.25 means 25%. *(Chapter 6)*
- **Probability distribution** — A complete list of possible outcomes with their probabilities, which always add up to 1. A bag of marbles is a physical distribution: the share of each color is its probability of being drawn. *(Chapter 6, Chapter 16)*
- **Processing-in-memory** — Chip designs that place simple compute units inside or right next to memory arrays, so data does not have to travel to a distant processor. It is promising for memory-bound work but not yet mainstream for frontier AI. *(Chapter 33)*
- **Process node** — A named generation of chip-manufacturing technology, such as TSMC N3 or Intel 18A. Each new node packs transistors more densely and usually improves speed or power, but the number in the name is a marketing label, not a measurement. *(Chapter 36)*
- **Process reward model (PRM)** — A grader, usually a trained model, that scores each intermediate step of a solution, so mistakes can be caught where they happen. *(Chapter 21)*
- **Production** — The live version of an app that real users depend on. Careful teams first try changes on "staging", a private copy of production. *(Chapter 29, Chapter 31)*
- **Programming language** — A precise artificial language for writing instructions, such as Python, JavaScript or C++. Unlike English, every statement has exactly one meaning. Text written in one is called source code, or just code. *(Chapter 3)*
- **Program (software)** — A precise list of instructions that a computer follows exactly, step by step. Apps, websites and AI systems are all programs (also called software). *(Chapter 1, Chapter 3)*
- **Progressive disclosure** — Loading only a short summary of each available skill or tool at first, and the full instructions only when a task needs them, to save room in the context window. *(Chapter 25, Chapter 28)*
- **Project** — A workspace in a chat app that groups related chats with shared files and instructions (and, in Claude, a separate memory), so every chat in it starts with the same background. *(Chapter 25)*
- **Projector (connector)** — A small neural network, often just one or two layers, that converts the vision encoder's output vectors into vectors the language model can read as if they were token embeddings. *(Chapter 22)*
- **Prompt** — Everything you send to the model to get a response: instructions, background, data, examples and questions. In a chat app, the prompt you write is only one part of what the model sees. *(Chapter 25)*
- **Prompt caching** — Reusing the stored intermediate results for a repeated beginning of a prompt (such as a long document or system prompt), so it does not have to be processed again. Providers bill cached input at a large discount. *(Chapter 18, Chapter 23, Chapter 26, Chapter 27, Chapter 44)*
- **Prompt chaining** — Splitting a task into a sequence of prompts, where each step's output feeds the next, so that each step is simpler and easier to check. *(Chapter 25, Chapter 28)*
- **Prompt engineering** — The craft of writing prompts that reliably get the output you want. Despite the name, it is mostly clear briefing: context, examples, format and reasons, rather than secret phrases. *(Chapter 25)*
- **Prompt injection** — An attack that hides instructions inside content the model reads (a web page, document, email or tool result) in the hope that the model will follow them instead of its real instructions. *(Chapter 18, Chapter 25, Chapter 31)*
- **Proof assistant** — Software, such as Lean, that checks every step of a mathematical proof written in a precise formal language and accepts only proofs that are completely correct. *(Chapter 21)*
- **Prosody (paralinguistic cues)** — Everything in speech beyond the words: tone, pitch, rhythm, pauses, emphasis, laughter, hesitation and emotion. It often changes the meaning of the words. *(Chapter 22)*
- **Protocol** — An agreed set of rules for how computers communicate, like etiquette for machines. TCP/IP, HTTP and TLS are protocols. *(Chapter 4)*
- **Prototype** — A quick, rough version of a product built to test an idea or show it to people, not meant to be secure, complete or long-lived. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Proxy model** — A small, cheap model trained to test a choice (such as a data mixture) before committing to it in an expensive full-size run. *(Chapter 19)*
- **Proxy variable** — A feature that indirectly stands in for a sensitive attribute, such as a zip code standing in for race or a hobby standing in for gender. *(Chapter 11)*
- **Public-key cryptography** — Encryption that uses a pair of keys: a public key that anyone can use to lock (encrypt) data, and a private key, kept secret, that alone can unlock it. It lets strangers set up a shared secret over an open network. *(Chapter 4)*
- **PUE (power usage effectiveness)** — A data center's total power divided by the power used by its computing equipment. A PUE of 1.2 means 20 percent extra for cooling, power conversion and lighting. *(Chapter 37, Chapter 40, Chapter 44)*
- **Pull request (PR)** — A proposal to merge changes from one branch into another, where others can review, comment and approve before the changes are accepted. *(Chapter 3, Chapter 29)*
- **Push and pull** — Pushing sends your new commits from your computer up to the shared copy online; pulling brings other people's new commits down to you. Cloning makes your first full copy of a repository. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Pydantic** — A popular Python library for defining data shapes as simple classes and checking that data matches them. AI SDKs can turn a Pydantic class into a JSON Schema for you. *(Chapter 26)*
- **Python** — A popular, readable programming language that dominates AI work, largely because of its huge collection of ready-made libraries for math, data and neural networks. *(Chapter 3, Chapter 26)*
- **PyTorch** — The most widely used deep-learning framework, originally developed at Meta and now governed by the PyTorch Foundation. It is Python-first and dominates research and open-model development. *(Chapter 13)*

## Q

- **QK-clip (MuonClip)** — Moonshot's technique, used to train Kimi K2, of shrinking the query and key weights whenever attention scores grow past a threshold, paired with the Muon optimizer. *(Chapter 17)*
- **QK-norm** — Normalizing query and key vectors to a fixed length before computing attention scores, so the scores stay bounded and training stays stable. *(Chapter 17)*
- **QLoRA** — LoRA performed on a base model whose frozen weights are compressed to 4 bits, while the small adapters train in higher precision. It cuts memory roughly fourfold, so large models can be fine-tuned on a single GPU. *(Chapter 30)*
- **Quadratic cost** — Cost that grows with the square of the input length. Doubling the text length roughly quadruples the total work of full attention, because every token is compared with every other. *(Chapter 17)*
- **Quality classifier** — A model trained to score documents for usefulness (for example, educational value) so that a pipeline can keep the best ones. Often trained on grades produced by a larger model. *(Chapter 19)*
- **Quantization** — Converting a trained model's numbers to fewer bits (for example from 16-bit to 4-bit) to make it smaller and faster, accepting a small loss of accuracy. It is like saving a photo as a smaller JPEG. *(Chapter 5, Chapter 13, Chapter 18, Chapter 23, Chapter 30, Chapter 32)*
- **Quantization-aware training (QAT)** — Training (or fine-tuning) the model while simulating low-precision rounding, so it learns weights that survive being rounded. It loses much less quality at 4 bits than rounding afterwards. *(Chapter 23, Chapter 30)*
- **Quantization level (quant)** — A named recipe for how a model was compressed, such as Q4_K_M or Q8_0 in GGUF files. The number is roughly the bits per weight; "K" marks the newer block-wise methods, and S, M and L mean small, medium and large variants of the recipe. *(Chapter 30)*
- **Quantum computer** — A machine that computes with quantum bits that can hold combinations of 0 and 1. It may one day solve certain chemistry and math problems, but it is not an AI accelerator today and has no near-term path to training frontier models. *(Chapter 35)*
- **Query** — In attention, a vector each token computes from its current vector with a learned weight matrix, describing what information the token is looking for. *(Chapter 15)*
- **Query decomposition** — Splitting a complex question into simpler sub-questions that are searched separately, so that each part retrieves its own evidence. *(Chapter 27)*
- **Query pipeline** — The steps that run for every question: optionally rewrite the question, retrieve candidate passages, rerank them, assemble the prompt, generate an answer with citations, and optionally check the answer. *(Chapter 27)*
- **Query rewriting** — Using a model to turn the user's question into a better search query, for example by making a follow-up question standalone, fixing typos or expanding abbreviations. *(Chapter 27)*
- **Quick disconnect** — A dripless pipe connector that seals both sides automatically when unplugged, so a liquid-cooled server can be removed while the rest of the rack keeps running. *(Chapter 40)*

## R

- **Rack** — A standard metal cabinet that holds servers and network gear in data centers. A 2026 AI rack can draw around 120 to 140 kilowatts, about as much as 100 homes. *(Chapter 4, Chapter 34, Chapter 38)*
- **Rack and stack** — The work of physically installing IT equipment: unpacking racks or servers, placing them, connecting power, cooling and network, and testing them. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Rack density** — The electrical power drawn by one rack, in kilowatts. It is the single number that best predicts how a data hall must be designed, from floor strength to cooling method. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Rack power shelf** — A tray of power supply units inside an AI rack that converts the building's alternating current into roughly 50 volts of direct current for the servers, which is then carried to every tray by a busbar. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Rack unit (U)** — The standard unit of height inside a rack: 1.75 inches (4.445 cm). A "1U server" is one unit tall, like a thick pizza box; a 42U rack has 73.5 inches of mounting space. *(Chapter 38)*
- **RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation)** — A technique that searches a collection of documents for passages relevant to a question and inserts them into the prompt, so the model answers from those passages. *(Chapter 18, Chapter 27)*
- **Rail-optimized network** — A cluster wiring scheme in which GPU 1 of every server connects to one group of switches (rail 1), GPU 2 of every server to another (rail 2), and so on, so the most common GPU-to-GPU exchanges take the fewest hops. *(Chapter 34)*
- **Raised floor** — A floor made of removable tiles on pedestals, with an open space beneath it. Historically, data centers pushed cold air through that space and up through perforated tiles in front of the racks. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Random forest** — A bagged ensemble of decision trees in which each tree also sees only a random subset of features at each split, which makes the trees more different and their vote more reliable. *(Chapter 10)*
- **Random initialization** — Setting a neural network's parameters to small random numbers, often drawn from a normal distribution, before training begins. The randomness lets different parts of the network learn different things. *(Chapter 6)*
- **Randomized controlled trial (RCT)** — An experiment in which tasks or people are randomly split into groups that get different treatments (here, AI allowed or not), so that any difference in results can be credited to the treatment rather than to luck or selection. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Random search** — Trying randomly chosen combinations of hyperparameter values. It often finds good settings faster than a grid, because it tests more distinct values of the settings that matter most. *(Chapter 9)*
- **Rank (r)** — In LoRA, the width of the thin adapter matrices. Typical values are 8 to 64. Higher rank means more capacity to learn and more trainable numbers. *(Chapter 30)*
- **Rate** — A quantity measured per unit of something else, such as tokens per second, dollars per million tokens or operations per watt. Most AI speeds, costs and efficiencies are rates. *(Chapter 2)*
- **Rate limit** — A cap on how many requests or tokens you may send in a period of time, such as per minute. Going over it returns a 429 error. *(Chapter 4, Chapter 26)*
- **R² (coefficient of determination)** — The share of the variation in the true values that the model explains. 1 means perfect; 0 means no better than always predicting the average; below 0 means worse than that. *(Chapter 9)*
- **RDMA and RoCE** — Remote Direct Memory Access lets one machine read or write another machine's memory without involving either machine's CPU. RoCE ("rocky") is RDMA carried over Ethernet. AI networks use it so network cards can move data straight between GPU memories. *(Chapter 34)*
- **ReAct** — An agent pattern, from a 2022 paper, in which the model alternates between writing a short piece of reasoning ("thought"), taking an action such as a tool call, and reading the result ("observation"), repeating until it can answer. *(Chapter 28)*
- **Rear-door heat exchanger (RDHx)** — A radiator filled with cool water, mounted as the back door of a rack, that absorbs heat from the server exhaust as it leaves. Passive versions rely on the servers' own fans; active versions add fans to the door. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Reasoning effort** — A setting, chosen by you or a developer, for how much a reasoning model should think, usually with levels such as low, medium and high, and sometimes higher levels above those. *(Chapter 21, Chapter 24)*
- **Reasoning model** — A language model trained to produce a long internal chain of "thinking" before giving its final answer, which improves results on math, coding and logic problems. OpenAI's o1, first released as o1-preview on September 12, 2024, introduced the idea to the public. *(Chapter 1, Chapter 7, Chapter 21, Chapter 25)*
- **Recall** — Of all the real positives, the share the model caught: TP ÷ (TP + FN). Also called sensitivity or the true positive rate. It answers "how many of the wolves did it spot?" *(Chapter 9)*
- **Recall@k** — The share of test questions for which a correct passage appears in the top k retrieved results. If the right chunk is in the top 5 for 16 of 20 questions, recall@5 is 80%. *(Chapter 27)*
- **Receptive field** — The region of the original input that a particular neuron can "see," directly or through earlier layers. It grows with each layer, so deep neurons respond to large parts of the image. *(Chapter 14)*
- **Reciprocal rank fusion (RRF)** — A simple way to merge several ranked lists. Each document gets 1 ÷ (k + its rank) from each list it appears in, the points are added, and documents are re-sorted by total. k is a constant, commonly 60, that stops the very top ranks from dominating. *(Chapter 27)*
- **Reclaimed water** — Treated wastewater reused for non-drinking purposes such as cooling, instead of drawing on drinking-water supplies. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Recommender system** — Software that predicts which items (videos, products, posts, songs) you are likely to engage with and ranks them for you. Your social media feed and streaming suggestions are recommender systems. *(Chapter 1, Chapter 10)*
- **Reconstruction loss** — How different an autoencoder's rebuilt output is from its original input, often measured with mean squared error. Training pushes it down. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Rectified flow** — A form of flow matching that trains on straight-line paths between noise and data, so that generation needs far fewer steps. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Red-teaming** — Deliberately attacking your own system, the way an adversary would, to find weaknesses before real attackers or unlucky users do. *(Chapter 31)*
- **Reduce-scatter** — A collective that sums numbers across GPUs but leaves each GPU holding only one slice of the result. *(Chapter 37)*
- **Redundancy** — Having extra equipment beyond what is strictly needed, so that the failure or maintenance of one piece does not cause an outage. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Reference model** — A frozen copy of the model from before RL training began. It is used as an anchor: the policy is penalized for drifting too far from it. *(Chapter 20)*
- **Refusal** — When a model declines a request, usually for safety or policy reasons. Over the API it shows up as a stop reason, which your code should check before trusting the content. *(Chapter 26)*
- **Refusal calibration** — Training a model so that it refuses requests that are genuinely harmful, helps with the many requests that only look edgy, and handles the gray zone sensibly, often by helping with the safe part. *(Chapter 20)*
- **Region (cloud)** — A geographic area where a cloud provider runs a group of data centers, such as US East or Europe West. Choosing a region affects speed, price and which country's laws apply to your data. *(Chapter 4)*
- **Register** — One of a small number of storage slots inside a processor core that hold the numbers being worked on right now. Registers are the fastest memory in a computer. *(Chapter 2, Chapter 33)*
- **Regression** — A supervised task where the answer is a number on a scale, such as a price, a temperature, or a delivery time. (The name is historical; it has nothing to do with going backward.) *(Chapter 8, Chapter 29, Chapter 31)*
- **Regularization** — Any technique that discourages unnecessary complexity in order to reduce overfitting, such as penalizing large weights, limiting tree depth, stopping training early or adding noise during training. *(Chapter 9, Chapter 13)*
- **Re-identification** — Working out who a person is in a supposedly anonymous dataset by linking it with other available data. *(Chapter 11)*
- **Reinforcement fine-tuning (RFT)** — Fine-tuning a model with reinforcement learning against a grader that you define, such as a checker for correct answers in your domain, rather than with fixed examples. *(Chapter 20, Chapter 30)*
- **Reinforcement learning (RL)** — Learning by trial and error: a system takes actions, receives rewards or penalties, and gradually learns which behavior earns the most reward. *(Chapter 1, Chapter 7, Chapter 8, Chapter 20)*
- **Rejection sampling** — Generating many candidate outputs and keeping only those that pass a check (a correct final answer, passing tests, a high reward-model score), then training on the survivors. *(Chapter 20)*
- **Relational database** — A database that stores data in tables linked by shared columns, and is queried with SQL. Examples include PostgreSQL, MySQL and SQLite. *(Chapter 11)*
- **ReLU (Rectified Linear Unit)** — The most common activation function: it outputs the input if it is positive and zero otherwise. ReLU(3) = 3, ReLU(−2) = 0. *(Chapter 5, Chapter 12, Chapter 14)*
- **Remote hands** — A data center service in which on-site technicians perform physical tasks, such as rebooting a server, swapping a part or checking a cable, on behalf of a customer who is not there. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Reparameterization trick** — The VAE technique of writing a random sample as "mean + spread × random noise," so the randomness comes from outside the network and gradients can still flow back through the mean and the spread during training. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Replica** — One complete running copy of a model, spread over however many chips it needs. A popular model runs as thousands of replicas, and the router spreads requests among them. *(Chapter 23)*
- **Repository (repo)** — A project folder whose complete history of changes is tracked by Git. *(Chapter 3, Chapter 29)*
- **Request and response** — The two halves of a web conversation. The client sends a request (what it wants, plus any data); the server sends a response (a status code plus the result). *(Chapter 4)*
- **Request body** — The main content of a request, the letter inside the envelope. For an AI API, it is the model name, your messages and your settings, written in JSON. *(Chapter 26)*
- **Reranker** — A second-stage model, usually a cross-encoder, that re-scores the top 50 to 150 candidates from the first search and keeps the best 10 to 20 for the prompt. *(Chapter 27)*
- **Reshoring** — Moving manufacturing back to a company's home country (or to allied countries, sometimes called "friend-shoring") to reduce supply-chain risk, usually with government subsidies. *(Chapter 36)*
- **Residual** — The error for one example: the true value minus the model's prediction. A positive residual means the model guessed too low. *(Chapter 8, Chapter 10)*
- **Residual connection (skip connection)** — A shortcut that adds a block's input to its output (output = input + f(input)), giving signals and gradients an unobstructed path around the block. It made networks with 100 or more layers trainable. *(Chapter 13, Chapter 14)*
- **Residual stream** — In a Transformer, the running vector each token carries through all the layers; every layer reads it and adds its own contribution back, so information and gradients flow freely from bottom to top. *(Chapter 13, Chapter 15, Chapter 17, Chapter 18)*
- **ResNet (residual network)** — A family of image networks introduced in 2015 that stacks residual blocks; a 152-layer version won that year's ImageNet competition. *(Chapter 13, Chapter 14)*
- **Resolution (lithography)** — How many pixels an image or screen has, written as width × height, such as 1920 × 1080. More pixels means more detail and more data. *(Chapter 3, Chapter 36)*
- **Restricted (trusted-access) model** — A model released only to vetted organizations, or in a safer public version with extra safeguards, because its full capabilities in areas like cyberattacks are judged too risky for open release. *(Chapter 7)*
- **Reticle limit** — The largest area (about 26 × 33 mm, roughly 858 mm²) that a lithography machine can print in one exposure, which caps the size of a single die. *(Chapter 34, Chapter 36)*
- **Retrieval** — Finding the pieces of information most relevant to a question from a larger collection, so they can be shown to a model or a person. A search engine is a retrieval system. *(Chapter 27)*
- **Retriever** — The part of a RAG system that takes a question and returns candidate passages, for example by hybrid search over a vector database and a keyword index. *(Chapter 27)*
- **Reverse process (denoising)** — The learned procedure that turns pure noise into a clean sample by repeatedly predicting and removing a portion of the noise. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Reward** — A number that tells a learning system how well it just did, like a score in a game or a treat for a dog. *(Chapter 1, Chapter 8)*
- **Reward hacking** — When a model finds a way to score highly on its reward without doing the intended task, such as flattering a judge that likes flattery or editing a test so that broken code "passes." *(Chapter 20, Chapter 29)*
- **Reward model** — A model trained on preference data to output a single number (a "reward" or score) predicting how much humans would like a given response to a given prompt. *(Chapter 20)*
- **Reward model over-optimization** — Training so hard against a reward model that the policy exploits its flaws: the reward model's score keeps rising while real quality falls. *(Chapter 20)*
- **RGB** — Describing a color as amounts of red, green and blue light, each typically from 0 to 255. Mixing them makes every color a screen can show. *(Chapter 3)*
- **Ridge point** — The arithmetic intensity at which a chip's memory-bandwidth limit meets its compute limit: peak FLOPS divided by bandwidth. Jobs below it are memory-bound; jobs above it are compute-bound. *(Chapter 33)*
- **Ring all-reduce** — An all-reduce in which GPUs are arranged in a circle and pass chunks of data to their neighbor step by step, first adding (reduce-scatter) and then sharing the finished sums (all-gather), so every link carries an equal share of the traffic. *(Chapter 37)*
- **RISC-V** — A free, open instruction set that anyone can use without paying license fees. It is popular with startups and appears in some AI chips, such as Meta's MTIA and Tenstorrent's processors. *(Chapter 32, Chapter 35)*
- **RLAIF (Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback)** — RLHF in which an AI model, usually guided by written principles or a rubric, supplies most of the preference labels instead of humans. *(Chapter 20)*
- **RL environment** — A simulated task, such as a software sandbox, a website clone or a math problem with an automatic checker, where a model can practice and be scored. Labs increasingly treat these as "the new training data." *(Chapter 11, Chapter 21)*
- **RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback)** — A training step where people rate or compare a model's answers, and the model is adjusted toward the kinds of answers people preferred. It helped turn raw text predictors into helpful assistants. *(Chapter 1, Chapter 7, Chapter 8, Chapter 20)*
- **RLVR (Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards)** — Reinforcement learning in which the reward comes from an automatic, objective check (a correct final answer, passing tests, a verified proof) rather than from human judges or a learned reward model. *(Chapter 21)*
- **RMA (return merchandise authorization)** — The process of sending a failed part back to its manufacturer for repair or replacement under warranty. Large AI sites ship failed GPU trays out by the pallet. *(Chapter 38)*
- **RMSE (Root Mean Squared Error)** — The square root of the mean squared error. It puts the error back into the original units (dollars, degrees), while still weighting big misses heavily. *(Chapter 9)*
- **RMSNorm** — A simpler, cheaper layer normalization (2019) that only divides by the root-mean-square of the values, without subtracting the mean. Most open large language models use it. *(Chapter 13, Chapter 15)*
- **RNN (Recurrent Neural Network)** — A neural network that processes a sequence one step at a time, combining each new input with a memory vector carried over from the previous step. The same weights are reused at every step. *(Chapter 14)*
- **robots.txt** — A file on a website telling automated crawlers which parts they may visit. It is a request that reputable crawlers honor, not a lock that stops anyone. *(Chapter 11, Chapter 19)*
- **ROC curve** — A graph of the true positive rate (recall) against the false positive rate as the threshold slides from strict to lenient. The name comes from "receiver operating characteristic," a term from World War II radar engineering. *(Chapter 9)*
- **ROCm** — AMD's open-source software platform for programming its GPUs, its answer to CUDA. It includes compilers, math libraries and the RCCL communication library. *(Chapter 35)*
- **Role** — The label on each turn of a conversation, such as system, developer, user, assistant or tool. The model was trained to treat each role differently. *(Chapter 18)*
- **Role prompting** — Telling the model who to be ("You are an experienced tax accountant") to focus its vocabulary, tone and the considerations it brings up. *(Chapter 25)*
- **Rollout** — One complete attempt by the model at a task during RL training: the full chain of thought and answer for a question, or the full sequence of actions in an environment. *(Chapter 21)*
- **Roofline model** — A chart that shows the highest speed a job can reach on a given chip, based on the job's arithmetic intensity. Speed is capped by memory bandwidth for low-intensity jobs (a sloped roof) and by peak compute for high-intensity jobs (a flat roof). *(Chapter 33)*
- **RoPE (Rotary Position Embedding)** — A way of encoding position by rotating each token's query and key vectors by an angle proportional to the token's position. When two tokens are compared with a dot product, the result depends on how far apart they are, not on their absolute positions. *(Chapter 15)*
- **RoPE scaling** — Stretching the rotary position scheme that tells a model where each token sits (see Chapter 15), using methods such as YaRN, so a model trained mostly on shorter texts can handle much longer ones after brief extra training. *(Chapter 23)*
- **Round-trip time (RTT)** — The time for a small message to travel to a destination and for the reply to come back, often measured with the ping command. It is the basic unit of network delay. *(Chapter 4, Chapter 44)*
- **Router (gating network)** — A device that forwards data between networks, choosing where each piece should go next. Your home Wi-Fi box is a router; the internet has millions more. *(Chapter 4, Chapter 17, Chapter 21)*
- **Routing** — Classifying each incoming request and sending it to the most suitable prompt, model or specialist, such as easy questions to a small model and hard ones to a large one. *(Chapter 26, Chapter 28)*
- **Routing collapse (expert collapse)** — An MoE failure where the router sends most tokens to a few experts, which keep improving while the rest go unused. *(Chapter 17)*
- **Row, pod and cluster (data center layout)** — A row is a line of racks standing side by side. A pod is a repeatable block of rows with its own network layer and shared power and cooling. A cluster is a set of pods networked together to work on shared jobs. *(Chapter 38)*
- **RPM and TPM** — Requests per minute and tokens per minute, the usual units of rate limits. Providers usually raise your limits as you spend more and build a track record, through what they call usage tiers. *(Chapter 26)*
- **Rubric** — A written list of criteria, often with point values, used to grade a response consistently. Rubrics let AI judges grade open-ended work in a more checkable, less biased way. *(Chapter 21, Chapter 31)*
- **Rule-based system** — A program whose behavior comes entirely from IF-THEN rules written by people. It is predictable and easy to inspect, but it only knows what its authors thought of. *(Chapter 1)*
- **RULER** — A long-context benchmark from NVIDIA researchers (2024) that goes beyond one needle: multiple needles, tracing chains of variables, and aggregating information spread through the text, to measure a model's effective context length. *(Chapter 23)*
- **Rules-based system** — A program whose behavior is entirely spelled out by human-written if-then rules, such as "if the email contains the word lottery, mark it as spam." Older AI systems built this way were called expert systems. *(Chapter 8)*
- **RWKV and xLSTM** — Two modern recurrent architectures that train in parallel yet generate with constant memory: RWKV from an open-source community, and xLSTM, a 2024 update of the LSTM. *(Chapter 17)*

## S

- **Saddle point** — A spot in the loss landscape where the slope is zero but which is a low point in some directions and a high point in others, like the center of a horse saddle. Training can stall near them because the gradient there is tiny. *(Chapter 13)*
- **Safetensors** — A file format for model weights that stores only numbers and cannot contain hidden code. Older formats based on Python's "pickle" could run code when loaded, so prefer safetensors (or GGUF) files from trustworthy uploaders. *(Chapter 30)*
- **Safety case** — A structured, evidence-based argument that a specific AI system is acceptably safe to deploy in a specific setting, open to review by others. *(Chapter 24)*
- **Safety classifier** — A separate, usually smaller model that labels inputs or outputs (for example "this request seeks weapons help") so that they can be blocked, rewritten or routed to a more cautious model. *(Chapter 20, Chapter 24, Chapter 44)*
- **Safety framework** — A lab's published policy that defines dangerous capability levels and the safeguards required at each level, such as Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy, OpenAI's Preparedness Framework and Google DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework. Chapter 56 compares them. *(Chapter 24)*
- **Sample (audio)** — One measurement of a sound wave's height at one instant. A recording is a long list of samples. *(Chapter 3)*
- **Sample rate** — How many samples are taken per second, measured in hertz (Hz), which simply means "per second". CD audio uses 44,100 samples per second. *(Chapter 3)*
- **Sampler (scheduler)** — The algorithm that decides how big each denoising step is and how predictions are combined to move from noise to image. Better samplers reach good images in fewer steps. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Sampling** — Picking an outcome at random according to a probability distribution, like drawing a marble from a bag. Chatbots sample each next token, which is why their answers vary. *(Chapter 6, Chapter 16, Chapter 18)*
- **Sampling bias** — A distortion that arises when the data collected is not representative of the situation the model will face, such as training a speech recognizer mostly on one accent. The model then learns patterns that do not hold in general. *(Chapter 6, Chapter 11)*
- **Sampling rate** — How many times per second a microphone's signal is measured and stored as a number. Speech is often recorded at 16,000 samples per second; music at 44,100 or more. *(Chapter 22)*
- **Sandbox** — An isolated, disposable computer environment where a model can run code, browse or edit files without affecting anything real. *(Chapter 21, Chapter 28, Chapter 29, Chapter 31)*
- **Scalar** — A single ordinary number, like 2 or −0.5, as opposed to a list (vector) or table (matrix) of numbers. Multiplying a vector by a scalar stretches or shrinks it. *(Chapter 5)*
- **Scale-across (multi-site training)** — Linking several data center campuses, often hundreds of kilometers apart, with high-capacity fiber so they can work on one training job. Microsoft calls its version an "AI WAN." *(Chapter 34, Chapter 38)*
- **Scaled dot-product attention** — The standard attention recipe: score each query against each key with a dot product, divide by the square root of the key length, apply softmax, and use the resulting weights to average the values. Written compactly: softmax(Q × K-transposed ÷ √d) × V. *(Chapter 15)*
- **Scale-up, scale-out and front-end networks** — Scale-up joins GPUs inside a rack or domain with ultra-fast links such as NVLink. Scale-out (the back-end) joins many racks for shared jobs using InfiniBand or Ethernet. The front-end carries user traffic, internet traffic and storage. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Scale-up vs. scale-out** — Scale-up means making one tightly coupled unit bigger, such as putting more GPUs into one ultra-fast NVLink domain. Scale-out means connecting many such units over a conventional network. *(Chapter 34)*
- **Scale-up vs. scale-out network** — The scale-up network (such as NVLink) tightly links a small group of accelerators with very high bandwidth; the scale-out network (InfiniBand or Ethernet) connects many such groups across the cluster at lower bandwidth per chip. *(Chapter 37)*
- **Scaling law** — An empirical formula showing how a model's loss falls predictably as training compute, data and parameters grow. *(Chapter 19)*
- **Scaling laws** — Observed patterns, usually straight lines on log-log plots, showing that a model's error falls predictably as training compute, data and parameters grow together. *(Chapter 6, Chapter 7, Chapter 13)*
- **Scheduler (inference)** — The software that decides which group of accelerators handles each incoming request and when, packing requests into batches to keep the hardware busy while meeting speed targets. *(Chapter 44)*
- **Scientific notation** — Writing a number as a value between 1 and 10 multiplied by a power of ten, such as 2 × 10^25 instead of 20,000,000,000,000,000,000,000,000. The exponent tells you how many places to move the decimal point. *(Chapter 2)*
- **scikit-learn** — The standard free Python library for classical machine learning. It can train a linear or logistic regression model in one line. *(Chapter 8, Chapter 10)*
- **SDK (software development kit)** — An official library that wraps an API so you can call it with a few lines of code in a given language, instead of building raw web requests by hand. Anthropic, OpenAI and Google all publish SDKs for Python, TypeScript and other languages. *(Chapter 26)*
- **Secret (environment variable)** — A place to store a key outside your code, such as Colab's Secrets panel or a system setting, so the key never appears in a file, notebook or screenshot you might share. *(Chapter 26)*
- **Section 232 tariff** — A US import tax imposed on national-security grounds under Section 232 of a 1962 trade law. It was applied to semiconductors in 2026 and used to collect the 25 percent charge on H200 exports to China. *(Chapter 36)*
- **Security vulnerability** — A flaw in software that an attacker could exploit to steal data, take control or cause damage, such as a database that anyone on the internet can read. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Seed** — The number that fixes the starting random noise. The same seed with the same model, prompt and settings reproduces the same image; a different seed gives a different image for the same prompt. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Selectivity (selective state)** — Letting the input decide, token by token, how much of the existing state to keep and how much new information to write, so a fixed-size memory can hold what matters and drop filler. *(Chapter 17)*
- **Self-attention** — Attention in which a sequence attends to itself: every token computes how relevant every other token (in a chatbot, every earlier token) is to it, and gathers a weighted blend of their information. *(Chapter 15)*
- **Self-build vs. lease** — A company can build and own its data centers (self-build), or rent them from a developer under a long-term lease. Most hyperscalers do both. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Self-consistency (majority voting)** — Sampling several independent reasoning paths for the same question and choosing the final answer that appears most often. *(Chapter 21)*
- **Self-hosting** — Running software on a server you control instead of using the vendor's hosted service, which gives you control over data and cost but makes you responsible for upkeep. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Self-play** — Training a game-playing program by having it play against copies of itself, so it always faces an opponent at its own level and can eventually surpass every human teacher. *(Chapter 7)*
- **Self-preference bias** — A judge's tendency to prefer answers written by itself or by models from its own family. *(Chapter 31)*
- **Self-reported score** — A benchmark result published by the company that built the model, rather than measured by an independent group. Self-reported scores are not lies, but the company chose the settings, the harness and which benchmarks to show. *(Chapter 24)*
- **Self-supervised learning** — Learning where the labels are created automatically from the data itself, for example by hiding the next word of a sentence and asking the model to predict it. This is how large language models are pretrained. *(Chapter 8, Chapter 16, Chapter 19)*
- **Semantic and acoustic tokens** — Two kinds of audio tokens. Semantic tokens capture what is said (the words and their meaning); acoustic tokens capture how it sounds (the voice, tone, accent and background). Speaking models typically need both. *(Chapter 22)*
- **Semantic search** — Searching by meaning rather than by matching keywords: embed the query and every document, then return the documents whose vectors are closest to the query's. *(Chapter 14, Chapter 27)*
- **Semiconductor** — A material, mainly silicon, whose ability to carry electricity can be precisely controlled, so it can act as a conductor or an insulator. Transistors are made from semiconductors, and the chip industry takes its name from them. *(Chapter 2, Chapter 36)*
- **Semi-structured data** — Data with some labels and organization but no rigid table, such as JSON files, web pages or log files. *(Chapter 11)*
- **Semi-supervised learning** — Learning from a small set of labeled examples plus a much larger set of unlabeled ones. *(Chapter 8)*
- **Sentence embedding** — A single vector that represents the meaning of a whole sentence or passage, so that passages with similar meaning have similar vectors. *(Chapter 14)*
- **Seq2seq (sequence-to-sequence)** — A design in which one network reads an input sequence and another produces an output sequence, possibly of a different length. Machine translation was its first big use. *(Chapter 14)*
- **Sequence** — An ordered list of items where the order matters, such as the words of a sentence, the samples of a sound recording or the daily prices of a stock. *(Chapter 14)*
- **SerDes (serializer/deserializer)** — Circuits that turn many slow parallel signals into one very fast stream of bits for transmission, and back again at the other end. They are the basic building blocks of every high-speed link. *(Chapter 34)*
- **Server** — A computer that waits for requests and answers them. It usually has no screen or keyboard and sits in a rack in a data center. *(Chapter 4, Chapter 26)*
- **Server-sent events (SSE)** — A web technique where a server keeps a connection open and pushes a stream of small updates to the client. Most AI APIs use it to stream tokens. *(Chapter 4, Chapter 26)*
- **Server-side tool** — A tool that the AI provider runs on its own servers, such as web search, web fetch or code execution. You switch it on in your request; the provider runs it and feeds the result to the model without your code doing anything. *(Chapter 28)*
- **Service-level objective (SLO)** — A target a service commits to internally or to customers, such as "95% of requests get their first token within 1 second." *(Chapter 23)*
- **Service tier** — A pricing option that trades speed or priority for cost on the same model: for example a cheaper tier that may queue your request, or a premium tier that answers faster. *(Chapter 26)*
- **Serving** — The engineering of running inference for many users at once: receiving requests, scheduling them onto chips, generating tokens and sending them back, reliably and cheaply. *(Chapter 23)*
- **Serving engine** — The software that loads a model onto chips and serves requests efficiently, implementing batching, KV-cache management, caching, speculative decoding, quantized kernels and streaming. *(Chapter 23, Chapter 35)*
- **SFT (Supervised Fine-Tuning)** — Fine-tuning on examples of the desired input and output, such as a user's question paired with an ideal assistant answer. The model learns to imitate the examples. *(Chapter 20)*
- **Shadow AI** — Employees using AI tools their employer has not approved, often by pasting work data into personal chatbot accounts. It is widespread and is usually the first rung of adoption. *(Chapter 31)*
- **Shape** — The size of a matrix or tensor, written as rows × columns (for example 3 × 4), or as a longer list for tensors. Shapes must line up for operations like multiplication to make sense. *(Chapter 5)*
- **Sharding** — Splitting a large set of numbers into pieces ("shards") stored on different GPUs, so each GPU holds only its own piece. *(Chapter 37)*
- **Shared expert** — An expert in an MoE layer that processes every token, holding broadly useful knowledge so the routed experts can specialize. *(Chapter 17)*
- **Shared memory** — On NVIDIA GPUs, a block of SRAM inside each streaming multiprocessor that the programmer controls directly. Fast kernels load tiles of matrices into it and reuse them many times before going back to main memory. *(Chapter 33)*
- **Shortcut learning** — When a model succeeds by exploiting an accidental clue in the training data, such as the background of a photo or a marker on an X-ray, instead of the thing it was supposed to learn. *(Chapter 9)*
- **Short-term memory (agents)** — Whatever is in the agent's context window right now: the goal, recent tool calls and results, and its own notes. It disappears when the context is cleared. *(Chapter 28)*
- **SigLIP** — A 2023 Google variant of CLIP that scores each image–caption pair independently with a sigmoid ("match or not?") instead of a softmax over the whole batch. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Sigmoid** — An S-shaped activation function that squeezes any number into the range 0 to 1: large negative inputs give nearly 0, large positive inputs give nearly 1, and 0 gives exactly 0.5. *(Chapter 12, Chapter 20)*
- **Sigmoid function** — The S-shaped curve that turns any number into a value between 0 and 1. Large positive inputs give values near 1, large negative inputs give values near 0, and an input of 0 gives exactly 0.5. *(Chapter 8)*
- **Silent data corruption** — A hardware fault in which a chip produces wrong results without crashing or reporting an error. At large scale it is rare per chip but common in total, and hard to detect. *(Chapter 37)*
- **Silent truncation** — When a model quietly stops early, skips rows or summarizes part of a long input without saying so, often because the job was too long for one answer. *(Chapter 25)*
- **Silicon** — A chemical element (symbol Si) found in sand and rock. Its crystal structure and its ability to be made more or less conductive by adding tiny amounts of other elements make it the base material of almost every chip. *(Chapter 36)*
- **SiLU (also called Swish)** — A smooth ReLU-like activation computed as the input times the sigmoid of the input. Its gated cousin SwiGLU is the standard building block in the feed-forward part of most current large language models. *(Chapter 12)*
- **SIMD (Single Instruction, Multiple Data)** — A way of computing where one instruction acts on many numbers at once, for example adding two rows of 16 numbers in a single step. CPUs have SIMD units; GPUs take the idea much further. *(Chapter 32)*
- **SimPO (Simple Preference Optimization)** — A 2024 method that drops the reference model and uses the average per-token log-probability of a response as its implicit reward, plus a required margin between chosen and rejected answers. Averaging per token reduces the pull toward ever-longer answers. *(Chapter 20)*
- **SIMT (Single Instruction, Multiple Threads)** — NVIDIA's way of running parallel code: thousands of threads each work on their own piece of data, but they are grouped so that one instruction drives a whole group at once. It feels like writing code for one worker that is magically run by thousands. *(Chapter 32)*
- **Slab-on-grade** — A design where racks sit directly on a solid concrete floor, with power, network cables and coolant pipes running overhead or in trenches. It is now common for heavy AI racks. *(Chapter 38)*
- **SLA (service-level agreement)** — A contract that promises a level of uptime or performance, such as 99.99% availability, with refunds or penalties if the provider misses it. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Sliding-window attention** — Attention in which each token looks only at the most recent N tokens instead of the whole history, capping the cost. Models often alternate sliding-window layers with full-attention layers. *(Chapter 15)*
- **Slope** — How steep a line is, measured as "rise over run": the change in output divided by the change in input. A positive slope goes up to the right; a negative slope goes down; zero is flat. *(Chapter 5)*
- **Slope and intercept** — The two parameters of a straight line. The slope says how much the prediction rises for each one-unit increase in the input; the intercept is the prediction when the input is zero. *(Chapter 8)*
- **Soft labels** — Training targets that are full probability distributions (cat 0.70, kitten 0.20...) rather than a single right answer. They let a student learn the teacher's sense of what is nearly right. *(Chapter 20)*
- **Softmax** — A function that turns any list of scores into probabilities that are all positive and add up to 1: raise e to each score, then divide each result by their total. Bigger scores get disproportionately bigger shares. *(Chapter 6, Chapter 12, Chapter 14, Chapter 18)*
- **Software moat (lock-in)** — An advantage that comes from customers' investment in a vendor's software ecosystem, which makes switching to a rival costly even when the rival's hardware is competitive. *(Chapter 35)*
- **Source code** — The human-readable instructions that make up a program, written in a programming language such as Python or JavaScript and stored as plain text files. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Space** — A small hosted demo app on Hugging Face where you can try a model in the browser without downloading it. *(Chapter 30)*
- **Spacetime patch** — A small block of a video latent that spans a little area of the frame and a short stretch of time, treated as one token by a video Transformer. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Sparse and compressed attention** — Attention variants that compare a new token with only a selected subset of earlier tokens, or with compressed summaries of them, instead of every token in full. DeepSeek V4's Compressed Sparse Attention is an example. *(Chapter 17)*
- **Sparse attention** — Attention in which each token attends to a selected subset of earlier tokens, such as the top-k most relevant ones, instead of all of them. DeepSeek Sparse Attention is an example. *(Chapter 15, Chapter 23)*
- **Spatial reasoning** — Understanding where things are relative to each other and to the viewer, including left and right, in front and behind, distances, sizes and how objects would move or fit. *(Chapter 22)*
- **Special token** — A reserved token with a control meaning rather than text content, such as "end of text" or "start of the assistant's turn." It lets the model see the structure of a conversation. *(Chapter 15, Chapter 18)*
- **Specific heat capacity** — How much energy it takes to warm one kilogram of a substance by 1 °C. Water needs about 4,186 joules; air needs about 1,005 joules. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Specificity** — Of all the real negatives, the share correctly left alone: TN ÷ (TN + FP). A test with high specificity rarely raises false alarms on healthy people. *(Chapter 9)*
- **Spec (specification)** — A written description of what a piece of software must do, for whom, with what constraints and how you will know it works. A good spec is the brief you hand a coding agent. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Spectrogram** — A picture of sound showing how much energy each frequency has at each moment in time. Speech models often treat slices of it like image patches or tokens. *(Chapter 15, Chapter 22)*
- **Speculative decoding** — A speed-up in which a fast "draft" proposes several next tokens and the large model verifies them all in one pass, keeping every proposed token up to the first one it disagrees with. With the right acceptance rule, the output follows exactly the same probabilities as the large model alone (Leviathan and colleagues, 2022). *(Chapter 23)*
- **Spending limit** — A cap you set in a provider's billing settings so that usage stops (or you are alerted) once you have spent a chosen amount in a month. *(Chapter 26)*
- **SQL injection** — A classic web attack in which text typed into a form is mistakenly executed as a database command. It was largely tamed by strictly separating commands from data, a separation language models do not have. *(Chapter 31)*
- **SQL (Structured Query Language)** — The standard language for asking a database questions and changing its data, such as SELECT name FROM customers WHERE plan = 'Pro'. *(Chapter 3, Chapter 11)*
- **Square root** — The number that, multiplied by itself, gives the number you started with. The square root of 25 is 5, because 5 × 5 = 25. Spreadsheets compute it with =SQRT(25). *(Chapter 5)*
- **SRAM (static RAM)** — Very fast memory built directly into the chip, using about six transistors per bit. It is used for caches and registers, and in some AI chips (Groq, Cerebras) as the main memory. It is expensive in chip area, so there is never much of it. *(Chapter 33, Chapter 35)*
- **SSD (solid-state drive)** — Storage built from flash memory that keeps data when the power is off. It holds datasets and saved checkpoints and is far slower than DRAM. *(Chapter 33)*
- **Stable Diffusion** — An open-weight latent diffusion image model first released in August 2022. Because anyone could download and run it, it became the base of a huge ecosystem of tools, fine-tunes and add-ons. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Standard deviation** — The square root of the variance, which puts the spread back into the original units. Roughly, it is how far a typical value sits from the mean. *(Chapter 6, Chapter 21)*
- **Standardization** — Rescaling a numeric feature by subtracting its average and dividing by its standard deviation, so values become "how many standard deviations from typical." *(Chapter 11)*
- **STaR (Self-Taught Reasoner)** — A 2022 method in which a model writes step-by-step reasoning for problems, keeps the reasoning that reached correct answers, and, for problems it got wrong, is shown the right answer and asked to produce reasoning that leads to it. It then fine-tunes on all of this and repeats. *(Chapter 20)*
- **Stateless** — Keeping no memory between requests. LLMs are stateless: any "memory" you see is the app re-sending past text or inserting saved notes into the context. *(Chapter 18, Chapter 25)*
- **State (memory state)** — A fixed-size set of numbers a layer carries from token to token as its running summary of everything it has read. Unlike a KV cache, it does not grow with the length of the text. *(Chapter 17)*
- **State-space model (SSM)** — A sequence layer that updates a fixed-size internal state at each step, as an RNN does, but is designed so it can also be trained in parallel on GPUs. The idea comes from control engineering. *(Chapter 17)*
- **Statistical machine learning** — Learning patterns from data using probability and statistics, the dominant style of AI from the 1990s to about 2012. Spam filters and early recommendation systems are examples. *(Chapter 7)*
- **Status code** — A three-digit number at the start of every HTTP response summarizing what happened: 200 means OK, 404 means not found, 429 means too many requests. *(Chapter 4, Chapter 26)*
- **Step distillation** — Training a fast student model to reproduce in one to four steps what a slow teacher model produces in dozens of steps. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Step function** — An activation that outputs 0 below a threshold and 1 above it, like a light switch. The perceptron used it. It cannot be trained with slopes, because its slope is zero almost everywhere. *(Chapter 12)*
- **Step (iteration)** — One parameter update: one batch through the forward pass, the loss, the backward pass and the optimizer. *(Chapter 12)*
- **Stochastic gradient descent (SGD)** — Gradient descent where each step estimates the gradient from a small random batch of examples instead of the entire dataset. The steps are noisier but vastly cheaper, so training makes much faster progress. *(Chapter 6, Chapter 8, Chapter 12)*
- **Stop condition** — A rule that ends an agent's loop: the model declares the goal met, or a limit is hit, such as a maximum number of steps, a spending cap, a time limit, or a checkpoint that requires a person's approval. *(Chapter 28)*
- **Stop reason** — The API's note on why generation ended: it finished naturally (end_turn), hit your max_tokens cap, wants to call a tool (tool_use), or declined the request (refusal). *(Chapter 26, Chapter 28)*
- **Stop token (end-of-turn token)** — A special token the model learned to produce when a reply is finished. When the sampler picks it, generation ends. *(Chapter 18)*
- **Storage (SSD and hard drive)** — Slower but permanent places to keep files, which survive power-off. An SSD (solid-state drive) stores bits as trapped electric charges in flash chips; an older hard drive stores them magnetically on spinning disks. *(Chapter 2)*
- **Straggler** — A GPU or server that runs slower than the rest, for example because of a failing part or overheating, and holds up thousands of others at every synchronization point. *(Chapter 37)*
- **Stranded capacity** — Power or cooling that was built but cannot be used because some other limit runs out first, such as floor space, weight capacity or cooling for a particular row. Designers try to keep power, cooling and space in balance so none is wasted. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Stratified sampling** — Splitting data so that each part keeps the same mix of classes as the whole, for example 2% spam in the training, validation and test sets alike. *(Chapter 9)*
- **Streaming multiprocessor (SM)** — The repeated building block of an NVIDIA GPU. Each SM contains many simple arithmetic cores, several tensor cores, its own fast local memory and schedulers that juggle warps. AMD's equivalent is called a Compute Unit (CU). *(Chapter 32)*
- **Streaming (server-sent events)** — Sending a response piece by piece as it is produced, instead of all at once at the end. It is why chatbot answers appear word by word. *(Chapter 4, Chapter 26, Chapter 44)*
- **Stride** — How many pixels a filter moves between one position and the next. A stride of 1 checks every position; a stride of 2 skips every other one and roughly halves the size of the output. *(Chapter 14)*
- **Structure-aware chunking** — Splitting documents along their own structure (headings, sections, list items, table boundaries, slides, code functions) rather than at fixed lengths, so each chunk holds one coherent idea. *(Chapter 27)*
- **Structured and unstructured data** — Structured data is organized into fixed fields, like a table of customers with a column for each attribute. Unstructured data has no fixed layout, like emails, documents, images and audio. *(Chapter 3)*
- **Structured cabling** — An organized, standardized system of permanent cables, patch panels and labeled pathways that connects every rack to the network, so that changes are made by moving short patch cords rather than pulling new long cables. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Structured data** — Data organized in rows and columns with a fixed format, such as a spreadsheet or database table. *(Chapter 11)*
- **Structured note-taking** — Having the model (or yourself) maintain a running notes file, such as a decisions log, a to-do list or a progress report, that lives outside the context window and is read back in when needed. *(Chapter 25)*
- **Structured outputs** — An API feature that forces the model's answer to match an exact data shape you specify, so programs can read it reliably. *(Chapter 26)*
- **Structured sparsity (2:4)** — A hardware feature, introduced with NVIDIA's A100, that skips multiplications when a weight matrix is pruned so that in every group of four numbers, two are zero. Chips quote a "sparse" speed that assumes this pattern, which can be up to double the "dense" speed. Most real models do not use it. *(Chapter 32)*
- **Sub-agent** — A helper agent started by a main agent for one focused subtask. It works in its own clean context window and reports back only a short summary, so the main agent's desk stays uncluttered. *(Chapter 25)*
- **Subagent** — A helper agent started by a main agent to handle a focused subtask in its own fresh context window. It does its work and returns only a short summary, which keeps the main agent's context clean. *(Chapter 28)*
- **Subsea cable** — A fiber-optic cable laid on the ocean floor between continents. Such cables carry nearly all intercontinental internet traffic (Chapter 4). *(Chapter 38)*
- **Substation** — A fenced yard of transformers, breakers and switches that steps transmission-line voltage down to a lower voltage and routes it onward. Big AI campuses usually build their own substation on site. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Success criteria** — Specific, measurable statements of what "good" means for an AI feature, such as "matches policy in at least 95% of policy questions" or "first words within 2 seconds". *(Chapter 31)*
- **Summarized thinking** — A shortened, readable version of a model's chain of thought shown to users instead of the raw reasoning, for readability, safety and protection against copying. *(Chapter 21)*
- **Superchip** — NVIDIA's term for a CPU and one or more GPUs tightly linked on one board with a fast chip-to-chip connection, such as Grace plus two Blackwell GPUs (GB200) or Vera plus two Rubin GPUs. *(Chapter 32, Chapter 34)*
- **Supervised fine-tuning (SFT)** — Fine-tuning on example pairs of an input and the ideal output, so the model learns to imitate those outputs. *(Chapter 30)*
- **Supervised learning** — Learning from examples that come with labels (the right answers), such as photos tagged "cat" or "dog." Most practical machine learning is supervised. *(Chapter 1, Chapter 8)*
- **Supply-chain attack** — An attack that compromises something you depend on, such as a software package, plugin, tool server or model file, instead of attacking your system directly. *(Chapter 31)*
- **Support vector machine (SVM)** — A classifier that finds the boundary leaving the widest possible margin between two classes. *(Chapter 10)*
- **SWE-bench** — A benchmark of real GitHub issues from open-source Python projects; the model must change the code so that the project's own tests pass. "Verified" is a 500-task subset checked by humans; harder successors include SWE-bench Pro. *(Chapter 24)*
- **SwiGLU** — A gated feed-forward design used in most current LLMs. The input is projected two ways; one branch goes through the smooth Swish activation and then multiplies the other branch number by number, acting as a learned volume knob, before a third matrix projects the result back down. *(Chapter 15)*
- **Sycophancy** — A model's tendency to agree with, flatter or tell users what they want to hear instead of what is true. *(Chapter 18, Chapter 20, Chapter 25)*
- **Symbolic AI (GOFAI)** — The approach of building intelligence from explicit, human-written symbols and rules, reasoned over with logic and search. Often nicknamed "GOFAI," for "good old-fashioned AI." *(Chapter 1, Chapter 7)*
- **Symmetry breaking** — Starting the weights of a layer at different random values so that its neurons compute different things and learn different features. With identical starting weights, neurons would remain identical forever. *(Chapter 13)*
- **Synthetic data** — Artificially generated data, made by simulations or by AI models, used in place of or alongside real data. *(Chapter 11, Chapter 19, Chapter 20, Chapter 30)*
- **System card** — A longer document a lab publishes with a frontier model, covering safety evaluations, red-teaming results, dangerous-capability tests and the safeguards applied. It describes the whole deployed system, not just the raw model. *(Chapter 24)*
- **System prompt** — Instructions placed at the start of the context by the app or developer, setting the model's role, rules, tone and available tools. Users usually cannot see it. *(Chapter 18, Chapter 25, Chapter 44)*
- **Systolic array** — A grid of simple multiply-add cells where data pulses from each cell to its neighbors on every clock tick, like blood pumped by a heart (the name comes from "systole," the heart's pumping beat). Each number is reused by many cells without going back to memory. It is the heart of Google's TPU. *(Chapter 32, Chapter 35)*

## T

- **Tabular data** — Data arranged like a spreadsheet: each row is one example (a customer, a loan, a day) and each column is one feature. Most business data is tabular. *(Chapter 10)*
- **Tabular foundation model** — A model pretrained, often on synthetic tables, to make predictions on new spreadsheet-style datasets with little or no training of its own. TabPFN is the best-known example. *(Chapter 10)*
- **Take-or-pay contract** — A contract in which the customer pays for reserved capacity whether or not it uses it. Such contracts give lenders the predictable income that data center loans depend on. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Tanh** — An S-shaped activation like sigmoid, but ranging from −1 to 1 and centered on zero. Short for "hyperbolic tangent". *(Chapter 12, Chapter 14)*
- **Tape-out** — The moment a chip design is finished and sent to the foundry to be manufactured. It marks the end of design and the start of a months-long wait for first silicon. *(Chapter 35, Chapter 36)*
- **Tap-off box** — A plug-in unit that clips onto a busway at any point along its length and feeds power down to one rack, with its own breaker. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Target leakage** — A kind of leakage where a feature is a consequence of the answer, recorded after the fact, such as "treatment given" when predicting a disease. *(Chapter 9)*
- **Target modules** — The specific weight matrices in a model that receive LoRA adapters, typically the attention matrices and often the feed-forward ones too. More targets usually means better results and a bigger adapter. *(Chapter 30)*
- **Task vector** — The difference between a fine-tuned model's weights and the base model's weights. It captures "what fine-tuning changed" and can be added to or subtracted from other models. *(Chapter 20)*
- **TCO (total cost of ownership)** — All the costs of running hardware over its life, including purchase, power, cooling, building space, networking, staff and depreciation, often expressed per GPU-hour. *(Chapter 37)*
- **TCP (Transmission Control Protocol)** — The protocol that makes packet delivery reliable: it numbers packets, reassembles them in order and resends any that are lost. *(Chapter 4)*
- **ΔT (delta T)** — The temperature difference between two points, for example between water entering a rack and water leaving it. Heat only flows when there is a ΔT, and for a given flow of fluid, a bigger ΔT means more heat carried. *(Chapter 40)*
- **TDM (text and data mining) exception** — Laws in places such as the EU, UK and Japan that allow automated analysis of content under certain conditions; in the EU, rights holders can opt out of commercial mining in machine-readable ways. *(Chapter 11)*
- **TDP (thermal design power)** — The heat a chip is designed to produce, and that its cooling must remove, under sustained heavy load. An H100 is rated at 700 W; Blackwell-class GPUs are around 1,000 W or more. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Teacher forcing** — Training a sequence model by feeding it the true previous tokens at every step, rather than its own possibly wrong predictions, so all positions can be trained in parallel. *(Chapter 15)*
- **Technical debt** — The future cost of shortcuts taken now: messy, poorly understood or untested code that makes every later change slower and riskier. Vibe coding can pile it up quickly because nobody understands the code. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Technology cooling system (TCS)** — The closed, carefully filtered liquid loop that runs through the servers' cold plates. It is kept separate from the building's larger, dirtier water loop. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Temperature** — A sampling setting that divides the model's scores before softmax. Low temperature (below 1) makes output more predictable; high temperature (above 1) makes it more varied and more error-prone. *(Chapter 6, Chapter 15, Chapter 18, Chapter 26)*
- **Temporal consistency** — Whether objects, faces, lighting and motion stay coherent from frame to frame in a generated video. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Tensor** — A block of numbers with any number of directions (axes): a vector is a 1-D tensor, a matrix 2-D, a color image 3-D, a batch of images 4-D. In AI, "tensor" simply means a multi-dimensional array of numbers. *(Chapter 5, Chapter 12, Chapter 32)*
- **Tensor core** — A specialized circuit inside modern NVIDIA GPUs that multiplies small matrices (grids of numbers) in one step. Other chip makers have equivalents under different names. *(Chapter 2, Chapter 32)*
- **TensorFlow and Keras** — Google's deep-learning framework (2015), with a large production base, and Keras, a high-level, beginner-friendly interface for building networks that in version 3 runs on TensorFlow, JAX or PyTorch. *(Chapter 13)*
- **Tensor parallelism (TP)** — Splitting each layer's weight matrices across several GPUs so they compute parts of every token together and combine results. It adds the GPUs' memory bandwidths but requires very fast links between them. *(Chapter 23, Chapter 37)*
- **Terminal-Bench** — A benchmark of tasks an AI agent must complete inside a computer terminal, such as installing software, debugging a program or processing data. It has several versions, and scores on different versions cannot be compared. *(Chapter 24)*
- **Terminal (command line)** — A text window where you type commands to your computer instead of clicking. Programmers use it to run programs, tests and Git; several leading coding agents run inside it. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Test set** — Data locked away until the very end and used once, to give an honest final score. If you peek at it while making decisions, it stops being a test. *(Chapter 9)*
- **Test suite** — The full collection of a project's automated tests, usually run all together with a single command. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Test-time compute** — Computation spent while the model is answering, for example by thinking longer or trying several answers, as opposed to computation spent during training. *(Chapter 7, Chapter 21)*
- **Test-time memory** — A memory component whose contents or weights keep updating while the model reads at inference time, so it can retain information beyond its normal context. *(Chapter 17)*
- **Text encoder** — The network that turns a prompt into a sequence of embeddings the image generator can use. CLIP's text encoder and Google's T5 are common choices. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Text extraction (boilerplate removal)** — Pulling the main readable text out of a web page and discarding repeated page furniture such as menus, footers, ads and code. *(Chapter 19)*
- **TF32 (TensorFloat-32)** — NVIDIA's 19-bit format, introduced with the A100, that keeps FP32's range but only FP16's detail. It lets tensor cores speed up code written for FP32. *(Chapter 32)*
- **TF-IDF** — "Term frequency times inverse document frequency": a way to turn text into numbers by counting each word in a document and weighting rare, informative words up and common words down. *(Chapter 11)*
- **The Bitter Lesson** — Rich Sutton's 2019 essay arguing that, over the long run, general methods that make use of growing computation (search and learning) beat approaches built on hand-crafted human knowledge. *(Chapter 13)*
- **Thermal interface material (TIM)** — A thin paste, pad or soft metal layer squeezed between a chip and its cooler to fill microscopic gaps, because even tiny pockets of trapped air block heat badly. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Thermal radiation** — Heat given off as invisible infrared light. It is a minor effect inside a data center, but it is the only way to shed heat in the vacuum of space. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Thermal throttling** — A chip's self-protection: when it gets too hot, it automatically slows its clock and draws less power, so it does less work. A throttled GPU is an expensive GPU doing less. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Thinking budget** — A developer-set limit on the number of tokens a model may spend reasoning before it must answer. *(Chapter 21)*
- **Thinking tokens** — The tokens a model generates while reasoning, before its visible answer. You usually pay for them, even when the app hides them or shows only a summary. *(Chapter 21, Chapter 26, Chapter 44)*
- **Thread** — A single sequence of instructions being executed. One core can run one or more threads, switching between them. *(Chapter 32)*
- **Threshold** — The cutoff that turns a probability into a yes or no decision. It is often 0.5, but you can move it: a cautious bank might only approve loans where the predicted chance of repayment is above 0.9. *(Chapter 8)*
- **Throughput (tokens per second)** — The total number of tokens a system produces per second across all users. Providers maximize throughput to lower cost; users care about their own speed. The two pull in opposite directions. *(Chapter 23, Chapter 26, Chapter 32, Chapter 37)*
- **Tiling (dynamic resolution)** — Splitting a large image into several tiles, encoding each tile at full detail, and usually adding one shrunken thumbnail of the whole image for context. More tiles means more detail and more tokens. *(Chapter 22, Chapter 33)*
- **Time horizon (METR)** — The length of task, measured by how long it takes a skilled human, that an AI agent can complete with a given success rate, usually 50%. A 2-hour time horizon means the agent succeeds on about half of tasks that take a person two hours. *(Chapter 28)*
- **Time series** — A sequence of measurements recorded in time order, such as daily sales, hourly temperatures or monthly subscribers. *(Chapter 10)*
- **Time-series foundation model** — A model pretrained on a large, varied collection of time series that can forecast a new series with little or no task-specific training. *(Chapter 10)*
- **Timestep** — A number saying how far along the noising process an example is, from 0 (clean) to the final step (pure noise). The network is always told the current timestep. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Time to first token (TTFT)** — How long you wait after sending a message until the first piece of the answer appears. It dominates how fast an AI app feels. *(Chapter 4, Chapter 23, Chapter 26, Chapter 33, Chapter 44)*
- **TLS handshake** — The opening exchange in which your device and a server agree on encryption keys and check the server's certificate, before any real data flows. Modern versions need about one round trip; reusing a connection skips it. *(Chapter 44)*
- **TLS (Transport Layer Security)** — The protocol that encrypts HTTPS connections. It verifies the server's identity and sets up a shared secret key during a short handshake. *(Chapter 4)*
- **Token** — A small chunk of text, often a word or part of a word, that a language model reads and writes. In English a token averages about three-quarters of a word. *(Chapter 1, Chapter 3, Chapter 5, Chapter 8, Chapter 11, Chapter 15, Chapter 18, Chapter 25, Chapter 37)*
- **Token dropping** — What happens when an expert is over capacity: extra tokens skip that expert and carry on through the residual connection without its contribution. *(Chapter 17)*
- **Token ID** — The whole number that stands for one token in the vocabulary, such as 9906. The model only ever sees these numbers, never the letters. *(Chapter 18)*
- **Tokenizer** — The program that splits text into tokens and turns them into ID numbers for a model, and turns the model's output numbers back into text. *(Chapter 3, Chapter 15, Chapter 18, Chapter 25, Chapter 26, Chapter 44)*
- **Tokens per second** — How fast a model writes once it has started, often tens of tokens per second for a chatbot. Divide the answer length by it to estimate how long a reply takes. *(Chapter 4, Chapter 30, Chapter 33)*
- **Ton of cooling (refrigeration ton)** — A cooling rate of 12,000 BTU per hour, about 3.5 kW. The name comes from the cooling delivered by melting one ton of ice over a day. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Tool** — A function that an AI model is allowed to ask for, described to the model by a name, a plain-language description and a list of inputs. Web search, a calculator, a database lookup and "send email" can all be tools. *(Chapter 28)*
- **Tool call** — The structured request a model writes when it wants a tool run: the tool's name, an ID, and the inputs as JSON, such as get_weather with {"city": "Paris"}. *(Chapter 28, Chapter 44)*
- **Tool choice** — An API setting that controls whether the model may decide for itself to call tools, must not call any, or (on models that allow it) must call a particular tool. *(Chapter 28)*
- **Tool definition** — The description an application gives a model for each tool: a name, a plain-language description of what the tool does and when to use it, and an input schema listing the inputs it needs and their types. *(Chapter 28)*
- **Tool poisoning** — An attack in which malicious instructions are hidden in a tool's description or its results, so that an AI agent reading them misbehaves, for example by leaking data or calling other tools it should not. *(Chapter 28, Chapter 31)*
- **Tool result** — The output of a tool, sent back to the model by the application and linked to the tool call it answers by a matching ID. *(Chapter 28)*
- **Tool use (function calling)** — A model's ability to respond with a structured request to run a named tool with specific inputs, instead of (or before) answering in prose. The application runs the tool and sends the result back to the model. *(Chapter 28)*
- **Top-5 error** — The share of test images for which the correct label is not among the model's five highest-scoring guesses. It was ImageNet's headline score, because some photos contain several plausible objects. *(Chapter 14)*
- **Top-k** — The k best-scoring results a search returns, such as the top 5. In RAG, k is how many passages you paste into the prompt. *(Chapter 27)*
- **Top-k routing** — The rule that each token is sent to the k experts with the highest router scores (top-1, top-2, top-8 and so on). The rest are skipped for that token. *(Chapter 17)*
- **Top-k sampling** — Keeping only the k most likely tokens (for example k = 40), setting all others to zero, rescaling the survivors to sum to 1, and sampling among them. *(Chapter 18)*
- **Top-of-rack (ToR) switch** — A network switch installed in or next to a rack that connects all the servers in that rack to the rest of the network. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Topology** — The wiring pattern of a network: which devices connect to which. Common AI topologies include the fat tree, the rail-optimized design, the torus and the dragonfly. *(Chapter 34)*
- **Top-p (nucleus sampling)** — A second randomness control that limits the model's choices to the most likely tokens whose probabilities add up to p, such as the top 90%. *(Chapter 26)*
- **Top-p sampling (nucleus sampling)** — Sampling only from the smallest set of most-likely tokens whose probabilities add up to at least p (such as 0.9), after rescaling them. It cuts off the long tail of unlikely tokens while keeping variety. *(Chapter 6, Chapter 18)*
- **TOPS (trillions of operations per second)** — Trillions of operations per second, usually integer operations (like INT8) on phone and laptop neural processors. A TOPS figure is not directly comparable to a FLOPS figure, because the precision and kind of operation differ. *(Chapter 32, Chapter 35)*
- **Torus** — A grid-shaped network in which each chip links directly to its neighbors and the edges wrap around to the opposite side, like a video game map where walking off the right edge brings you back on the left. In a 3D torus each chip has six neighbors. *(Chapter 34)*
- **Total parameters** — Every learned number stored in a model, including all experts. It decides how much memory the model needs. *(Chapter 17, Chapter 18)*
- **TPP (Total Processing Performance)** — The US export-control measure of a chip's computing power, roughly its peak operations per second (in trillions) multiplied by the number of bits per operation. The January 2026 H200 policy covers chips with a TPP below 21,000. *(Chapter 36)*
- **TPU (Tensor Processing Unit)** — Google's family of chips designed specifically for neural-network math, first deployed in Google's data centers in 2015 and also rented to outside customers through Google Cloud. *(Chapter 2, Chapter 32, Chapter 35)*
- **Trace and span** — A span is one recorded step in handling a request (one model call, one tool call, one guardrail check) with its timing, tokens and cost. A trace is the complete tree of spans for a single request. *(Chapter 31)*
- **Training** — The process of adjusting a model's parameters using data until it performs well. Training a frontier chatbot model takes months on tens of thousands of chips; it happens once per model, before you ever use it. *(Chapter 1, Chapter 5, Chapter 8, Chapter 12)*
- **Training compute** — The total number of arithmetic operations (FLOPs) used to train a model, the main measure of how much effort went into it. *(Chapter 19, Chapter 24)*
- **Training data** — The collection of examples a model learns from, such as emails, photos, sentences or game positions. Its size and quality largely decide how good the model becomes. *(Chapter 1)*
- **Training example** — One unit of training data, such as a single question paired with its ideal answer, or a full conversation. *(Chapter 30)*
- **Training loop** — The repeated cycle at the heart of machine learning: take a batch of examples, predict, measure the loss, compute the gradient by backpropagation, and update the parameters. One trip around the loop is a step. *(Chapter 6)*
- **Training opt-out** — A setting that stops a provider from using your conversations to train future models. Defaults differ between companies and between consumer and business plans, and they have changed over time. *(Chapter 25)*
- **Training run** — One large training job, from the first random weights to the final saved model, often lasting weeks or months on thousands of chips. *(Chapter 19)*
- **Training set** — The portion of the data the model learns from. Its parameters are adjusted to fit these examples. *(Chapter 9)*
- **Training set, validation set and test set** — The three portions a dataset is split into: examples the model learns from, examples used to tune choices during development, and examples held back for a final honest score. *(Chapter 12)*
- **Training, validation and test sets** — Three separate portions of data: the training set is used to learn the parameters, the validation set to make design choices during development, and the test set is held back for one final, honest exam on unseen data. *(Chapter 6)*
- **Trainium and Inferentia** — Amazon Web Services' in-house AI chips. Trainium is built mainly for training (and increasingly for inference); Inferentia is built for inference. Both are programmed through AWS's Neuron software kit. *(Chapter 35)*
- **Transfer learning** — Reusing what a model learned on one task to help with a different task. A model that learned to recognize thousands of everyday objects already "knows" edges, textures and shapes, so it can learn to spot, say, cracked phone screens from only a few hundred examples. *(Chapter 8, Chapter 14)*
- **Transformer** — The neural-network design introduced in 2017 that underlies nearly all modern chatbots. Its key ingredient, "attention," lets every token in the input weigh how much every other token matters. *(Chapter 1, Chapter 7, Chapter 15, Chapter 18, Chapter 38)*
- **Transformer block** — One repeating unit of a Transformer: a normalization, a self-attention sub-layer and a residual add, followed by a normalization, a feed-forward sub-layer and a residual add. Models stack dozens of identical-looking blocks, each with its own weights. *(Chapter 15)*
- **Transformer-specific ASIC** — A chip whose circuits are hard-wired for the operations of transformer models. It can be very efficient but becomes obsolete if the dominant model architecture changes. *(Chapter 35)*
- **Transistor** — A microscopic switch, made of silicon, that lets electric current flow or blocks it depending on a small voltage applied to its control terminal, called the gate. It has no moving parts and can switch billions of times per second. *(Chapter 2, Chapter 32)*
- **Translation invariance** — The property of giving the same answer when the object in an image is shifted to a different position. CNNs get much of this from weight sharing plus pooling. *(Chapter 14)*
- **Transport (stdio and Streamable HTTP)** — How MCP messages physically travel. stdio ("standard input/output") connects the host to a server running as a program on the same computer. Streamable HTTP connects to a server elsewhere on the internet, usually with OAuth for sign-in. *(Chapter 28)*
- **Trend and seasonality** — In a time series, the trend is the long-run upward or downward drift, and seasonality is a pattern that repeats on a fixed cycle, such as busier weekends or a December peak. *(Chapter 10)*
- **Trigger** — The event that starts an automation, such as "a new email arrives with the label Receipts", "a form is submitted" or "every Monday at 9 a.m.". *(Chapter 29)*
- **True negative (TN)** — The model said no and the answer really was no, such as a healthy person correctly cleared. *(Chapter 9)*
- **True positive (TP)** — The model said yes and the answer really was yes, such as a sick person correctly flagged by a test. *(Chapter 9)*
- **Trusted access program** — Gated access to a more capable or less restricted model version for vetted organizations, such as cybersecurity defenders, while the public gets a version with more safeguards. *(Chapter 24)*
- **Truth table** — A table listing every possible combination of a circuit's inputs alongside the output it produces. It completely describes what a logic gate or circuit does. *(Chapter 2)*
- **t-SNE and UMAP** — Methods for squashing high-dimensional data into a 2-D or 3-D map in which similar items end up near each other, used mainly for visualization. *(Chapter 10)*
- **TSV (through-silicon via)** — A vertical wire drilled straight through a silicon die and filled with metal, connecting it to the dies above and below. Thousands of TSVs wire an HBM stack together like elevator shafts in a skyscraper. *(Chapter 33, Chapter 36)*
- **TTFT (time to first token)** — How long after a request is sent the first piece of the answer appears. With streaming, it dominates how fast a product feels. *(Chapter 31, Chapter 37)*
- **TTS (text-to-speech)** — Generating spoken audio from text. Modern neural TTS, descended from DeepMind's WaveNet (2016), sounds natural and can imitate specific voices. *(Chapter 22)*
- **Turing machine** — Alan Turing's 1936 imaginary computer: a tape of symbols, a read-write head and a table of rules. A "universal" Turing machine can imitate any other machine, which is the idea behind every general-purpose computer. *(Chapter 7)*
- **Turing test (imitation game)** — Alan Turing's 1950 proposal to judge machine intelligence by whether a human judge, chatting in text, can tell a machine apart from a person. *(Chapter 7)*
- **Two-phase cooling** — Cooling in which a specially chosen fluid boils on or near the hot chip, absorbing heat as it turns to vapor; the vapor is then condensed back to liquid elsewhere and returned. *(Chapter 40)*

## U

- **UALink (Ultra Accelerator Link)** — An open scale-up interconnect standard (version 1.0 in April 2025) backed by AMD, Intel, Google, Meta, Microsoft and others as an alternative to NVLink. It supports up to 1,024 accelerators in one domain using 200 Gb/s lanes. *(Chapter 34)*
- **UCIe (Universal Chiplet Interconnect Express)** — An open industry standard for connecting chiplets from different designers inside one package, so they can be mixed like standard parts. *(Chapter 34, Chapter 36)*
- **Ultra Ethernet (UEC)** — An industry consortium and specification (version 1.0 released June 2025) that adapts Ethernet for AI and supercomputing traffic, for example by spreading one flow's packets across many paths and handling congestion better. *(Chapter 34)*
- **Underfitting** — When a model is too simple to capture the real pattern, so it does poorly even on its training data. *(Chapter 9, Chapter 13)*
- **Undersea (subsea) cable** — A fiber-optic cable laid on the ocean floor between continents. Such cables carry nearly all intercontinental internet traffic. *(Chapter 4)*
- **Unembedding** — The final matrix that turns a token's vector into one score for every token in the vocabulary. Many models reuse the embedding matrix for this, a trick called weight tying. *(Chapter 15, Chapter 18)*
- **U-Net** — An encoder-decoder CNN from 2015 with skip connections between matching resolutions. Designed for medical image segmentation, it later became the classic denoising network inside image generators such as early Stable Diffusion (Chapter 16). *(Chapter 14, Chapter 16)*
- **Unicode** — The worldwide standard that assigns a unique number to every character in every writing system, including emoji. It is why a message in Hindi, Arabic or Chinese displays correctly on any modern device. *(Chapter 3)*
- **Unified memory** — A design in which the CPU and GPU share one pool of memory, as on Apple Silicon Macs. It lets a laptop with 32 to 128 GB of memory load models far larger than a typical graphics card could hold. *(Chapter 30, Chapter 33, Chapter 35)*
- **Unit economics** — The revenue and costs of one unit of a business, such as one customer, one subscription or one completed task, used to judge whether growth makes or loses money. For AI products the key cost is the cost per task: the model, tool and infrastructure cost of delivering one unit of value. *(Chapter 31)*
- **Unit test** — A small automatic check that a piece of code does what it should, for example "calling add(2, 3) must return 5." Coding agents are rewarded when their changes make tests pass. *(Chapter 21, Chapter 29)*
- **Universal approximation theorem** — A mathematical result (Cybenko 1989, Hornik 1991) that a neural network with one sufficiently wide hidden layer can approximate any reasonable function. It guarantees that good weights exist, not that training will find them or how many neurons are needed. *(Chapter 13)*
- **Unrolling** — Drawing (or computing) a recurrent network as a chain of identical copies, one per time step, each passing its hidden state to the next. It turns the loop into an ordinary deep network. *(Chapter 14)*
- **Unstructured data** — Data without a predefined table format: free text, images, audio and video. *(Chapter 11)*
- **Unsupervised learning** — Finding structure, such as groups or patterns, in data that has no labels, for example grouping customers by shopping habits. *(Chapter 1, Chapter 8)*
- **Upsampling** — Deliberately showing some data more often than its natural share, sometimes repeating it several times, because it is especially valuable. *(Chapter 19)*
- **Uptime Institute Tiers (I to IV)** — A rating system for data center infrastructure. Tier I is basic capacity with no redundancy, Tier II adds redundant components, Tier III is concurrently maintainable, and Tier IV is fault tolerant. *(Chapter 38)*
- **URL (Uniform Resource Locator)** — A web address, such as https://claude.ai/new. It names the protocol, the server and the specific page or resource. *(Chapter 4, Chapter 26)*
- **Usage** — The part of every response that reports how many tokens you were billed for: input tokens, output tokens, and tokens written to or read from the cache. Usage is your receipt. *(Chapter 26)*
- **User and assistant messages** — The turns of a conversation in an API request. "User" is what the human or program sends; "assistant" is what the model replied earlier. Because the API is stateless, you send the whole list of turns every time. *(Chapter 26)*
- **UTF-8** — The most common way to store Unicode code points as bytes. It uses 1 byte for basic English characters and 2 to 4 bytes for everything else, and it is the default encoding of the web. *(Chapter 3)*
- **Utilization (MFU)** — The fraction of a chip's theoretical top speed that a real job actually achieves. Large training runs typically reach about 35 to 50%, because time is lost to communication, memory waits and failures. MFU stands for model FLOPs utilization. *(Chapter 5, Chapter 44)*

## V

- **Vacuum tube** — A glass bulb with the air removed that can switch or amplify electric current. It was the switch inside the first electronic computers in the 1940s, before transistors replaced it. *(Chapter 2)*
- **VAE (variational autoencoder)** — An autoencoder whose encoder outputs a fuzzy region (a mean and a spread) instead of a single point. This makes the latent space smooth and gap-free, so you can pick a random point and decode it into a plausible new example. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Validation set** — Held-out data used during development to compare models and tune hyperparameters. The model never trains on it, but your choices are shaped by it. *(Chapter 9, Chapter 30)*
- **Value** — In attention, a vector each token computes with a third learned matrix, holding the information the token hands over when another token attends to it. *(Chapter 15)*
- **Value model (critic)** — A helper network in PPO-style RL that predicts how much reward the policy should expect from a given situation, so that each actual result can be judged as better or worse than expected. *(Chapter 20)*
- **Vanishing gradient** — The problem that learning signals shrink as they are multiplied backward through many layers, so the early layers of a deep network barely learn. *(Chapter 13, Chapter 14)*
- **Vapor-compression refrigeration cycle** — The loop inside every fridge and chiller: a cold refrigerant evaporates while absorbing heat, a compressor squeezes the vapor so it gets hot, the hot vapor condenses while releasing heat outdoors, and an expansion valve drops its pressure so it is cold again. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Variable** — A named box in a program that holds a value, like price = 20. The value can change while the program runs. *(Chapter 3, Chapter 5, Chapter 26)*
- **Variance (statistical)** — A measure of spread: the average of the squared distances of the values from their mean. Bigger variance means the values are more scattered. *(Chapter 6, Chapter 9)*
- **Vector** — An ordered list of numbers, such as [3, 120, 15], where each position has an agreed meaning. A vector can describe a thing's features, or a point or arrow in space. *(Chapter 5, Chapter 12, Chapter 27)*
- **Vector database** — A database built to store embeddings (lists of numbers representing meaning) and quickly find the items most similar to a query. It is the backbone of retrieval-augmented generation. *(Chapter 11, Chapter 27)*
- **Vector quantization (codebook)** — A way to turn continuous data such as image patches or sound into discrete tokens. A learned "codebook" holds thousands of reference vectors, and each patch is replaced by the ID of its closest entry, so an image becomes a list of ID numbers just like text. *(Chapter 22)*
- **Vendor lock-in** — Being so dependent on one company's product that leaving it would be expensive or difficult. Most app builders reduce this by letting you export your code to GitHub, which you should do early. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Verbosity bias** — The tendency of human raters and AI judges to prefer longer, more elaborately formatted answers regardless of whether they are better, which training then amplifies. *(Chapter 20, Chapter 31)*
- **Verifier** — A program or model that checks whether an output is correct, such as an answer checker for math, a set of unit tests for code, or a proof checker for formal mathematics. *(Chapter 21)*
- **Version control (Git)** — A system that records every change to a set of files over time, so you can see who changed what, compare versions and roll back mistakes. Git, created by Linus Torvalds in 2005, is by far the most widely used version-control tool. *(Chapter 3, Chapter 29)*
- **Vibe coding** — Building software by describing what you want to an AI and accepting the code it writes, often without reading it closely. The term was coined by Andrej Karpathy in February 2025 (see Chapter 29). *(Chapter 7, Chapter 29)*
- **Video VAE (3D VAE)** — A variational autoencoder that compresses video across width, height and time into a compact latent, and decodes latents back into frames. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Virtual machine** — A software imitation of a whole computer, running alongside others on one physical server. Clouds rent them so many customers can share a machine safely. *(Chapter 4)*
- **Vision encoder** — The part of a multimodal system that turns an image into a sequence of meaningful vectors (one per patch or group of patches) that a language model can use. It is usually a Vision Transformer pretrained on huge numbers of images. *(Chapter 22)*
- **Vision-language-action model (VLA)** — A model that takes camera images and a spoken or written instruction ("fold the towel") and outputs robot actions directly, built on top of a pretrained vision-language model so it inherits knowledge from the web. *(Chapter 22)*
- **Vision-language model (VLM)** — A model that takes images and text together and can describe, answer questions about, or reason over images. Every frontier chatbot that accepts photo uploads contains one. *(Chapter 22)*
- **Vision Transformer (ViT)** — A Transformer that reads an image by cutting it into small square patches, turning each patch into a vector and treating the patches as a sequence of tokens. It was introduced by Google in 2020. *(Chapter 15, Chapter 22)*
- **Visual grounding** — Linking words to specific locations in an image, for example answering "where is the Submit button?" with the coordinates of its center. Computer-use agents depend on precise grounding. *(Chapter 22)*
- **Visual token** — One vector that represents part of an image inside a language model's sequence, after the encoder and projector. Providers bill visual tokens like input text tokens. *(Chapter 22)*
- **vLLM** — A high-performance open-source server for running models for many users at once, using techniques such as PagedAttention (Chapter 23). It is what companies use to serve open models in production, not a laptop chat app. *(Chapter 30)*
- **Vocabulary** — The fixed list of every token a language model can read or write. Modern chatbots typically have vocabularies of roughly 100,000 to 250,000 tokens. *(Chapter 5, Chapter 14, Chapter 15, Chapter 18)*
- **Voice activity detection (VAD)** — A small, fast model that decides, many times per second, whether someone is currently speaking. *(Chapter 22)*
- **Voice mode** — Talking to the AI with your voice and hearing it answer in near real time, as in a phone call. *(Chapter 25)*
- **Volatile** — Describes memory that loses its contents when power is removed. Registers, caches and RAM are volatile; storage drives are not. *(Chapter 2)*
- **Voltage regulator (VRM)** — Small power circuits placed right beside a chip that convert board power (around 48–54 V DC) down to the roughly 1 V the chip's transistors need, delivering very large currents over a very short distance. *(Chapter 34)*
- **Von Neumann architecture** — The basic design of almost every computer since 1945: a processor that executes instructions, a separate memory that stores both the program and its data, and a pathway (bus) that moves information between them. *(Chapter 2, Chapter 33)*
- **Von Neumann bottleneck** — The slowdown caused by having to shuttle data back and forth between a separate processor and memory through a limited connection. It is the root of today's memory wall. *(Chapter 33)*
- **VRAM (video memory)** — The memory built into a GPU. A model and its training data must fit in VRAM to run fast; the free Colab T4 GPU has 16 GB. *(Chapter 30)*

## W

- **Wafer** — A thin, round, polished slice of single-crystal silicon on which hundreds of chips are built at once. Leading-edge factories use wafers 300 mm (about 12 inches) across. *(Chapter 36)*
- **Wafer-scale engine** — Cerebras's approach of using an entire silicon wafer as one giant chip instead of cutting it into many small chips. Its WSE-3 covers 46,225 mm², about 54 times the largest single die a normal lithography machine can print. *(Chapter 32, Chapter 35)*
- **Walk-forward validation** — Testing a forecasting model by repeatedly training on everything up to a point in time and predicting the next period, then moving the point forward and repeating. Also called backtesting. *(Chapter 9)*
- **Warmup** — The first phase of a learning-rate schedule, in which the rate rises from nearly zero to its peak, so the early, chaotic steps are small. *(Chapter 13, Chapter 19)*
- **Warm-water cooling** — Liquid cooling designed to work with relatively warm supply water, often 30–45 °C, so that outdoor air can cool it through dry coolers for most or all of the year without chillers. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Warp** — On NVIDIA GPUs, a group of 32 threads that execute the same instruction together, in lockstep. It is the basic unit the GPU schedules. *(Chapter 32)*
- **Warrant** — A contract giving its holder the right to buy a company's shares at a set price, often only after conditions are met. Chip vendors have used warrants to tie big customers' purchases to a stake in the vendor's success. *(Chapter 35)*
- **Water consumption** — The part of withdrawn water that is not returned to its source, mostly because it evaporated. Consumption is usually the number that matters for local water stress. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Water stress** — How much of an area's available water is already being used. A liter evaporated in a water-stressed desert basin matters far more than a liter evaporated in a wet region. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Water withdrawal** — Water taken from a river, lake, aquifer or utility. Some or all of it may be returned afterward. *(Chapter 40)*
- **Watt-hour (Wh)** — An amount of energy: one watt for one hour, or 3,600 joules. A 10-watt LED bulb uses 10 Wh in an hour. Electric bills use kilowatt-hours (1,000 Wh). *(Chapter 44)*
- **Watt (W)** — The unit of power, meaning energy used per second (one joule per second). A kilowatt (kW) is 1,000 watts, a megawatt (MW) a million, a gigawatt (GW) a billion. *(Chapter 2)*
- **Wavelength** — The distance between successive peaks of a light wave. Shorter wavelengths can draw finer details, the way a fine pen draws thinner lines than a marker. *(Chapter 36)*
- **Wavelength division multiplexing (WDM)** — Sending many separate streams of data down one fiber at the same time, each on a different color (wavelength) of laser light. It lets one strand of glass carry many terabits per second. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Web crawler** — A program that automatically visits web pages, saves them and follows their links to discover more pages. Search engines and AI companies both run crawlers. *(Chapter 11, Chapter 19)*
- **Webhook** — A web address that one app calls automatically, sending a small message of data, the moment something happens. It is how apps "ring the doorbell" of an automation instead of being checked repeatedly. *(Chapter 29)*
- **Web scraping** — Automatically extracting content from web pages, such as the article text without the menus and ads. *(Chapter 11)*
- **Weight** — The most common kind of parameter: a number that is multiplied by an input and so sets how strongly that input influences the output. In the taxi fare, the weight is the price per mile. *(Chapter 5, Chapter 8, Chapter 12)*
- **Weight decay** — Shrinking every weight by a tiny fraction at each training step, so that weights only stay large if the data keeps pushing them up. It is a simple form of regularization. *(Chapter 13)*
- **Weighted sum** — Adding up several numbers after multiplying each by its own importance (weight). A dot product is a weighted sum, and so is the house-price rule earlier in this chapter. *(Chapter 5, Chapter 12)*
- **Weight sharing** — Using the same small set of weights at every position in the input, as a convolution does. It slashes the number of parameters and lets a pattern learned in one place be recognized everywhere. *(Chapter 14)*
- **Weights (parameters)** — The learned numbers inside an AI model that set how strongly each input influences each output. A large model has billions of them, and together they encode its knowledge and skills (Chapter 5). *(Chapter 3, Chapter 30)*
- **Wet-bulb temperature** — The lowest temperature you can reach by evaporating water into the air. It depends on humidity: in dry air it can be far below the dry-bulb temperature; in very humid air it is close to it. Evaporative coolers can approach the wet bulb. *(Chapter 40)*
- **White space** — The floor area where the IT equipment (racks of servers, storage and network gear) actually sits. The name comes from the clean, brightly lit look of classic computer rooms. *(Chapter 38)*
- **Width** — The number of neurons in a layer. A wider layer can detect more different patterns at the same level. *(Chapter 12)*
- **word2vec** — A 2013 technique that learns a vector for every word by training on the task of predicting nearby words. It became famous for analogies like king − man + woman ≈ queen. *(Chapter 5, Chapter 14)*
- **Workflow** — A system in which language models and tools are orchestrated through steps fixed in advance by code. The developer decides the sequence; the model fills in each step. *(Chapter 28, Chapter 29)*
- **World model** — A model that predicts how an environment will change, especially in response to actions, sometimes rendering it as interactive video. *(Chapter 16, Chapter 22, Chapter 24)*
- **WSD (warmup–stable–decay)** — A schedule that warms up, holds the learning rate constant for most of training, and then decays it quickly at the end, which makes it easy to extend training or produce checkpoints at any point. *(Chapter 13)*
- **WUE (water usage effectiveness)** — Liters of water consumed on site per kWh of IT energy. It usually counts only the site's own water (mostly cooling-tower evaporation), not the water used by power plants to make its electricity. *(Chapter 40)*

## X

- **x86** — The instruction set used by Intel and AMD processors in most PCs and many servers since the 1980s. *(Chapter 32)*
- **Xavier (Glorot) initialization** — A 2010 rule for setting the spread of random starting weights from the number of inputs and outputs of each layer, designed to keep signal sizes steady through sigmoid and tanh networks. *(Chapter 13)*
- **XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost** — The three most popular open-source gradient-boosting libraries. XGBoost was released in 2014; LightGBM comes from Microsoft and CatBoost from Yandex. *(Chapter 10)*
- **XML tags** — Labels in angle brackets, such as <article> and </article>, that wrap sections of a prompt so the model can tell what is an instruction and what is data. The tag names are up to you; models simply learn to respect the labels. *(Chapter 25)*
- **XOR (exclusive OR)** — A logic gate whose output is 1 when exactly one of its two inputs is 1, and 0 when both are the same. It is the key ingredient in circuits that add numbers. *(Chapter 2, Chapter 12)*
- **XOR problem** — The classic example of a pattern that a single-layer perceptron cannot learn, because no single straight line separates its "yes" cases from its "no" cases. Networks with an extra "hidden" layer of neurons can learn it. *(Chapter 7)*

## Y

- **Yield** — The percentage of chips on a wafer that work correctly. It falls as dies get bigger, because a bigger die is more likely to contain at least one defect. *(Chapter 36)*

## Z

- **Zero data retention (ZDR)** — An arrangement, usually for business customers, under which the provider does not store your prompts and outputs after processing them. *(Chapter 26)*
- **ZeRO / FSDP** — Techniques (Microsoft DeepSpeed's ZeRO and PyTorch's Fully Sharded Data Parallel) that split ("shard") the weights, gradients and optimizer state across many GPUs so each holds only a slice, gathering pieces over the network when needed. *(Chapter 33, Chapter 37)*
- **Zero-shot** — Doing a task without any task-specific training examples, for example classifying photos into categories just by comparing them with text descriptions of those categories. *(Chapter 16)*
- **Zero-shot, one-shot and few-shot prompting** — Giving the model no examples, one example, or several examples of the input and the output you want. Few-shot prompting is also called multishot prompting. *(Chapter 25)*
- **Z-score** — How many standard deviations a value is from the average: (value − average) ÷ standard deviation. Values with a z-score beyond about 3 are often flagged as unusual. *(Chapter 10)*
